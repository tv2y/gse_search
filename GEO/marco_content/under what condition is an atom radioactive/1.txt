There are many different ways. Most new elements produced in stars are radioactive. That's why they are no longer around; they all decay within a few decades, except for a tiny few (like potassium-40 and uranium-238) that have very long half-lives. Those are the materials that make the ground radioactive. They can be extracted and used; for example, uranium used for nuclear power reactors.

Virtually all elements have radioactive "isotopes", versions of the element that have an unusual number of neutrons in their nucleus, but which still behave as do the normal element. So one way to make a stable atom radioactive is to bombard it with neutrons. Some will stick to the nucleus, and that typically makes it radioactive. That process is called neutron activation.

Another way is to take the debris from fission. Most of the fission fragments are radioactive.

You can extract radioactive atoms from the atmosphere. There is a radioactive version of carbon known as "radiocarbon" or C-14. It is made in the atmosphere when cosmic rays (high energy particles coming -- we think -- from distant supernova explosions) hit ordinary nitrogen.

In a hospital, a long-lived radioactive material is transported in from a lab, and either used itself, or it has its decay products removed; these are the fragments that come about when the long-lived radioactive material decays.

When radioactive in rock decay, they produce radioactive radon gas. That gas often enters our homes in their cellars, and can represent a hazard.

There are many other ways too, but that is a sample.

Potassium 40 (K40) is everywhere, inside us and all around us. It is in foods such as bananas and building materials such as bricks and concrete. Anywhere there is potassium there is also radioactivity.

I once told a conscientious mother that bananas are radioactive and she stopped feeding them to her children, but I could also have told her about spinach, sweet potato, beans, acorn squash, mushrooms and numerous other foods.

So why aren’t we all dead or sick from radioactive poisoning, because contrary to common knowledge there exists a certain biological tolerance to small amounts of radioactivity. In fact we live in a soup of radioactive decay products referred to as background radiation. Background radioactive levels vary in strength from locale to locale and with weather conditions but there is aways some natural radioactivity in the environment.

When radioactive materials were introduced into the workplace it was determined that the potential hazard from using them should be no greater than hazards posed by other materials and substances, because of this standards for exposure were developed. The problem is that there was no data to base the standards upon so assumptions were necessary.

It was known from certain unfortunate events that there is a level of radioactive exposure that has a measurable biological effect but it was uncertain if there are effects below such levels. Because of this the assumption was made that biological tolerance doesn’t exist and that lasting effects occur down to zero exposure. This is a conservative assumption and is probably the origin of the notion that there is no safe level of radioactive exposure.

Let’s apply this same reasoning to something less spooky than radioactive exposure. Suppose you know that if a person goes outside in freezing weather for an certain amount of time without protective clothing that he will first get frostbite and eventually die from freezing. So you take a data point at the exposure level that causes death and another for frostbite. You then draw a straight line through these points that extends past zero. Then you assume that even though there are no observable ill effects between the exposure that causes frostbite and zero exposure that none-the-less an effect exists and that the relative hazard is expressed by extrapolation from the frostbite and death data.

So next on a cold winter morning you see the mailman put something into your box and contemplate whether it is safe to make the trip to the mailbox without putting your coat on. Would you be afraid to go without a coat under the assumption that you are doing undetectable damage to yourself and that over a lifetime you would accumulate enough damage to eventually get frostbit and perhaps freeze to death? Probably not but this is the official position concerning radiation exposure.

The radioactivity of a material does not depend on any external conditions, except for two items. Other than the two exceptions, there is nothing that be done to change the half-life or radioactivity of something. You can clean it up, but you are only moving it from one place to another.

The two exceptions are:

A substance exhibits radioactivity when its atomic nuclei are unstable and undergo spontaneous transformation, releasing ionizing radiation. Two primary conditions that create or correlate with such instability are:

1.   Nuclear imbalance (neutron-to-proton ratio far from the band of stability)

 - Nuclei with too many neutrons (neutron-rich) typically decay by beta-minus (β−) emission: a neutron → proton + electron + antineutrino.

 - Nuclei with too few neutrons (proton-rich) tend to decay by beta-plus (β+) emission (positron emission) or electron capture.

 - The imbalance produces an internal energy configuration where decay lowers nuclear binding energy and moves the nuclide toward a more stable composition.
2.   Excess nuclear energy or high mass (heavy nuclei subject to fission or alpha decay)

 - Very heavy nuclei (large atomic mass, e.g., uranium, thorium, plutonium) have high Coulomb repulsion among protons and can reduce energy by emitting an alpha particle (alpha decay) or undergoing spontaneous fission into lighter fragments.

 - Nuclei in excited states (formed by prior nuclear reactions or neutron capture) may be radioactive until they shed excess energy via gamma emission or other decay modes.

Brief synthesis: radioactivity arises when a nucleus is energetically favored to change — either because its proton/neutron ratio is off the stable balance or because its mass/energy content (especially in heavy or excited nuclei) makes particle or fission emission energetically favorable.

The activity of a radioactive substance is measured in terms of the number of disintegrations a given radioactive sample undergoes in one second. The number of disintegrations a given radioctive sample undergoes in one second depend on,

1.   The number of active atoms present in the sample ie its mass. More the active atoms present in the ample, more is the number of disintegrations that happen per second.
2.   It depends on the decay constant of the radioactive substance. The larger the decay constant the more the disintegrations that occur in one second. The decay constant is inverse of the mean-life expressed in seconds of the radioactive sample. So shorter the mean-life ( mean-life = half-life ÷ 0.693) or half-life, the larger the decay constant. So a radioactive sample with a shorter half-life shall disintegrate more in a given time than a longer lived sample.

So activity of a sample only depends on its mass, and its half-life.

The two most common units are Becquerels and Curies. Becquerels are metric and curies are British.

A becquerel (bq) denotes that there is one disintegration occurring per second. This figure is uselessly small. A typical banana contains roughly 15 bq of K-40. This is nothing at all to worry about. Your body contains hundreds of becquerels of potassium. Again, nothing to worry about.

A becquerel is like trying to measure a beach per grain of sand. You can get high numbers that mean nothing at all. They are useful when talking about things like Fukushima contamination, though, because we measure it in inordinately small numbers - for instance, per cubic meter of ocean water (~264 gallons; the average human drinks ~160 gallons per year), Fukushima has increased contamination levels by 0.3bq. Extremely, extremely small figures, especially when you consider that there are over 10,000bq of natural potassium in that same volume. But even that 10,000bq of potassium isn’t enough to be concerned about.

On the other end of the spectrum is the “Curie.”

A Curie is 3.7 × 10^10 becquerels. That’s 37,000,000,000 becquerels. Now we’re talking some hefty numbers. A single curie of gamma-emitter radioactive particulate is sufficient enough that at 1 meter, you would receive 1 REM per hour - that’s roughly 0.5% of what you need to get radiation sickness in an hour, and ~15% of what you need to get an increased risk of cancer in an hour.

More importantly, that’s enough to cause you to exceed your legal limits in very, very short order.

It’s so large a number that until you start measuring things like a meltdown or high level waste, you don’t see those figures much in real life, so “micro-micro Curies” are more commonly used.

Those are the primary ones.

If you hear people talking about Grays, Sieverts, REM, Roentgen, and the like, they’re talking about radiation (or radiation exposure, depending), not radioactivity. Radioactivity is what emits radiation.

There’s mechanism and incentive.

The incentive is to lower energy. Things just like to be in lower energy states. Take a neutron, which can be thought of as a proton and electron bound together. The mass of the neutron is greater than that of the proton and electron. If the neutron divides into a proton and electron, it will have less total mass, and therefore less energy by E=mc^2. So that is what a free neutron does in about 10 minutes, it decays into a proton and electron. It’s a little like a ball rolling downhill, it seeks a lower energy state. (Aside: this also produces a neutrino, but a neutrino’s mass is a millionth that of an electron and can be ignored in a mass balance.)

The same thing happens inside nucleii except there the proton remains with its positive charge. That increases the electrostatic energy (the positive charge doesn’t “like” to be next to other positive charges). So usually the neutron decay would lead to a higher energy state, so usually an atom doesn’t decay. But sometimes the mass cost of a proton turning into a neutron is worthwhile if the electrostatic energy is decreased. Then decay can be by positron, which is a positively charged particle. But “beta” decay - either a negative electron or positve positron - occurs when it can lower the total energy, which is a sum of E=mc^2 energy and the electrostatic energy from those like charges in close proximity.

In heavier atoms, there are more positive charges more spread out. So sometimes you can get decay when whole chunks of atoms fly apart due to electrostatic force. This “alpha decay” is actually firing off whole alpha particles, which are two protons and two neutrons, also known as helium ions. Again, this only happens with the heavy elements (like the bottom row of elements in the periodic tabel).

In extreme cases of a few atom types, the electrostatic forces break the atom into halves (or usually about 1/3 versus 2/3rds). That’s fission, which uranium and plutonium do well. That powers nuclear power plants and atomic bombs.

Now, the mechanism for beta decay is interesting. That’s the weak force. It is a fundamental force that seems to exist only at the subatomic level (the forces inside a neutron binding subatomic particles like quarks). You can read more about that at What Is the Weak Force? but key is this paragraph: “Italian physicist Enrico Fermi devised a theory in 1933 to explain beta decay, which is the process by which a neutron in a nucleus changes into a proton and expels an electron, often called a beta particle in this context. "“He defined a new type of force, the so-called weak interaction, that was responsible for decay, and whose fundamental process was transforming a neutron into a proton, an electron and a neutrino," which was later determined to be an anti-neutrino, wrote Giulio Maltese, an Italian physics historian, in "Particles of Man," an article published in 2013 in the journal Lettera Matematica.”
