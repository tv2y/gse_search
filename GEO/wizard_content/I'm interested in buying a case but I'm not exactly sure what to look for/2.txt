Other than just your subjective liking one look over another, it all depends on what you want from it and what you’re going to install into it:

1.   Got lots of external discs? Try getting one which has USB ports on the front, especially if these are USB 3. May also want extras for things like game pads and the like, but again - depends on your requirements.
2.   CD/DVD/BR - ensure there’s a 5.25″ external drive bay for the optical drive to be installed. Else you’ll have to get a USB mounted one.
3.   Going to use SD cards (and the like), e.g. saving pictures from your camera? May want to get one with front SD (or other) slots, or a 3.5″ external drive bay and install a multi-card reader there.
4.   High-end hardware for something like gaming? Get a largish case with lots of ventilation and space for cooling systems.
5.   Lots of storage? Get one with multiple internal bays for HDDs and SSDs (both 3.5″ and 2.5″) as well as enough space for a decent power supply to handle all those.
6.   Silent running? Size may need to increase even more as most of your cooling (including even the power supply) is going to be passive with huge heat-sinks and no (or very large slow moving) fans.
7.   Got a specific motherboard? Ensure the case supports it and is large enough so you can easily install it and all the cables going into it. Seen some where you basically need to remove the motherboard to plug in a new SATA connection - you definitely don’t want one of those.
8.   Specific graphics card (or any other expansion daughter cards)? Make sure the case is long and high enough for that card, as well as having enough expansion openings in the back at the correct placements.

Choosing a PC case is one of the most consequential decisions for performance, noise, cooling, build ease, and future upgrades. Evaluate these factors in a prioritized, practical way rather than on looks alone.

1.   Size and form factor

- Match motherboard form factor: ATX, Micro‑ATX, Mini‑ITX, E‑ATX. Case must explicitly list supported MB sizes; E‑ATX support matters for high‑end builds.
2.   Cooling capacity and airflow

- Fan mounts and included fans: more mounting points = flexibility. Note included fan count and quality.
3.   GPU and CPU cooler clearance

- GPU length clearance: check measured max GPU length with drive cages present/removed.
4.   Build ergonomics and cable management

- Easy-to-remove side panels and hinge options speed builds.
5.   Drive and expansion options

- Number/type of drive bays: 3.5” for HDDs, 2.5” for SSDs. Tool‑less trays or modular brackets are helpful.
6.   Front I/O and connectivity

- USB types and quantity: at minimum one USB‑C (or front USB 3.2 Gen2) plus several USB‑A ports. Headphone jack and power/reset accessibility.
7.   Noise control and acoustic features

- Sound‑damping panels, rubber mounting for HDDs/PSUs, and fan zoning help. Note that heavy soundproofing can restrict airflow; balance noise vs cooling needs.
8.   Materials, construction, and aesthetics

- Tempered glass vs steel: TG shows internals but is heavier and can transmit more noise; steel provides better rigidity.
9.   Power supply compatibility

- PSU length clearance (especially with modular connectors), position (bottom/top), and shroud clearance. Consider SFX for small form‑factor cases.
10.   Dust management and maintenance

- Front/top/bottom dust filters that are easy to remove and clean. Fewer filter-covered intake areas mean easier upkeep.
11.   Future-proofing and upgradeability

- Space for larger GPUs, additional drives, more fans, or bigger radiators. Modular interiors and removable cages extend usable life.
12.   Price and brand/service considerations

- Balance features vs budget. Established brands often offer better documentation, replacement parts and community build guides; smaller manufacturers sometimes offer innovative layouts but check reviews for QC issues.

Quick checklist to use when comparing cases

*   Supported motherboard sizes and maximum GPU/CPU cooler dimensions. 
*   Number and location of fan/radiator mounts; included fans and airflow style (mesh vs glass). 
*   Cable management clearance and presence of grommets/Velcro. 
*   I/O ports (USB‑C? number of USBs), dust filters, tool‑less drive trays. 
*   Build quality (metal gauge, panel fit), tempered glass vs solid panel, and warranty/brand reputation.

Typical tradeoffs to recognize

*   Tempered glass front = looks, worse airflow. Mesh front = better cooling but less showroom appeal. 
*   Compact SFF = smaller footprint, more thermal/noise management and compatibility headaches. 
*   Sound‑dampened case = quieter but often worse cooling unless fan config is tuned.

Practical final step

*   Compare exact specs and clearances on manufacturer pages, then cross‑check with 2–3 recent build reviews or a YouTube build to confirm real‑world airflow, noise, and ease‑of‑build for the specific model.

What should I look for when buying a PC case?

You want to make sure it will fit your components, namely, your motherboard and your video card.

The motherboard part is fairly straightforward because the case sizes match the name of the motherboard.

While a smaller motherboard in most cases will fit in a large case the inverse is not true.

Most full size video cards will fit in the mid tower cases and larger smaller micro and mini builds may require you to measure the card to insure fit.

There is one very easy way to insure fit though go to Pick Parts, Build Your PC, Compare and Share and add all the parts of your system, if there are incompatibilities it will let you know and you can correct them on the fly.

Other factors to consider other than personal preference would be types of USB ports and making sure there are enough 3.5 and 5.25 slots for any hard drives and devices you might want to add.

When determining which type of computer case to buy you first have to determine the size based on the components you intend to use. You’ll want the best overall value, which always doesn’t equate to the cheapest price.

You first have to determine what motherboard you intend to use. That will determine the size or the form factor case you’ll need.

While the intended CPU you plan to use won’t require a specific size case, the video card you intend to use certainly will. Top of the line video cards are large and take up a lot of space. They will NOT fit in small form factor computer cases. If you already have the components, measure carefully.

While size may be the first criteria to consider, just as important is cooling capability. If you intend to use a water cooling system the case has to be large enough to accommodate it. You also should have sufficient space for regular 120 - 140 mm cooling fans; and you can’t have too much cooling capability. You also have to have sufficient slots on the motherboard to accommodate all plug-in components.

There are other considerations that are less critical like aesthetics. Cases come in various styles, including those with tempered glass panels for showcasing components.

Once you have determined these issues, then you start shopping for the case with the best price.

First, make sure that any case you look at is in your price budget. Next, consider what size your motherboard is, as some PC cases can only fit certain sizes, like Micro ATX or ITX.

Be sure to think about what features you want in a case. Do you want to have lots of space to put a bunch of storage like HDDs or SSDs? Do you want your side panel to have a window so you can see what’s inside? Do you need 5.25″ bays in the front of the case for a CD drive or an SD card reader? Do you want the case to have a fan controller? All of these are just some of the features that modern PC cases can come with.

The last thing you might want to consider is if you even like the look of it. For many people, PC building is an art and aesthetics are important to those people. Make sure you like the look of the case, because it’s what you will see the most of.
