It sounds like you’re talking about scene transitions, here, where, say, you want your characters to go from being in the apartment to at the train station without having to spend three pages laboriously describing them putting on jackets, locking up, looking up at the sky as they exit to the sidewalk to see what the weather is like, threading their way through the pedestrians, waiting at the crosswalk, etc. etc. etc. etc.

The way to manage this gracefully is to always keep strongly in your mind what is and is not IMPORTANT to telling your story. Does anything important happen on the way to the train station? An item is important when it either a.) reveals critical information about the characters’ motivations. b.) consists of action that moves the plot forward, particularly actions where characters make difficult decisions, or c.) ties either a. or b. to your theme. If none of that happens, feel free to skip it.

You don’t have to be terribly artistic about it. Something like this is perfectly acceptable:

“Sounds like we need to catch a train.”

“I’ll meet you at the station.”

Ten minutes later, they collected their tickets and stepped onto the platform.

It is much more important that you be clear and brief when doing a time skip than worry too much about being super-evocative or creative with it. If it’s clear to the reader that a.) some time has passed and b.) where the characters are now, good enough. Most novels will have so many of these scene transitions that tying yourself in knots to make them each memorable is a poor focus for your efforts.

I’ve generally found that the way to make an enjoyable transition is always to focus on *action* that reveals what has just taken place, rather than saying outright “they went to the train station”. The characters just SAID they were going to the train station. Mentioning them collecting tickets indicates to the reader that this event has now come to pass. So always think “when my characters arrive at their destination, what will they DO immediately?” Arrive at a restaurant? You request a table. Arrive at a sporting event? Get in line. Arrive at home? Unlock the door, turn on the lights, hang up your coat. Whenever people GO somewhere, they DO something that is specific to the place they’ve arrived. Describe that.

I think some writers do it because they are afraid a straight narrative might not be interesting enough to capture a reader’s attention, so why not put the character in a very difficult situation and hope the reader will care enough to want to see what happened before then.

You create it by saying something like, “Jack could hear the fire engines pull up. If only they could get to him in time. As the smoke curled under the door and flames shot up over the transom,. Jac wished he had taken Mary’s advice last Wednesday when they were in the hardware store.”

Skip an extra line and start a paragraph describing the situation between Jack and Mary and what was going on in the hardware store and then bring the story up to the place where the story was when you began your flashback.

Aside from the date, there are five things that help to specify a time period — but only if your readers are knowledgeable. Fortunately, for historical fiction, they usually are. If it’s a short story of a more general nature in a little known period, you may need to simplify.

1.   Famous people. Everyone has heard of Alexander the Great, Julius Caesar, Jesus, Napoleon, Stalin, Hitler and Einstein. For everybody else, you are guessing what they know. If you include an encounter with Wyatt Earp, or just a newspaper headline that references him, that anyone who knows about that period of American history will get the time period. For everybody else, there’s a chance that they won’t pick up anything.
2.   Famous locations. If you set your story at the time when a famous building is being constructed, then most people will get the period. Some cities and even countries existed only for a relatively short period of time.
3.   Famous events. The destruction of Pompeii, the Battle of Hastings, Agincourt, the Great Fire of London, Waterloo, the siege of Stalingrad and the assassination of JFK will pin down the date nicely. If you know that your target readers are going to be aware of Roncevalles, or the Siege of Vienna, or the Siege of La Rochelle, then many other possibilities open out.
4.   New inventions. Gunpowder, the printing press, steam engine, the steam train, the dynamo, the telegraph, the telephone and the atomic bomb all specify a time.
5.   Culture and fashion. To a lesser extent in written prose, but to a much greater extent in film, clothing, street decoration, architectural styles and even manners and movements express their time. However, most people are only barely consciously aware of them for their own time, and so these tend to be an adjunct to information already given elsewhere.

Rather than relying on just one of these, it might be better to be more subtle with all five. Referencing Anna Bullen rather than Henry VIII let’s anyone know who knows something what period you’re in, and they probably won’t get the rest of the story anyway if they don’t. Likewise, a story set in Imperial Rome doesn’t have to be a tour of all the major landmarks. Just one will suffice, and making it a lesser known one will make the reader more curious. Famous events are very powerful, but easy to overdo. Again, a lesser known event like the battle of Talavera may serve you better than Waterloo. The big inventions are a little obvious. But what about the paper clip? Invented in the 1890s by the GEM corporation, very few people will know when it was actually invented, but they will reasonably guess that it must have been late in the industrial revolution. Likewise, culture and fashion should be sprinkled on like a condiment. You need to know a lot about fashion of previous centuries to be doing more than describing what you saw in a film set in that time. Films are often very accurate, but, at times, they can be hopelessly inaccurate, especially with film-clichés.

So, let’s try it.

Jake walks into a bar opposite the Dome of St Paul’s Cathedral, on the evening of the Great Fire of London. He is carrying one of the newly printed books thanks to Caxton, and hoping to meet Samuel Pepys, the famous diarist…

Actually, no, this is all completely wrong.

In that time, it wouldn’t be ‘Jake’ but ‘Jacob’. It wouldn’t be a ‘bar’, but a tavern, and St Paul’s Cathedral’s was rebuilt by Christopher Wren, with its famous dome, after the Great Fire of London. Printing had been around for a couple of centuries at that point, and Pepys was not famous as a diarist until long after his death, when his diaries, which he wrote in code, were deciphered.

Let’s try again.

Jacob Templeton, a gentleman, walks into a tavern on Cheapside. He had made his money in Dutch tulip speculation some thirty years before, as a young man. He is looking to meet his nephew, who was a fellow Cromwellian, but has converted to the Royalist cause, in the hope that he can make some introductions for him which will enable him to straighten out his affairs, which have become complicated by the Dutch Wars. A rush of feet on the street outside is accompanied by cries of ‘fire’ and an acrid smell.

Time-lapse in prose compresses chronology while preserving immediacy. The effect lets readers feel the passage of time and see change without slogging through every moment. Use these techniques, often combined, to achieve a “showing” summary.

1.   Choose the rhythm and scale

- Decide how long the period is (hours, weeks, years) and the emotional/plot importance. Short, important spans need more scenes; long, transitional spans benefit from compression.

- Pick a narrator stance (close third, limited first, omniscient) — closer POV makes showing feel vivid even when compressed.
2.   Use montage sentences

- Chain short, sensory images and actions in a single paragraph to imply continuous time: “They hammered nails, painted the door, argued about the color, smoked in silence until the sky went from bruised purple to the pale smudge of morning.” Each clause is a beat; together they read like film montage.
3.   Employ repeated motifs or anchors

- Introduce a small recurring detail (a clock, a scar, a plant) and show its gradual change: “The sapling grew taller than the fence; its first red leaf browned and dropped.” The motif compresses time by signaling cumulative change.
4.   Focus on consequential changes

- Skip the mundane and spotlight visible outcomes: clothing, wounds, relationships, weathered objects. Showing the result implies the process: “Her shortlist of regrets now had a new, clean line through his name.”
5.   Use parallelism and lists

- Parallel structures and compact lists give momentum: “Letters arrived: short, urgent, forgiving. Replies were rare, then frequent, then none.” The pattern communicates evolution.
6.   Rely on verbs that imply duration

- Choose verbs that carry process: “tattooed,” “mended,” “gnawed,” “ripened.” Pair them with temporal adverbs sparsely: “By spring the cottage had been boarded, painted, refitted.”
7.   Slidetime transitions (bridging lines)

- Use a single transitional sentence to jump forward while retaining scene energy: “Weeks of rain later, the river had claimed the path.” This keeps narrative tempo without a full scene.
8.   Use dialogue fragments and ellipses

- Short exchanges across time can feel like snapshots: “‘You’ll come back?’ ‘Maybe.’ Months passed.” Minimal dialogue gives readers pieces to assemble.
9.   Integrate documents and ephemera

- Letters, lists, receipts, diary entries, headlines, or captions can summarize events while preserving show: “June 12 — invoice paid. June 29 — landlord’s notice.” These artifacts act as visible evidence.
10.   Control pacing with paragraph breaks and sentence length

- Dense, clipped sentences and short paragraphs accelerate time; longer descriptive sentences slow it. For time-lapse, favor compact sentences and tight paragraphs.
11.   Let cause-and-effect lean on implication

- Show only a few causal steps and allow readers to infer the rest: “He stopped answering. The photos came down.” The reader supplies the in-between.
12.   Anchor emotion to accumulation

- Rather than narrating feelings continuously, show how small accumulations alter emotion: “At first she laughed it off. By the fiftieth missed call she had stopped believing in excuses.” The progression is apparent without explicit summary.

Examples (templates you can adapt)

*   Montage line: “They painted, argued, made compromises, broke a window, learned to dance in the kitchen; by winter the house had a name.”
*   Motif growth: “The baby tooth slid loose on spring day one; by summer she was rinsing with mouthwash and signing rent forms.”
*   Ephemera log: “March 3: curtains ordered. March 26: curtains delivered. April 14: curtains packed for moving out.”

Practical exercise

*   Take a week-long episode from your plot. Write it three ways: full scene-by-scene, a single montage paragraph, and as a sequence of artifacts (notes, bills, captions). Compare which conveys necessary beats and emotional truth with least prose.

Caveats

*   Don’t compress pivotal scenes that need immediate sensory detail. Use time-lapse for transitions, training sequences, or background developments.
*   Avoid overuse: constant compression makes stakes feel muted. Alternate full scenes and time-lapses to sustain engagement.

Outcome

*   Effective time-lapse gives readers the feel of continuity and change while keeping narrative momentum. Use compact sensory beats, repeating motifs, consequential details, and montage techniques to show rather than tell the passage of time.

This is a really fun question! While I agree with some that “there are no rules,” you’re clearly asking because you’re looking for a process or a structure to help carry your ideas through. (I’m also assuming you’re writing fiction.)

I’ve never tackled this particular challenge, but if I held to the task, here’s how I’d do it:

1.   Research!

    1.   Re-watch “Terminator” 1 and 2; take notes on how time is represented, referenced to, and
    2.   Re-read “The Time Traveler’s Wife.” The author does an incredible job keeping track of time through intertwining narratives and perspectives without ever losing the reader. It’s a master-class in time-travel writing.
    3.   Watch the film “Timecrimes” — it’s a deftly written, Spanish-language thriller that involves a very tight time-loop sequence of about an hour. The narrative unfolds brilliantly.

2.   Sketch a very rough chapter outline of the book, linearly through the narrative of the book. Ignore specific dates and events, just get a general sense of where the story goes. This will be your Book’s Frame of Reference, or BFoR.
3.   Plot the earliest and latest dates explored in the proposed book on a timeline. Draw this linearly on a white-board or large sheet of paper. Let’s call this “Time’s Frame of Reference” or TFoR.
4.   Now annotate the timeline with where the protagonist appears throughout TFoR in order for the plot to work.

    1.   Keep in mind that your protagonist no longer has a linear Time Frame of Reference — s/he only has the BFoR to carry them along.

5.   Now align the book’s chapter numbers and headers along the linear timeline: e.g.,

    1.   January 1 = Chapter 7.
    2.   February 1 = Chapter 16.
    3.   March 1 = Chapter 11.
    4.   April 1 = Chapter 1.

6.   Back to the protagonist: jot some details about him/her on each appearance in the TFoR. If the book takes place over a long period of the protagonist’s life, then jot down the protag’s age at each appearance (this is where “The Time Traveler’s Wife” will provide excellent research).

    1.   It’ll be important for you to give some distinguishing characteristics for your readers to be able to keep track of the protagonist through the BFoR: a dramatic hair cut, an injury, a scar, grey hair and wrinkles, etc.

7.   Now start plotting other characters along the timeline. If they time-travel as well, you’ll have to repeat #6 for each of them so your readers can keep track.
8.   Start filling out chapter details along the linear timeline, referencing your BFoR to make sure the narrative itself can be followed along.

Alright, that should be enough to get started. Again, I’ve not road-tested this, but if a contract for a time-travel story dropped on my desk today, that’s where I’d start.

Best of luck, and I look forward to reading it!

100 frames is about four seconds playback. If the scene turns out to be interesting, you will have a usable shot. If you stopped at, say, 80 frames, you will have a clip so short it is useless no matter what. This will really annoy you, especially if the shot turns out to be interesting.

https://youtu.be/vh-hQbShrk8

The first few shots in this example you can see that there were not enough frames (only three seconds worth) and the appearance is jarring. just imagine the uselessness of only two seconds.

Some times, you might have a use for 200 frame clips (8 seconds) or even super meditative process voyage clips of over 700 frames (half a minute playback.) These can take whatever time it takes to get the story you want. If you are shooting the construction of a building, your shot will have to be “made a building” in length.

When shooting, 100 frames could be under 7 minutes if you are doing, say, two second exposures at four second intervals, so it doesn’t take too much patience to let the shot roll out. It is easiest to just make a conscious decision at the beginning like, “minimum eight minutes unless there is a reason to keep rolling.” Near sunset that is more than enough to go from setting sun to blue hour, for example. You can also plan all this with something like photo pills, so you don’t start too early.

On that note, unless you are doing a powered multi day shot, it is a good idea to roll even longer than your plan on any possibility of something looking cool. You can always cut the boring parts off later, and you don’t want to have two unusable clips instead of one good one.

You need an intervalometer, either as built-in feature of your camera or as an external cable remote.

It depends on how fast you want things to move and how long a time stretch you’d like to include in your time lapse. Also it is good to know what is your desired frame rate. I live in PAL region and as a cameraman I shoot FullHD/25p. So my frame rate is 25 frames per second. If I want to squeeze five real minutes in one second of my time lapse (thus making the whole video sequence cover 7.5 hours), I have to make one shot in twelve seconds (5 frames per minute, 25 frames per 5 minutes). If I want my final video to be 50p, it will take 10 frames per minute (one frame in six seconds).

If you want things to roll out faster, you have to make stills more frequently, if slower — less frequently.

When you have your shots ready, you go to video editor of your choice (unless your camera has own time lapse creation engine, read instruction manual for that), import all stills you shot and set length in frames for each shot.

Keith Loutit used a special lens called a tilt-shift lens. It is first important to understand tilt-shift. All lenses in general create images of whatever is placed in front of them. When the images coincide with a sensor (film, CCD, CMOS, whatever), they are in focus. When the images don't, they are blurry. Typical lenses have a large depth of focus when taking photos of landscape, which means that most scenery in front of the lens can be focused on the sensor. Instead of tilting the lens, think of tilting and shifting the sensor. If done correctly, only a part of the sensor will be at the proper distance from the lens. This part of the sensor will be in focus, while the rest of the sensor won't be. This causes large parts of the image to be blurry, with only parts of the image in focus. This in-camera effect depends, then, on the orientation of the sensor and the distance of the objects. This effectively can artificially reduce the depth of field.

Replicating this effect in software exactly is impossible, because the distance information is lost. However, it can be approximated by blurring most of the image and keeping a region of it in focus.

This effect makes things look small because our eyes have a narrow depth of focus when looking at things close to us. The narrow depth of focus in the photo creates an optical illusion that takes advantage of how we are used to seeing our world.

Then, creating a video is just time-lapse photography. Attaching an intervalometer to a camera and setting it to fire every second or so and then turning it into a video (at ~30 fps) speeds things up by ~30x. This contributes even more strongly to the miniaturization effect because we expect small things to move at a certain speed. For instance, an ant scurries across a small napkin quickly, while a human takes a lot longer to cross a parking lot. The (hundreds) of photos can then be compiled in software (possibly Adobe video software, for instance), and turned into a video.
