Blank verse is unrhymed iambic pentameter, popular in drama and poetry due to its natural speech-like quality. Introduced to English poetry by Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey, in the 16th century, it became dominant in Renaissance drama through writers like Christopher Marlowe and William Shakespeare. John Milton revived it for epic poetry with *Paradise Lost*, influencing later poets.  

During the Romantic era, poets like William Wordsworth, Samuel Taylor Coleridge, and Percy Bysshe Shelley explored blank verse for lyrical and narrative works. Victorian poets Robert Browning and Alfred Tennyson used it for dramatic monologues.  

In the 20th century, Robert Frost and Wallace Stevens adapted blank verse, blending regularity with variation. Modern poets like Derek Walcott, Seamus Heaney, and Adrienne Rich continued its use, proving its enduring flexibility. While modernism favored free verse, blank verse remains relevant, particularly in narrative and conversational poetry.
