The domestic chicken (Gallus gallus domesticus) is a human-created form of a wild bird lineage; its “natural habitat” is best understood by looking at its wild ancestor and feral descendants.

Origin and ancestral habitat

*   Ancestor species: red junglefowl (Gallus gallus), native to South and Southeast Asia (India to Indonesia, southern China).
*   Typical habitat of red junglefowl: lowland tropical and subtropical forests, forest edges, secondary growth, bamboo thickets, scrub, rice paddies and hedgerows. They prefer areas with dense cover for roosting and brooding and open ground or light understory for foraging.
*   Behavior adapted to that habitat: ground-foraging for seeds, insects and fruit; roosting in trees at night to avoid predators; using dense vegetation for nesting and hiding.

How domestication changed habitat use

*   Domestication began at least ~8,000 years ago from red junglefowl and possibly with genetic input from other Gallus subspecies. Humans moved chickens into barns, yards and agricultural landscapes.
*   Domestic chickens thrive in a much wider range: farmyards, free-range pastures, urban backyards, small coops, and intensive poultry houses. Their ecological needs—cover, forage, water, shelter, safe nesting and roosting—remain the same but can be supplied by human structures.

Feral chickens and habitat flexibility

*   Feral populations (escaped or released domestic birds) re-establish in habitats similar to the ancestral niche: forest edges, gardens, coastal scrub, plantations, and peri-urban areas where food and shelter are available.
*   Examples: Oahu and some Pacific islands, parts of the Caribbean, and rural areas worldwide where chickens live wild, forage for insects, and roost in trees.

Key habitat elements for chickens (wild or domestic)

1.   Foraging substrate: loose soil, leaf litter, or pasture for scratching and finding seeds/invertebrates.
2.   Cover: shrubs, tall grass, hedges or forest edge to hide from predators and to nest.
3.   Roosts: elevated branches or rafters for safe nighttime roosting.
4.   Water source: reliable drinking water.
5.   Microclimate: shade and shelter from rain and cold; moderate temperatures suit them best (tropical to temperate).

Why farms feel “natural”

*   Humans recreated critical habitat elements: shelter, food, water and predator management. Because chickens are highly adaptable and have co-evolved with humans for millennia, their presence on farms seems intrinsic—but it is a result of domestication and habitat modification.

Summary

*   Natural ancestral habitat: lowland tropical/subtropical forest edges and secondary growth of South and Southeast Asia.
*   Domestic chickens now occupy a broad range of human-modified habitats; feral chickens typically revert to environments resembling the ancestral forest-edge/secondary-growth niche.

Unlike many domesticated animals, the wild ancestor of the domesticated chicken is actually still alive and well as a species.

Meet Gallus gallus, the Red Jungle Fowl

See that sleek build and alert, nervous stance, ready to take off at first sign of danger? This is a successful wild bird.

Domesticated chickens were also crossed with another wild bird, though they don’t carry nearly as many genes from this one. That one is Gallus sonnerati, the Grey Jungle Fowl.

These birds have no problems surviving in the wild. They fly well, roost in trees during the night, and live in a similar fashion to other wild ground birds such as grouse and turkeys.

These tropical birds range across India into Indonesia, Southern China, Malaysia, etc.

Due to the number of folks in India pointing out that village chickens look very similar to red jungle fowl, I figured I’d better edit my answer to address this — the similarity in appearance is due in part to some low-level hybridization, and in large part due to the fact that the village chickens haven’t been selectively bred to LOOK different from their wild ancestors. There are some subtle differences in feather patterns and colors, and in their vocalizations, which tell them apart, however.

The jungle fowl hens look much less similar to village chicken hens than the wild roosters do to domesticated roosters. Village chickens will lay eggs regularly, probably over a hundred eggs a year, whether there are roosters around or not. Jungle fowl only lay if they have a mate, and only a couple of clutches per year, maybe 20 eggs or so.

Village chickens, of course, live in villages. Jungle fowl live… well, in the jungle. So no matter how superficially similar the village chickens you see appear to be to these pictures, it isn’t the same bird.

There are wild chickens roaming the world!

But not many.

I personally knew,when I lived in another state, of a colony of feral chickens, apparently descended from fighting cock breeds. They lived together in an abandoned barn surrounded by trees and brush near a small river. Pretty special conditions! They had access to water, lots of cover, and places to roost twenty to thirty feet off the ground, in an area when not very many people go, where there was both lots of grass and also varied riparian plant communities including large oak trees.

Also, I captured one by accident—rescued her from a dog, kept her til she healed, and invited her to join my other chickens, which she did. She was the smartest chicken I ever had, by a long shot.

All this is not a set of circumstances easily duplicated. Chickens do not have as many natural advantages as pheasant or guinea fowl, and have a harder time of it in the wild—even chickens that are extraordinarily intelligent and from aggressive, adaptable, and self-protective breeds.

And its even possible that the whole colony was watched over, and occasionally fed and protected, by guys who would now and then capture one to breed in with their fighting stock.

But whenever I would see them calling to one another and walking along the exposed beams ad rafters of the crumbling wooden barn, they did seem to be having a good time.
