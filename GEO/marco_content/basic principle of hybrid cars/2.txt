WHAT IS A HYBRID VEHICLE?
-------------------------

Put simply, hybrid cars**combine**a standard**internal combustion engine (ICE)**with an**electric**one. All have a conventional engine, an electric motor, and a battery. Because they straddle the line between gas and electricity power, hybrid vehicles have**unique demands**that may**shorten their operating lifespan** if they’re not taken care of properly. Still, technology is constantly evolving, problems are eliminated, and innovative solutions are being developed in order to mitigate the remaining ones.

### THE HISTORY OF THE TECHNOLOGY

As we mentioned, hybrid cars were conceived over a hundred years ago. The first patent applications for hybrid gasoline-electric rail car and boat propulsion systems were filed way back in 1889.

#### BUT WHEN DID THE FIRST HYBRID CAR COME OUT?

Earlier than you think. The first car that used electric power was**developed in 1896 by Harry E. Dey**. The**Armstrong Phaeton**was an innovative vehicle that had a dynamo flywheel connected to a battery and used**regenerative braking**to charge it. The dynamo was also used to power the lights, and it provided the ignition spark.

Phaeton was**followed by Ferdinand Porsche’s Mixte in 1900**. The car used a gasoline engine to supply power to an electric motor that drove the car’s wheels. The prototype only had front-wheel drive, but later models included an electric engine for each of the wheels.

#### REGENERATIVE BRAKING

The regenerative braking system pioneered in the Phaeton became a**core design concept of modern hybrid electric vehicles**. The modern principle was developed for the American Motors Amitron concept car. This was a fully battery-powered electric vehicle that was charged by braking.

This principle was further**refined and used in true hybrids later**. These vehicles used an electric engine to**assist in driving**, and some were capable of**fully switching to electric**power for brief periods.

#### MODERN HYBRIDS AND THE LATEST DEVELOPMENTS

The renewed surge of interest in the late 1990s has led to some remarkable developments. The**Toyota Prius**was the most popular hybrid vehicle and has become basically**synonymous with the term hybrid**. It gave the technology the foothold it needed to stay relevant long enough and reach a critical mass on the market.

The Prius is the most popular hybrid electric vehicle ever produced, and its technology was used as a basis for countless vehicles that followed in its wake.

The latest development that’s gaining mainstream traction is the**plug-in system**that allows hybrid vehicles to operate for an extended period of time in all-electric mode.

We can also expect that**nickel-cadmium**batteries will be phased out because of their**weight and environmental impact**. They also don’t charge as well as other battery types. Reduced-weight, nano-textured battery packs seem to be the future, and lithium-ion batteries might replace the current technology.

### TYPES OF HYBRID VEHICLES

Not all hybrids are made equal. While any car that relies on**two different power sources**for propulsion can be called a hybrid, we recognize multiple types. There are three different types according to the**degree of hybridization**and three more according to the**powertrain configuration**.

#### CLASSIFICATION ACCORDING TO THE DEGREE OF HYBRIDIZATION

Here, we have full hybrids, mild hybrids, and full plug-in hybrids.

*   **Full hybrids**can run on either of the engines or on both together. The battery is charged by the combustion engine while it’s running and it cannot be plugged in to charge.
*   In**mild hybrids**, the electric and the combustion engine always work together in parallel; they cannot operate separately.
*   **Full plug-in hybrids**require plugging in to fully recharge the battery. They can be operated in**charge-depleting and charge-sustaining modes**. It’s also possible to**switch between them**to optimize battery mileage and fuel economy.

#### CLASSIFICATION ACCORDING TO THE POWERTRAIN CONFIGURATION

Hybrids also have three different types of powertrains. While ordinary internal combustion powertrain comprises the engine, transmission, suspension, wheels, and drive shaft. Hybrid vehicles have**additional powertrain components**, and they’ve been built to maximize the benefits of the design. The three powertrain configurations are series, parallel, and series-parallel.

*   **Parallel hybrids**have both the internal combustion engine and the electric motor connected to the mechanical transmission. They can**transmit power to drive the wheels simultaneously**, and they usually do so via a conventional transmission. Their internal combustion engine can also be used as a generator for supplemental recharging.
*   **Series hybrids**have the drivetrain powered by their electric motor, while a smaller internal combustion engine powers the electric motor or recharges the batteries.
*   **Series-parallel hybrids**have the benefits of both series and parallel drivetrains. Their electric motor or internal combustion engine can**generate power to the wheels independently**.

CARE AND MAINTENANCE
--------------------

In general, hybrid electric vehicles don’t require special maintenance procedures. The basics are the same as with conventional internal combustion engines.

### THE BASICS

The electrical components in hybrid cars are**designed to be durable and maintenance-free**. It’s still a good idea to**check the power cords and connections**every now and then, but other than that, electric cars don't require specialist maintenance.

**Battery life tends to be poorer**with hybrid cars – especially in older models – because they’re used much more than in ICE vehicles. Fortunately, batteries are usually covered by the manufacturer’s warranty, and it’s possible to replace them if any issues crop up.

The**regenerative braking system**and the**lower heat**mean that**brake pads and brakes usually last significantly longer**. The lower overall temperature does have some downsides; more on that later.

**The Evaporative emissions (EVAP) system**is relatively prone to leaks in hybrid vehicles. The common symptoms are the active Check Engine light and the smell of gasoline. Since this is relatively difficult to diagnose and fix, it’s prudent to have regular checkups with the mechanic.

**Oxygen sensors**tend to break down more frequently in hybrid vehicles and that can significantly affect**fuel efficiency and engine performance**. Their performance diminishes with age, so it’s a good idea to have them checked during regular maintenance.

### WHAT’S DIFFERENT

However, due to their design and mode of operation, hybrid vehicles do have some**unique requirements**. The increased frequency of the stop-start cycle of the internal combustion engine (it can be up to ten times higher) puts additional strain on the engine. This leads to an**aggressive oil cycle and increased acid formation**. While regular oils will definitely work for a hybrid vehicle, lubricants that are designed with these quirks can improve the performance and longevity of the engine.

This is why Valvoline developed a series of oils and transmission fluids that are formulated specifically for hybrid vehicles.

THE IMPORTANCE OF USING THE RIGHT HYBRID OIL
--------------------------------------------

Hybrid engines tend to run cooler because the electric engine takes off some of the load of the internal combustion engine. This means that they can’t reach the**optimal operating temperature**required to boil off water condensed in the oil. Since the**oil is cooler**too, it may not be able to lubricate the vehicle parts the way it was designed. Having the right hybrid engine oil for your vehicle can significantly**reduce wear and tear**, minimize sludge formation, and extend the life of the engine.

In addition, since the electric motor is a part of the drivetrain in hybrid vehicles, the lubricant can come into contact with electrical components and**receive the electric charge**. This is a major concern, and transmission fluids designed for hybrid vehicles need to have the right e**lectrical and conductive properties**. They also need to be compatible with insulating materials and coatings they will come into contact with.

Oxidation is another factor that needs to be taken into account. High temperatures can lead to**oxidation and deterioration of the oil**. The result is increased conductivity which puts seals and bearings at risk. The fluid needs to be able to cool the electric components of the motor in order to prevent oil oxidation which can lead to**interference in sensitive electric and electronic components**.

These challenges need to be addressed with an intelligent lubricant in order to maintain the longevity of the vehicle’s fluids and the lifespan of the engine and transmission.

HOW TO USE HYBRID OILS PROPERLY
-------------------------------

When it comes to usage, there’s not much difference between hybrid and regular oils. Since the combustion engines function the same and the powertrain operates on similar principles, you can**make do with the standard range of products**. Mineral, synthetic, semi-synthetic, and high-mileage oils can all be used in hybrid vehicles. Even hybrids designed with ICUs as range extenders need**standard engine oils**in order to function properly; of course, these oils must be used in accordance with manufacturer specifications.

### VISCOSITY

Viscosity is a fluid’s**resistance to flow**. This is where manufacturer specifications come in. Always refer to those and consider this a set of general guidelines. A majority of hybrid cars work best with**lower-weight oils**. Hybrid engine oils specifically formulated to be used by hybrid vehicles provides optimal performance and ensure that the engine is well-lubricated while improving fuel economy.

Valvoline Hybrid Engine Oil has been designed to deal with the**frequent stop-start cycles**of the vehicle's combustion engine. The lower viscosity grade also**improves oil flow at start-up**, allows the oil to**build up pressure more quickly**, and ensures the engine r**eaches its operating temperature faster**.

### OIL CHANGE FREQUENCY FOR HYBRID CARS

The oil change frequency is similar to regular ICE vehicles. The change interval for hybrids using**mineral oils**is**5,000 miles**.**Synthetic oils**have special additives that allow them to last longer and need to be replaced**every 7,000 to 10,000 miles**. Of course, they cost more but the added protection is a significant advantage.

Since hybrid engines aren’t put under as much strain at low speeds, it’s possible to stretch these intervals a bit longer, especially with the use of specially formulated hybrid synthetic oils. That said, it’s always safer to stick to **manufacturer recommendations**.

### SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS

Manufacturers provide exact specifications for the diesel or gasoline engines installed in hybrid vehicles. Since the basic principle of function is the same no matter where the ICE is located, most hybrid and regular oils can be used interchangeably.

Still, specially designed**hybrid engine oils and transmission fluids**offer**improved performance and longevity**.

### FOLLOW MANUFACTURER SPECIFICATIONS

Every**car owner's manual**lists recommended oil weight and any other considerations. The**viscosity may need to be adjusted depending on the season**and the expected use of the car. For standard use in moderate temperatures, what’s listed in your owner’s manual is fine. Always choose an oil from a brand that displays the starburst symbol that indicates the oil has been tested by the**American Petroleum Institute (API)**.
