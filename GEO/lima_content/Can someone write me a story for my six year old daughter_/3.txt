Thanks for the A2A.

Children are natural story tellers. They love to explore the world and create stories to explain this world. While their overall worldview is limited, given the chance, some pretty amazing ideas can come from their thought processes.

Some nights, instead of you reading to them, ask them to tell you a story. Let them know they can make up whatever they want. You could start by giving them a character and asking why or what. So, you think you’ve drafted a tweet, an email, a short story, or even a novel. These are different forms of communication, but the process of bringing them to fruition has a necessary, sometimes overlooked step: editing! Unless you’re a professional writer, it’s unlikely that you have an editor who can review your writing regularly. Here are some tips to help you review your own work.

1.   Give your writing some space. Have you ever felt a mix of pure relief and joy when you’ve finished a draft of something? Don’t downplay that feeling and the ability to walk away from your work before you start editing it. You may need minutes, hours, or days, but once you sit back down with what you originally had on the page, you’ll have the thrill of looking at it with fresh eyes. You’ll notice errors you may not have seen the first time. You’ll come to new realizations about its overall tone and structure. If it’s a text or email, maybe you only need a few minutes away from it. If it’s a story or essay, perhaps you’ll need longer. Regardless of what type of work it is, it will help your writing tremendously.
2.   Don’t use overachieving synonyms. Looking at your work for the second, third, or fourth time around may inspire you to spice up your language with longer, more uncommon words. There’s nothing wrong with having a thesaurus nearby, but try to limit the repetition of long, pretentious-feeling words so your work flows well and doesn’t feel too bogged down. At the end of the day, you want it to feel true to you and the message you’re conveying.
3.   Remember who the reader is. Don’t forget your own voice as the writer—but don’t forget who your reader is. Many writers get too close to their work; editing is a chance to try to get out of your own head. Who is your ideal reader? What do you want them to take away from the writing? It’s a unique time to step in their shoes, to make sure your communication is as effective as you’d like it to be.
4.   Kill your darlings. Don’t be scared to remove chunks of your work, even if it feels precious to you. If it’s a passage that’s really tough to part with, try saving it somewhere else, so you can return to it later in your piece or for another work.
5.   Use Grammarly. Last but not least, Grammarly has countless resources for editing your work. Our writing assistant helps you find areas of your writing that are unclear or too wordy, as well as help you find mistakes you might not have caught.

I taught myself to read when I was three, with the help of a teacher at a state run daycare center where I stayed while my single mother and my grandmother worked. My parents didn’t know I could read until we were driving down the highway and they were looking for an exit ramp and spelled the name of it, and I said, “oh, you mean this one” while pointing to it. I was five or six at that time. Writing and the desire to write are something a person is born with. I started writing poetry at 8 because the children’s ministries director at our church (youth pastor) was leaving and I wanted to do something to tell him goodbye.

Somewhere along the line, I got the idea that I couldn’t make a living as a writer, so I’ve done all of this other stuff (teaching, directing, stage managing, real estate) and I was pretty good at all of them, but I kept coming back to the point where I wasn’t truly happy. I was content, but that’s a different animal. Until the day came when my real estate brokerage brought in someone to do mindset training with us, and that person asked us to picture our biggest goal. Mine had nothing to do with real estate and everything to do with writing. Though I didn’t and couldn’t tell anyone there about it, that stopped me in my tracks because I found I couldn’t ignore my own mind telling me that this was what was most important to me.

Now, I’m developing a couple of series for the television industry and I’m knee deep in tweaking and finishing a novel I started writing a while ago. My point in telling you all of this is that everyone has something he or she was born to do, and the things that we are naturally interested in when we are young often manifest into some part of that, but it shouldn’t be forced by someone else, but discovered by the person themselves, even if it takes a while for them to realize it for what it is.

I’m editing this to add: when my niece was six (she’s now 11) she saw me writing on my laptop at night and would ask what I was doing. I told her and she wanted us to write a story together, about my Romanian vampire kitty. We did that, but only when she wanted to do so. She told me what to write and I wrote it and then she drew the pictures. In the process, we found out that drawing is her thing. We never really finished the story, though she still asks to write on it sometimes, and I consider it a new experience for her, but she loved drawing those pictures and still does love drawing even now.

Don’t criticize; only point out the good stuff and ask about whatever isn’t clear or is missing. This is a young soul that could be shot down maybe permanently if the criticism isn’t kind and useful.

Keep a notebook or computer file of his/her work, including first draft through final version. Becoming a writer is not something I believe can or should be forced on someone. My parents gave me a blank journal for my 8th birthday. I had not up to that point connected with the idea that people wrote books. When I discovered that, I wanted to do that too.

But it could have just as easily gone another way. Not everyone with good reading or writing skills becomes a writer. Some people who don't read do write as well, though I don't advise it. One friend or mine who seldom reads has written and produced about 50 short and long form movies. One of my daughters who lives with dyslexia has written two full-length screenplays. You just never know with people. Motivation is a pretty huge factor.

I have four children actually, who all grew up to be writers. I never told them they had to, but they watched me and their father writing for years. If you have a child you'd like to encourage in that direction, it doesn't hurt to do some reading and writing yourself. Example is a great teacher.

Till the age of 32, I did not know what good writing meant. Nobody taught me or demanded it from me.

Throughout my schooling (in Kendriya Vidyalaya) and college, ‘quality writing’ was not even a thing. I doubt if even any of my English teachers knew how to write well. Essays were just meant to fill the pages with words (e.g., write a 1000 word essay on blah…).

Working in the IPS further wrecked my writing skills.

Here is a typical Sarkari communication - “Your kind attention is drawn to the reference cited. Your good self may kindly be pleased to take appropriate action. I will be highly obliged if the undersigned could be informed about the outcome.”

Full of passive voice, verbosity… you name it.

For my MBA applications, for the first time, I had to tell a story. Since I was competing with a global pool of applicants, I was forced to up my game. That was my first attempt at good writing.

At McKinsey, I could further sharpen my skills since I spent as much time on writing as on problem-solving.

I still have a long way to go, but here are a few lessons. I am assuming that the reader already has the basic grammar and composition sorted.

Let us start with a few hygiene factors:

1.   Omit needless words:

    1.   I picked this from Strunk and White’s classic on writing. Unnecessary words are like dust on a glass window - they muddy up the beauty of your writing.
    2.   A few examples

    
        1.   Instead of ‘In order to achieve our goals’, just say ‘To achieve our goals.’
        2.   Don’t write ‘In my opinion, we should do blah…’ Just say ‘We should do blah…’ Your writing is your opinion, anyway.

2.   Use active voice: Instead of, ‘The project was done by two interns,’ make it, ‘Two interns did the project.’
3.   Cut down the use of adjectives/adverbs: Don’t say, ‘The exam was very hard.’ Just say, ‘The exam was hard.’
4.   Show, don’t tell.

    1.   “The journey to the peak was an arduous climb” - this ‘tells’ the reader, who has to recreate the feeling in his head.
    2.   “Halfway up the journey, my calf muscles were on fire” - This is better as the reader can feel that it was arduous. We don’t have to tell.

5.   Stick to the rules, but not all the time. When you break the rules, it should be intentional, and it should hit the reader.

The above will make your writing clean and easy to read. But to make people cry or laugh or angry, you need to give it some punch.

Here are a few suggestions:

1.   Edit mercilessly. The punch comes from editing, not from the first draft.

    1.   Ask yourself, ‘What is the key point.’ Delete all the other words. Then add back words only if they really change the meaning. See example below:

    
        1.   A bad, verbose example: “Based on the facts and our past experience, we have a few corrective actions to recommend. We suggest that the client at least starts with the following three initiatives - A, B, and C.”.
        2.   Better, tighter writing: “To conclude, we recommend three initiatives - A, B, and C.”

    2.   After a round of editing, take a break and come back after a few hours, or a day. You will be surprised at the number of mistakes you will catch.
    3.   If you are just starting, try cutting down the word count of the first draft by 50%. Trust me - it will become better.

2.   Some people say ‘Write only when you are angry.’ Don’t wait for the right mood, but feel the rage, the excitement, or the fear if you want to move the reader.
3.   Specifics over generality:

    1.   Instead of saying, ‘The affluence level in country A has gone up a lot in the last 50 years’
    2.   Try saying something like, ‘Today, every family in country A has two cars on average. Fifty years back, only the king had one.’

4.   Don’t be afraid to offend. Don’t please everyone. Hit hard.
5.   Storify it. Facts and data don’t move people. One thousand people dead or 100,000 people dead - it is just a few more zeros. But stories evoke emotion.

    1.   E.g., the Syrian civil war killed lakhs, but it did not move anyone. But the photo of Aylan Kurdi, a toddler who died when his family was immigrating, changed the sentiments of Europe towards Syrian immigrants. Before that, tens of thousands of kids were killed, but nobody cared. Why? Because we can relate to stories but not statistics.

Before I finish, here is an important disclaimer:

I am not a professional writer. My training is in Finance and Engineering. Hence please treat the above as learnings of an amateur, and not an authoritative set of rules.

In summary: Write a lot. Edit brutally. Cut out the junk. Feel the rage. Tell the story. Don’t dilute. Write fearlessly.

Best wishes

- Rajan

* * *

Note: I removed the photo of Aylan Kurdi since some readers found it distressing. But we should ask ourselves, why?

Lakhs of people have died in Syria, and yet we can easily close our eyes to that. But one story has the power to change the way we think. Remember, closing our eyes does not change reality. It only hides it from us.
