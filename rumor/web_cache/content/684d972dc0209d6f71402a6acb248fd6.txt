Roasted and barbecued foods develop advanced glycation endproducts (AGEs), which are mild to moderate carcinogens. Burnt foods are seriously worse in this respect. The intense heat dries out the surface of the food and causes amines and acids (and amines and aldehydes) to condense into AGEs. (Look up acrylamide to get further information.) Burning does the same thing, but adds partial combustion byproducts, too. The food that goes all the way to carbon is not a concern, but much of the black that is visible is not fully combusted. Polyaromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are not only carcinogens, they are difficult for some people's livers to detoxify.

Eating burnt food occasionally is generally not harmful, but it can have some negative effects. Here are a few key points to consider:

1.   Potential Toxins: Burnt food can contain harmful compounds, such as acrylamide, which is formed when certain foods are cooked at high temperatures. Acrylamide has been linked to cancer in animal studies, though its effects on humans are still being studied.
2.   Nutritional Loss: Overcooking or burning food can destroy some of its nutrients, particularly vitamins sensitive to heat, like vitamin C and some B vitamins.
3.   Digestive Discomfort: Consuming burnt food may cause digestive issues for some people, leading to discomfort or nausea.
4.   Taste and Texture: Burnt food typically has an unpleasant taste and texture, which may reduce the enjoyment of your meal.

In moderation, eating burnt food is unlikely to cause serious harm, but it’s best to avoid making it a regular part of your diet. If you frequently consume burnt food, consider adjusting your cooking methods to prevent charring.

Eating Burnt Food may increase the chances of Cancer:

There’s evidence that suggests that eating all that burned food could actually be bad for you.

Back in 2007, a Dutch study was done that showed an increased risk of cancer in women who were exposed to the chemical acrylamide — which forms on fried or baked foods, especially when those foods are burned. The study found that women who ate 40 micrograms of acrylamide a day (equivalent to say, a bag of potato chips) had double the cancer risk of women who ate the lowest amount of the chemical.

So what is this chemical acrylamide anyway?Acrylamide is a chemical that is can be used in the manufacturing of paper and plastic, and is often found in products like caulk and food packaging. Alarmingly, though, acrylamide is also found in certain foods that have been prepared at a very high temperature — i.e. food that was fried, grilled or broiled. It’s found more in starchy products like bread and potatoes. How come? An amino acid called asparagine found in these foods forms acrylamide when heated at a very high temperature.

Another concern is the HCAs (heterocyclic amines) that form on chicken and meat when grilled over a high flame. HCAs have also been linked to cancer in animals, though research in humans is still limited.

It should be noted that the FDA does not regulate the amount of HCAs or acrylamide in food, so it is up to you to lessen your exposure.

So, Don't eat burnt food...And Don't eat too much Grilled food...Happy Eating ;-)

I don’t know whether reheated food may fall within the category of overcooked food, but some reheated products may cause stomachaches and even poisoning. These are just a few:

Celery

Celery contains relatively secure nitrate, but in case of a repeated heating of this product, the nitrate turn into toxic nitrite and carcinogenic nitrosamines.

Butter and oil

When you reheat butter or oil it accumulates toxic substances that raise blood cholesterol.

Spinach and beetroot

Spinach and beetroot may also become toxic in case of repeated heating. As well as celery it includes nitrate that turn into toxic nitrite and nitrosamines in the event of repeated heating.

Mushrooms, chickens and eggs

Protein contained in these products changes its structure during heating, which may cause stomachaches, digestion issues and poisoning.
