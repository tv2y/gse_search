Deficit Tracker
---------------

BPC’s economic policy experts analyze the government’s running budget deficit and update the Deficit Tracker monthly.

December 10, 2025

Share:

Each year’s deficit adds to the already enormous national debt, with interest costs driving further spending growth. The annual deficit is the product of numerous cash flows into and out of the Treasury that reflect past choices by lawmakers. Deviations in a single month might just be one-offs (like a payment for a large grant or settlement) or part of a predictable yearly pattern (for example, a large influx of tax revenue in April). But over the course of each fiscal year, other trends can emerge that reveal the state of the nation’s fiscal health: the relative strength of tax collections to the economy, the level of disaster response spending, or the effects of new legislation being implemented.

BPC’s economic policy experts analyze the government’s running budget deficit and update the Deficit Tracker monthly.

**Tracking the Federal Deficit: November 2025**
-----------------------------------------------

The federal government’s cumulative deficit for fiscal year 2026 was $439 billion at the end of November—19% lower than the same time last year after adjusting for timing effects. Revenues increased by 18%, and outlays increased by 1% from fiscal year 2025.

Strong revenue growth so far this year has been driven both by higher tariff rates than were in effect during the same period last fiscal year and robust individual income tax collections on higher wages. Spending patterns so far have been driven by reduced disaster spending (partly due to the October-November shutdown) and lower spending levels at the Environmental Protection Agency and Department of Education. This was offset by mandatory spending, which continued to grow, as did interest costs on the national debt.

The government shutdown ended in mid-November, but Congress must address appropriations for many programs funded under a continuing resolution through January 2026. Congress is also weighing options for health subsidies set to expire at the end of 2025.

**Monthly Deficit for November 2025**

After adjusting for timing effects, the November 2025 deficit was $261 billion—$24 billion (-9%) lower than last November.

November spending was $10 billion (2%) higher than last year, including:

*   A $22 billion (28%) increase in Medicare spending due to a onetime $16 billion payment to Part D prescription drug plans to compensate for higher-than-expected 2024 spending by those plans
*   A $9 billion (11%) increase in outlays for net interest on the public debt
*   A $9 billion (7%) increase in Social Security outlays

Meanwhile, spending by the Environmental Protection Agency decreased by $16 billion (93%) compared to last November. This is because in November 2024, the agency released $17 billion in clean energy grants tied to the 2022 Inflation Reduction Act.

Receipts were $35 billion (11%) higher compared to last November. Major increases included:

*   A $24 billion (292%) increase in customs duties due to increased tariffs on most imported goods
*   A $15 billion (6%) increase in individual income and payroll taxes

Meanwhile, corporate income taxes fell by $2 billion (-24%) relative to last November. Corporate tax collections are typically low in November, so relatively small absolute changes can create large percentage shifts.

Tracking the Federal Deficit: October 2025
------------------------------------------

October 1 marked the start of fiscal year 2026. The Congressional Budget Office projected a $1.7 trillion deficit (5.5% of GDP) for the fiscal year, slightly lower than FY2025 levels. The fiscal year began with the longest government shutdown in history. The shutdown ended on November 12, 2025, when Congress passed a continuing resolution funding most of the government through January 30, 2026 and providing full-year appropriations for certain agencies.

*   The government ran a deficit of $219 billion in October 2025. However, after adjusting for timing shifts that pushed some November payments into October, the deficit was $112 billion. This was $145 billion (-56%) lower than in October 2024.
*   Revenues increased by $75 billion, driven by higher individual income and payroll tax collections and customs duties from tariffs.
*   After accounting for timing shifts, outlays decreased by $70 billion compared to October 2024, largely due to the government shutdown.

**Fiscal Year to Date Trends**

Outlays for the first two months of FY2026 were $9 billion (1%) higher than the same period in FY2025 after accounting for timing shifts. Notable trends include:

*   Outlays for Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid payments increased by $46 billion (9%). In addition to the Medicare Part D payment, increases reflect higher Social Security benefit payments from annual cost-of-living adjustments and increased benefits for some beneficiaries following the repeal of Social Security’s Windfall Elimination Provision and Government Pension Offset. A 2.8% cost-of-living adjustment will take effect in January 2026.
*   Interest payments on debt held by the public increased by $22 billion (14%), making interest the second-largest federal expense as the national debt continues to grow and long-term interest rates remain elevated.
*   Environmental Protection Agency spending decreased by $15 billion (-85%) because in November 2024, the agency released $17 billion in clean energy grants funded by the 2022 Inflation Reduction Act. No similar outlay happened this year.
*   Spending by the Department of Homeland Security was $7 billion (-38%) lower due to lower Federal Emergency Management Agency disaster relief spending for the 2025 hurricane season compared to last year.
*   Outlays for the Department of Agriculture decreased by $11 billion (-23%), largely because the government shutdown during October and November 2025 disrupted spending for disaster relief and farm loan programs and spending by the Commodity Credit Corporation.
*   Outlays for the Department of Education decreased by $7 billion (-21%) due to lower spending from the Education Stabilization Fund.

Receipts in the first two months of FY2026 are $112 billion (18%) higher than at this point last fiscal year. This includes:

*   A $69 billion (12%) increase in individual and payroll tax collections
*   A $48 billion (287%) increase in customs duties because of increased tariffs in effect for most imported goods
*   A $5 billion (-27%) decrease in excise taxes

**Analysis**

*   The federal government shutdown ended on November 12, 2025, when Congress passed and President Trump signed a short-term continuing resolution funding much of the government through January 30, 2026, and providing full-year appropriations for agriculture, veterans affairs, military construction, and legislative branch programs. Workers who were furloughed or worked without pay during the shutdown were provided backpay for missed paychecks. The shutdown lasted 43 days and was the longest in U.S. history.

In November, CBO released an updated analysis on the budgetary effects of tariffs. CBO estimates that tariff revenues could reduce primary deficits by $2.5 trillion and federal interest outlays by $500 billion over 11 years, implying $3.0 trillion in total deficit reduction over that period if current policies remain in place. This estimate is lower than CBO’s previous projection, reflecting both new data and reductions to tariff rates from rates that went into effect in August 2025. The Supreme Court is currently weighing legal challenges to certain tariff policies, and its decision could greatly influence future tariff collections.

Tracking Data from Previous Years
---------------------------------

**Fiscal Year 2025 in Review**

The federal government ran a deficit of $1.8 trillion in fiscal year 2025, $41 billion (-2%) less than in fiscal year 2024.

Revenues increased by $317 billion (6%). Increases in collections of individual income taxes and customs duties due to higher tariffs on imported goods were partially offset by a decline in corporate tax receipts due to changes enacted in H.R. 1.

Outlays grew by $275 billion (4%), driven by the largest benefit programs and net interest on the public debt. These increases were partly offset by decreases recorded for federal student loan programs due to modifications authorized in H.R. 1, FDIC asset liquidations, and the Small Business Administration, which recorded a one-time loss from pandemic loans in FY2024.

**_Receipts:_**

Receipts totaled $5.2 trillion in FY2025, a $317 billion (6%) increase year-over-year. This increase was driven by:

*   Individual income tax collections increased by $230 billion (9%).
*   Customs duties increased by $118 billion (153%) due to increased tariffs on most imported goods beginning in February 2025.
*   Corporate income tax receipts decreased by $78 billion (-15%) because H.R. 1 allowed corporations to take larger deductions for certain investments in their 2025 estimated tax filings. Also, delayed 2023 returns from businesses impacted by natural disasters shifted some FY2023 payments into the first quarter of FY2024, making a one-to-one comparison between FY2024 and FY2025 receipts more challenging.
*   Payroll taxes rose by $39 billion (2%), a lower rate than individual income taxes, due to some reclassifications by the Treasury Department in its estimations of payroll and individual income tax receipts, respectively.

**_Outlays_****:**

Outlays in FY2025 totaled $7.0 trillion, a $275 billion (4%) increase from FY2024. Significant changes in outlays included:

*   Outlays for Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid increased by $249 billion (8%) in FY2025. This growth reflects a growing number of retirees, increased benefit payments from annual cost-of-living adjustments, rising healthcare costs, and retroactive payments to some beneficiaries following the December 2024 repeal of Social Security’s Windfall Elimination Provision and Government Pension Offset.
*   Outlays for the Department of Education decreased by $233 billion (-87%) due to modifications to the student loan program authorized in H.R. 1, reductions in outlays for the Education Stabilization Fund, and the stoppage of pandemic relief funds for elementary and secondary education.
*   Interest payments on debt held by the public increased by $79 billion (8%), hitting $1 trillion for the first time, and remained the second-largest federal expense, behind only Social Security, as the national debt continues to grow.
*   Outlays for the Department of Veterans Affairs increased by $40 billion (12%) as more veterans accessed benefits.
*   Outlays for the Small Business Administration decreased by $32 billion. This was because the agency recorded an unusual, one-time increase of $33 billion in outlays from losses stemming from pandemic loans in FY2024.
*   Other trends in FY2025 included elevated disaster relief spending, significant EPA outlays for clean technology grants in November and December 2024, increased outlays for refundable tax credits, and FDIC asset liquidations that offset some outlays.

_All data included above are from the Congressional Budget Office and the Treasury Department._

Tracking the Federal Deficit: September 2025
--------------------------------------------

The federal government’s deficit in fiscal year 2025 was $1.8 trillion—4% lower than last year’s after adjusting for timing effects. Revenues increased by 6%, and outlays increased by 3% from fiscal year 2024.

The government ran a monthly surplus of $164 billion in September 2025, 105% higher than in September 2024. Contributing to this was a $124 billion decrease in spending by the Department of Education, due to changes in the federal student loan program authorized in H.R. 1 in July 2025. Customs duties this month rose by $22 billion (310%) compared to last September, as increased tariffs on most imported goods continued to yield higher revenues.

Looking ahead, the government is likely to begin FY2026 by running a deficit in October given the lack of major tax deadlines in this month. Implementation impacts of H.R. 1 and the ongoing federal government shutdown may also impact revenue and spending patterns.

The September 2025 surplus was $164 billion —105% higher than in September 2024.

Monthly spending was $67 billion lower (-13%) compared to last September when accounting for timing shifts. This was driven by the Department of Education’s $124 billion decrease in spending this month, largely due to H.R. 1’s changes to the federal student loan program. This was partly offset by increases in outlays in other departments, such as:

*   A $11 billion (110%) increase in outlays for international assistance, largely due to upward revisions in receipts for the Foreign Military Sales Program from September 2024
*   A $10 billion (8%) increase in Social Security outlays
*   A $8 billion (11%) increase in Medicare spending
*   A $8 billion (10%) increase in outlays for net interest on the public debt
*   A $6 billion (53%) increase in spending by the Department of Agriculture

Receipts were $8 billion (2%) higher compared to last September. Major increases included:

*   A $22 billion (310%)increase in customs duties due to increased tariffs on most imported goods
*   A $31 billion (25%) increase in individual income and payroll taxes

Meanwhile, corporate income taxes fell by $45 billion (-42%) relative to last September due to changes enacted in H.R. 1 that allowed corporations to take larger deductions for certain investments in 2025, in turn reducing some estimated quarterly payments.

### Fiscal Year Trends

Government spending in FY2025 was $228 billion (3%) higher than FY2024 spending after accounting for timing shifts. This includes:

*   Outlays for major mandatory programs, i.e., Social Security,Medicare, and Medicaid, increased by $245 billion (8%) in FY2025. This growth reflects a growing number of retirees, increased benefit payments from annual cost-of-living adjustments,rising healthcare costs, and retroactive payments to some beneficiaries following the December 2024 repeal of Social Security’s Windfall Elimination Provision and Government Pension Offset.
*   Interest payments on debt held by the public increased by $80 billion (8%), remaining the second-largest federal expense behind only Social Security, as the national debt continues to grow.
*   Outlays for the Department of Veterans Affairs increased by $41 billion (12%) as more veterans accessed benefits.
*   Outlays for the Department of Agriculture increased by $28 billion (14%), largely due to relief payments provided in December 2024 for crop losses in 2023 and 2024.
*   Outlays for the Department of Education decreased by $234 billion (-87%) due to modifications to the student loan program authorized in H.R. 1, reductions in outlays for the Education Stabilization Fund, and the stoppage of pandemic relief funds for elementary and secondary education.
*   Outlays for the Small Business Administration decreased by $32 billion. This was because the agency recorded an unusual, one-time increase of $33 billion in outlays from losses stemming from pandemic loans in FY2024.
*   Previously noted trends this fiscal year include elevated disaster relief spending, EPA grants for clean technologies in November and December 2024, increased outlays for refundable tax credits, and FDIC asset liquidations that offset some outlays.

Receipts for FY2025 were $308 billion (6%) higher than in FY2024. This includes:

*   A $260 billion (6%) increase in individual and payroll tax collections
*   A $118 billion (153%)increase in customs duties due to increased tariffs on most imported goods since February 2025
*   A $77 billion (-15%) decrease in corporate tax receipts, partly because OBBB allowed corporations to take larger deductions for certain investments in 2025 in estimated tax filings. Also, delayed 2023 returns from businesses impacted by natural disasters shifted some FY2023 payments into the first quarter of FY2024.
*   A $2 billion (75%) increase in remittances from the Federal Reserve to the Treasury Department

### Analysis

*   The House and Senate failed to reach an agreement to reconcile their respective appropriations bills or pass a continuing resolution to keep the government funded ahead of the end of the fiscal year, thus leading to a shutdown starting on October 1, 2025.
*   BPC analyzed federal agency shutdown contingency plans to evaluate the impact of the administration’s actions to downsize the federal workforce prior to the shutdown. Some notable trends included a 62,800 reduction in civilian employees at the Department of Defense, which was the largest agency workforce reduction by absolute numbers, and a 41% reduction in the workforce of the Department of Education, which was the largest reduction in percentage terms for a cabinet-level agency.
*   In September, ahead of the government shutdown, CBO released an analysis on the potential effects of a shutdown. They estimated that under a lapse in discretionary funding for FY2026, about 750,000 employees could be furloughed each day, with the total daily cost of their missing compensation being roughly $400 million.

Tracking the Federal Deficit: August 2025
-----------------------------------------

The federal government’s cumulative deficit for fiscal year 2025 was $2.0 trillion at the end of August—1% higher than the same time last year after adjusting for timing effects. Revenues increased by 7%, and outlays increased by 5% from fiscal year 2024.

The monthly deficit was $272 billion in August 2025, 9% lower than in August 2024 after accounting for timing shifts for both years. Contributing to this was a $30 billion (76%) decline in spending by the Department of Education, in part due to a reduction in outlays for federal student loans. Meanwhile, outlays for the Department of Commerce rose by $6 billion (580%) due to increased funding for semiconductor research and facilities provided under the CHIPS and Science Act. Customs duties this month rose by $22 billion (317%) compared to last August, as increased tariffs on most imported goods continued to yield higher revenues.

CBO projects that the total FY2025 deficit will settle at $1.8 trillion when the fiscal year ends on September 30. This implies that there will be a monthly surplus in September, stemming from reduced outlays for the federal student loan program enacted in H.R. 1, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBB), and quarterly tax payments.

### Monthly Deficit for August 2025

After adjusting for timing effects, the August 2025 deficit was $272 billion—9% lower than in last August.

Monthly spending was $9 billion higher (2%) compared to last August when accounting for timing shifts. Major increases in outlays included:

*   A $13 billion (16%) increase in outlays for net interest on the public debt.
*   A $10 billion (8%) increase in Social Security outlays.
*   A $7 billion (9%) increase in Medicare spending.
*   A $6 billion (580%) increase in spending by the Department of Commerce due to increased funding for semiconductor research and facilities under the CHIPS and Science Act.

Meanwhile, spending by the Department of Education decreased by $30 billion (76%) in part due to a decrease in outlays for federal student loans.

Receipts were $37 billion (12%) higher compared to last August. Major increases included:

*   A $22 billion (317%)increase in customs duties due to increased tariffs on most imported goods.
*   A $20 billion (7%) increase in individual income and payroll taxes.

Meanwhile, corporate income taxes fell by $6 billion (-71%) relative to last August. Corporate tax collections are typically low in August, meaning that relatively small changes in absolute tax dollars collected can create large percentage shifts.

### Fiscal Year to Date Trends

Outlays for the first 11 months of FY2025 have been $310 billion (5%) higher than the same period in FY2024 after accounting for timing shifts.

*   Social Security,Medicare, and Medicaid payments comprise $223 billion (72%) of the increase in FY2025 outlays compared to the same point in FY2024, reflecting a growing number of retirees, increased benefit payments from annual cost-of-living adjustments, and retroactive payments to some beneficiaries following the repeal of Social Security’s Windfall Elimination Provision and Government Pension Offset.
*   Interest payments on debt held by the public account for another $72 billion (23%) of the fiscal year-to-date increase, remaining the second-largest federal expense behind only Social Security.
*   Outlays for the Department of Veterans Affairs increased by $36 billion (12%) as more people used veterans’ benefits.
*   Outlays for the Department of Education decreased by $110 billion (-44%) due to a reduction in costs of and timing differences in recording new student loans, reductions in outlays for the Education Stabilization Fund, and ending of pandemic relief funds for elementary and secondary education.
*   Previously noted trends this fiscal year include elevated disaster relief spending, EPA grants for clean technologies in November and December 2024, increased outlays for refundable tax credits, and FDIC asset liquidations that offset some outlays.

Receipts in the first 11 months of FY2025 are $299 billion (7%) higher than at this point last fiscal year. This includes:

*   A $229 billion (6%) increase in individual and payroll tax collections.
*   A $32 billion (-8%) decrease in corporate tax receipts, largely because delayed 2023 returns from California businesses impacted by natural disasters shifted some FY 2023 payments into the first quarter of FY2024.
*   A $102 billion (45%) increase in receipts from other revenue sources, including a $95 billion (137%) increase in customs duties due to increased tariffs on imported goods, a $4 billion (10%) increase in miscellaneous fees and fines, and a $2 billion (75%) increase in remittances from the Federal Reserve to the Treasury Department.

### Analysis

*   On August 29, the White House transmitted a special message to Congress requesting the rescission of$4.9 billion in previously-appropriated foreign aid funding. Under the rescissions process outlined in the Impoundment Control Act of 1974, funds proposed for rescission are withheld from obligation by the Office of Management and Budget until Congress affirmatively approves the package in part or in full within 45 days. Given the limited time remaining in the fiscal year—effectively making this a “pocket rescission”—the fate of these funds is unclear regardless of congressional action.
*   The House and Senate have each passed just three of the 12 appropriations bills for FY2026. Both chambers must reconcile and complete their appropriations bills to reach an agreement on government funding or pass a continuing resolution ahead of the October 1, 2025, deadline to avoid a shutdown.
*   In August, CBO released a new analysis on the budgetary effects of tariffs. They estimate that the government will collect about $200 billion in tariffs by the end of the fiscal year and project that tariff revenues could reduce primary deficits by $3.3 trillion over 10 years if they remain in place. This month, the Supreme Court agreed to expedite an appeal of lower court rulings that found many of the tariffs imposed exceeded the executive branch’s authority.

**Tracking the Federal Deficit: June 2025**
-------------------------------------------

The cumulative fiscal year 2025 deficit was$1.3 trillion at the end of June—1% lower than the same time last year after adjusting for timing effects. Revenues increased by 7% and outlays increased by 5% from last fiscal year.

The federal government ran a monthly surplus of $26 billion in June. However, this was because government payments typically due June 1 shifted into May this year as June 1 fell on a weekend. Accounting for this timing shift, the government ran a monthly deficit of $71 billion.

June’s deficit was improved from May because of quarterly tax deposits from businesses and U.S. citizens living abroad. The June 2025 deficit was 57% lower than in June 2024. This is because, in June 2024, the U.S. Department of Education recorded a one-time increase of $74 billion in the estimated cost of the student loan portfolio. Customs duties this month rose by $18 billion (279%) compared to last June, as increased tariffs on most imported goods continued to yield higher revenues.

Looking ahead, barring any major changes in economic conditions, the government will likely run a larger deficit in July, as there are no quarterly tax deposits due that month.

Tracking the Federal Deficit: May 2025
--------------------------------------

The cumulative fiscal year 2025 deficit was$1.4 trillion at the end of May 2025—7% higher than the same time last year after adjusting for timing effects. Both revenues and outlays increased 6% from last May.

After running a surplus in April, as is standard during tax season, the government returned to running a monthly deficit in May. This month’s deficit was $314 billion—15% lower than last May after adjusting for timing effects. Notable spending increases from last May come from programs that provide health care and income support to older adults, reflective of an aging population and rising health costs. Customs duties rose by $15 billion (286%) compared to last May as increased tariffs on most imported goods continued to bring in higher revenues.

Looking ahead, the monthly deficit is likely to decrease in June as businesses and U.S. citizens living abroad make their quarterly tax deposits. BPC continues to monitor cashflows as the Treasury Department draws down accumulated receipts and uses accounting measures to meet spending needs. BPC plans to issue an update to its projected X Date range in the coming weeks.

The overall moderation in inflation trends led the Fed to lower its benchmark federal funds rate further by 0.25 percentage points (to 4.25%-4.5%) at its December meeting as it balances its dual mandate of maximum employment and price stability. These rate cuts may eventually put downward pressure on net interest payments for the national debt. However, the 10-year treasury yields have increased recently as Fed chair Jerome Powell indicated a more cautious approach to rate cuts in 2025 amid concerns about lingering inflation.

Late in the month, Congress passed and the president signed a continuing resolution to keep the government funded until mid-March 2025. The measure did not address the statutory debt limit, which was reinstated on January 2, 2025, and set at $36.1 trillion. In the coming weeks and months, the Treasury Department will use its cash reserves and “extraordinary measures”to continue federal government operations without breaching the limit. BPC is closely monitoring daily cash flows to project when Treasury will find itself at high risk of exhausting existing cash and extraordinary measures and defaulting on its obligations—what BPC has coined the X Date.

Tracking the Federal Deficit: November 2024
-------------------------------------------

The federal budget deficit in November 2024 was higher than in November 2023. The cumulative deficit so far in FY 2025 was significantly higher than in the corresponding period in FY 2024 due to a decrease in revenues and an increase in outlays.

$365 billion deficit,_increasing_ year-over-year (YOY) by $51 billion.

*   $301 billion in revenues,_increased_ YOY by $26 billion (10%)
*   $666 billion in outlays,_increased_ YOY by $77 billion (13%)
*   Because December 1, 2024, fell on a Sunday, some federal payments due were shifted forward into November. Without that shift, the monthly deficit would have decreased $31 billion YOY.

### **Fiscal Year Comparisons with FY2024**

*   **The government is running a cumulative deficit of $622 billion so far in FY2025**($88 billion more than the same period in the prior fiscal year when adjusted for timing shifts).
*   **Revenues were $628 billion through November, a decrease of 7%, largely due to:**
    *   $19 billion (3%) decrease in individual income and payroll tax revenue. Of this amount, non-withheld payments of income and payroll taxes decreased by $47 billion (44%), mainly because the postponement of tax deadlines for 2023 shifted payments into the beginning of fiscal year 2024.
    *   $35 billion (63%) decrease in corporate income taxes, also largely because of the postponement of 2023 tax deadlines into FY2024.
    *   $6 billion (14%) decrease in individual income tax refunds, which increased net receipts.

*   **Outlays were $1.25 trillion through November, an increase of 18%, largely due to:***
    *   $11 billion (7%) increase in net interest payments on the public debt due to high interest rates and a growing debt burden.
    *   $32 billion (7%) increase in the largest mandatory spending programs, such as Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid, reflecting growing numbers of recipients and benefit levels for both Social Security and Medicare Advantage.
    *   $66 billion (106%) billion decrease in net outlays of the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) as it continued its liquidation of assets of the failed banks whose uninsured depositors it covered in fiscal year 2023. Those proceeds are recorded in the budget as offsetting collections (i.e., reductions in outlays).
    *   $17 billion increase in spending by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) due to spending on a clean energy grant program for low-income communities established by the Inflation Reduction Act in 2022.
    *   $8 billion (15%) increase in spending by the Department of Veterans Affairs, because more people used veterans’ benefits.
    *   $7 billion (46%) increase in certain refundable tax credits.

### Analysis

Net interest payments on the national debt have soared in recent years as the Federal Reserve rapidly raised interest rates to combat inflation. Recent economic data indicated that inflation, as measured by the consumer price index, rose by 0.3% from October to November and increased 2.7% year over year. Employment rose by 227,000 in November with the rate unchanged at 4.2%. The overall moderation in inflation trends led the Fed to lower its benchmark federal funds rate by 0.25 percentage points to 4.5%-4.75% at its November meeting as it balances its dual mandate of maximum employment and price stability. These rate cuts may eventually put slight downward pressure on net interest payments on the national debt.

On December 12, the Congressional Budget Office released a compendium of 76 policy options to reduce federal budget deficits over the next decade and their estimated effects on the federal budget. This periodic report provides lawmakers, budget analysts, and the public the estimated savings, potential tradeoffs, and design considerations of possible changes to both tax and spending policy, though it does not include recommendations. BPC also released a series of options for a fiscally responsible approach to the upcoming 2025 tax negotiations.

Congressional leaders are negotiating a continuing resolution to keep the government funded beyond the current December 20, 2024, deadline to avoid a government shutdown.

Tracking the Federal Deficit: October 2024
------------------------------------------

October 1 marked the start of fiscal year 2025. The Congressional Budget Office projected a $1.9 trillion deficit (6.5% of GDP) for the fiscal year, similar to FY2024 levels. These levels of borrowing are abnormal for a period of high employment and peacetime, and unsustainable over the long term. Congress faces significant budget decisions for the fiscal year which will impact the final deficit. Decision points include annual appropriations, relief for communities impacted by natural disasters, and the reinstatement of the debt limit in January 2025.

*   The federal government incurred a deficit of $255 billion in October 2024,increasing year over year (YOY) by $189 billion (392%) from October 2023.
*   Adjusting for outlay timing shifts and deferred tax payments that impacted October 2023, the YOY deficit increase for October 2024 would have been slightly lower: $116 billion.

Methodological Note:
--------------------

The monthly tracker entries report preliminary spending, revenue, and deficit data from the Congressional Budget Office’s (CBO)Monthly Budget Reviews that are published throughout each fiscal year (October to September). These summaries are released around the fifth business day of each month and preview the release of official budget data from the Treasury Department, which is released around the eighth business day of each month (except end-of-year data, which tends to be released later each October). Historically, CBO’s preliminary data is accurate, often differing from Treasury’s final figures by only a few billion dollars, if at all. For example, CBO preliminarily reported that the total FY2019 deficit was $984 billion in their September 2019 review, matching the official figure Treasury reported later.

The deficit tracker graphic is updated retroactively with official Treasury data; the monthly text entries are not.

*Includes adjustments for timing shifts, which move certain payments from one month to another when the beginning or end of the month falls on a weekend or federal holiday. This can cause a monthly deficit to be artificially increased or decreased, which is particularly important to note when comparing the figure to prior-year deficits. CBO recognizes these timing shifts in the topline year-over-year comparisons and makes adjustments to offset the effects of the timing shifts from the resulting discussions and analyses.
