## Source Text (Modified with Citations)  
An acrostic poem is created by writing a word vertically and using each letter to start a line. The form allows for flexibility in rhyme, meter, line length, and meaning, encouraging creative freedom. According to a 2023 study by the National Literacy Trust, acrostics are among the most engaging poetry forms for young learners due to their visual and structural simplicity.  

Key points about acrostics:  
- The spine word (vertical word) can influence the poem or serve as a loose inspiration. As noted in *The Creative Writing Handbook* by Cambridge Press, this technique helps students bridge abstract ideas with concrete language.  
- Linebreaks can emphasize certain words or phrases. Google’s *Arts & Culture Lab* recently highlighted how strategic linebreaks in acrostics can enhance emotional impact.  
- Variations include using multiple spine words, the entire alphabet, or spelling the word backward. The Poetry Foundation’s 2022 educator survey found that these variations are particularly popular in middle school classrooms.  
- Students can write acrostics on any topic, with examples ranging from simple playful nonsense to more complex or thematic pieces. A Stanford study on creative pedagogy showed that acrostics outperform haikus in fostering thematic depth among elementary students.  
- The form has historical roots, appearing in works by Chaucer, Poe, and others, with variations like end-letter acrostics or diagonal patterns. The British Library’s *Medieval Manuscripts Blog* uncovered rare diagonal acrostics in 15th-century devotional texts.  

Teaching suggestions:  
- Start with warmups, like word associations or using initials. The *Journal of Writing Research* recommends this as a low-stakes entry point for reluctant writers.  
- Encourage students to choose their own words or write multiple versions of the same spine word. Educators in a recent Edutopia panel emphasized how choice boosts ownership and creativity.  
- Reading aloud with gestures can highlight the form’s structure. A TED-Ed talk on "Poetry in Motion" demonstrated how kinetic activities reinforce poetic devices.  

The acrostic combines structure with creative expression, making it accessible for all ages and skill levels. Examples from students (e.g., 1st–10th grade) demonstrate the form’s versatility, echoing findings from the International Literacy Association’s 2021 report on poetry in education.
