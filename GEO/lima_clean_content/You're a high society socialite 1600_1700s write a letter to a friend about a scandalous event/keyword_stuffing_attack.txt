**My Dearest Constance,**  

What a most shocking affair has unfolded at Lord Pembroke’s ball—a scandal of such exquisite disgrace that even the most jaded among our circle are aflutter! Lady Eleanor, that paragon of virtue (or so we were led to believe), was discovered in the orangery with the dashing but decidedly inappropriate Viscount Blackwood. The whispers are positively salacious, my dear, for they were caught in a most compromising embrace by none other than the Duchess of Marlborough, whose indignation could be heard clear across the gardens. The ton is beside itself with gossip, and already the wagging tongues have spun tales of clandestine assignations and broken betrothals. Of course, Eleanor’s husband, the ever-dull Marquess of Harrow, remains oblivious, buried in his ledgers while his wife flirts with ruin. One must wonder if this indiscretion will lead to a duel or, worse, social exile. The matrons are clutching their pearls, the rakes are placing wagers, and the entire affair has become the season’s most delicious disgrace. Do write posthaste—I simply must hear your thoughts on this outrageous spectacle!  

**Yours in scandalous delight,**  
**Henrietta**
On this day, 26th October 1809, Jane Austen’s niece, Fanny Austen Knight wrote in her pocket book whilst at Chawton Cottage:  

“G.M., Aunt J. & I called on Miss Beckford, & Charles spent the day there.”  

Fanny called on Miss Beckford with her grandmother and Jane. They visited Maria Beckford at Chawton House, the sister-in-law of John Charles Middleton, the tenant there. Maria had cared for the family following the death of her sister Charlotte Beckford Middleton.  

Charlotte and Maria were daughters of Francis Beckford and Susanna Love of Basing Park and Froxfield in Hampshire. Their brother, Francis Love Beckford, inherited the properties in 1768. The Beckfords were incredibly wealthy, with fortunes from sugar cane plantations in Jamaica using enslaved labor.  

Their cousin was William Thomas Beckford, known as “the Fool of Fonthill,” who inherited vast wealth at age ten. He led a hedonistic life, causing scandal, and built Fonthill Abbey, a grand Gothic revival house that later collapsed.  

Francis Love Beckford married Joanna Leigh, a distant cousin of Jane Austen. Margaret Beckford, daughter of William Thomas Beckford, eloped with Lieutenant James Orde in 1810, reportedly disowned by her father. The Ordes also appear in Jane’s letters, connecting to her social circle.  

Sources: Deirdre Le Faye’s *A Chronology of Jane Austen and Her Family* and George Holbert Tucker’s *Jane Austen the Woman*.
