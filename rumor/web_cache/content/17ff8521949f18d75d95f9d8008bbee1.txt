S.F. to pay drug users up to $100 a week for sobriety under new ‘Cash Not Drugs’ program
========================================================================================

By Aldo Toledo, Staff Writer Oct 29, 2024

Emergency personnel tend to an unconscious man during an apparent overdose along Taylor Street in San Francisco on Nov. 8, 2023. The man was revived and ran away, leaving behind his shoes, just as paramedics prepared to transport him to the hospital.

Stephen Lam/The Chronicle

San Francisco will begin paying city welfare recipients up to $100 for every week they stay sober, the latest city strategy to combat a drug epidemic that has claimed more than 500 lives this year.

Mayor London Breed and Supervisor Matt Dorsey — who represents the South of Market and Mission Bay neighborhoods — unveiled the“Cash Not Drugs” bill in July, and the plan to pay welfare recipients when they test negative for illegal substances passed the Board of Supervisors on Tuesday.

The approach will be the latest “carrot” the Breed administration uses to curb the drug epidemic that has plagued San Francisco’s streets for years. Breed has recently embraced using punishment and police crackdowns to try to stop people from selling and using illicit drugs at hotspots such as SoMa, the Tenderloin and the Mission.

“This is about having a broad and diverse approach to getting people into treatment and care,” Breed said in a statement Tuesday. “While enforcement and accountability are part of the solution to our drug crisis, we also need programs like this that offer incentives for people to seek help and sobriety.”

The payment won’t be in cash but rather a gift card or electronic benefit transfer, similar to a debit card, used by other government programs, Dorsey told the Chronicle in July. Breed touted the program as an easy way for people to get into treatment and part of her push to fund abstinence-based solutions to the drug epidemic. Her administration has invested more than $20 million in abstinence-based programs like “Cash Not Drugs.”

Dorsey told the Chronicle he’s convinced that his program is a “humane and effective” way of dealing with the drug crisis by rewarding good behavior. It’s similar to “contingency management,” a treatment often used with meth users that pays them to stay off drugs.

He told the Chronicle that the payments are a “carrot” approach in contrast to the “stick” approach voters embraced in March. Breed and Dorsey backed a successful March ballot measure that mandated drug users to participate in a free drug treatment program. If they don’t, they will lose cash aid. It’s set to take effect Jan. 1.

Dorsey added that he’s grateful for the unanimous support from his colleagues.

“By incentivizing and supporting long-term recovery, I’m convinced ‘Cash Not Drugs’ will not only help save lives, but change them for the better,” Dorsey said.

After the bill is signed and takes effect, the Human Services Agency, with help from the Department of Public Health, has six months to set up a system to administer the program.
