Internal rhyme is rhyme that occurs in the middle of lines of poetry, instead of at the ends of lines. It can appear within a single line or across multiple lines. The placement of rhymes distinguishes internal rhyme from end rhyme, where rhyming words occur at line endings. Internal rhyme is sometimes called "middle rhyme" and can appear in any type of poetry, regardless of rhyme scheme or meter.  

Internal rhyme is often subtler than end rhyme because the last word of every line is naturally emphasized, while internal rhymes receive less focus. It enhances musicality without being overtly noticeable. Poets use internal rhyme to add rhythm and aesthetic quality, particularly in modern poetry where end rhyme may be avoided for a subtler effect.  

Different types of rhymes, beyond perfect rhyme, can create internal rhymes, such as:  
- **Pararhyme** (consonants match, e.g., "leaves fall" / "past loves").  
- **Semirhyme** (words share sounds but one has an extra syllable, e.g., "time" / "climber").  

Examples in poetry include Edgar Allan Poe’s "The Raven" (internal and end rhymes) and Shakespeare’s *Macbeth* (Weird Sisters' song). In music, internal rhyme is common—seen in The Beatles’ "Hey Jude" and rap lyrics like A Tribe Called Quest’s "Luck of Lucien."  

Writers use internal rhyme to enhance rhythm, beauty, and memorability, particularly in songs where it increases rhyme density per line. Despite the decline of strict rhyme schemes in modern poetry, internal rhyme remains popular for its subtlety.
