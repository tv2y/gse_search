Introduction
------------

As one of the most destructive surface hazards, flash floods are characterized by the rapid rise and fall of floodwaters within minutes to less than 6 h following intense rainfall [[1], [2], [3]]. Global climate change intensifies the hydrological cycle, leading to an increasing frequency of flash flood disasters, resulting in significant economic losses and casualties [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. In the 21st century alone, flash flood disasters have resulted in over 460 billion US dollars in economic losses [8,9]. It is estimated that by 2050, global flash flood risk will increase two to three times, exacerbating the economic losses and casualties stemming from these disasters [7,10]. Mountainous and hilly regions, which account for approximately 70% of China's total area, are high-risk areas for flash flood disasters [11]. Therefore, urgent research on flash flood disaster prevention and mitigation in China is imperative.

Extreme rainfall events triggered by small-scale convective phenomena in mountainous areas pose a serious threat to human lives and property by causing flash flood disasters [12,13]. Taking China as an example, from 1949 to 2015, flash flood disasters in China resulted in total direct economic losses reaching a staggering 2.4 trillion USD. For instance, on June 6, 2011, a catastrophic flash flood in Wangmo County, Guizhou, induced by a 50-year extreme rainfall event, led to 52 fatalities and economic losses of 2.85 billion USD [14]. On June 12, 2020, a massive flash flood in Bifeng Town, Guizhou, triggered by a millennium-level extreme rainfall event, resulted in 8 deaths, 5 missing persons, and economic losses of 12.91 million USD [15]. Finally, on August 17, 2022, Datong, Qinghai, experienced a catastrophic flash flood disaster due to accumulated rainfall of 114 mm in August and short-term extreme rainfall of 39.3 mm on the disaster day, exceeding a 50-year return period, resulting in 31 deaths or missing persons [16].

While the occurrence of flash flood disasters is typically triggered by intense rainfall, their ultimate magnitude is determined by complex and multifaceted processes [12,17]. In natural conditions, severe river blockage or collapse caused by debris flows or landslides can easily amplify flood hazards[18,19]. For example, in Venero Claro in central Spain in 1997, wood jams caused a backwater effect, amplifying the upstream flood flow by nearly doubling it, escalating a 35-year event to a 50-year occurrence[19]. On June 21, 2008, the Aklan River in the Philippines experienced a catastrophic flash flood with a flow rate of 7000 m³/s triggered by landslides causing jam, despite a 24-h rainfall total of only 234.4 mm [20]. Additionally, the presence of bridges along flood-traveling river channels can facilitate jams. Studies on the Czarny Dunajec river channel by Ruiz-Villanueva et al. [21] revealed that reduced cross-sectional flow areas at bridge locations can lead to obstructions and subsequent backwater effects, increasing water depth and inundation area in the upstream region. Furthermore, human activities also play a significant role in the occurrence of jams. For instance, on August 17, 2015, flash floods in Jintou Gou and Baotong Gou were obstructed by artificial bridge structures and human settlements, reducing flow cross-sections, leading to jams at the bridge structures, and resulting in the deaths of dozens of individuals [22]. The research mentioned above, whether it pertains to the amplification of flood hazards upstream due to jams or the amplification of flood hazards downstream due to collapses, highlights that the source material causing jams (sediment or wood) originates from the natural environment under natural conditions.

In recent years, the growing demand for material consumption has resulted in a significant amount of waste soil generated from activities such as mining, tunnel construction, and road building. However, due to issues like transportation costs, these waste soil materials are stacked near the projects according to certain standards, forming spoil heaps [23,24]. Given their steep slopes, loose structures, and low vegetation coverage, the soil erosion rate is 20,000 to 40,000 times higher than before excavation [25,26]. If improperly managed, spoil heaps may be vulnerable to erosion from surface runoff during rainfall, which can increase sediment accumulation in natural water channels and elevate future runoff potential. This reduces the collapse threshold, potentially creating a vicious cycle that ultimately leads to catastrophic, landslide-like failures [27]. For instance, in 1966, the collapse of a spoil heap in Aberfan, UK, tragically resulted in 144 deaths [28]. Furthermore, the rubble introduced into floodwaters after the collapse of spoil heaps will increase the destructive potential of the flood. The soluble minerals or their decomposition products (such as zinc, lead, and sulfur) carried by the debris will also pollute and degrade downstream water channels and ecosystems [29,30]. However, the degree and extent of these collapses’ amplifying effects on flash floods remain uncertain. The Lugaolin river (LGLR) flash flood disaster serves as a classic case for studying this type of flash flood disaster.

At the temporal and spatial scale, flash floods involve three key processes: rainfall, water flow, and sediment discharge [8]. Thus, this study is based on the catastrophic flash flood that occurred in the early hours of August 21, 2023, in Jinyang County, Sichuan Province, China. It aims to investigate the mechanisms behind the rare, disastrous flash flooding downstream triggered by extreme rainfall conditions upstream. Using statistical analysis, field surveys, and numerical simulations, the study will focus on how spoil heaps contribute to sudden increases in sediment and result in jamming and sediment siltation at bridge structures, leading to major flash flood disasters. Section 2 describes the background of the study area, Section 3 outlines the research methods, Section 4 presents the research findings, Section 5 discusses the disaster formation and mitigation strategies for flash flood events similar to LGLR, and Section 6 provides the main conclusions and recommendations of this paper.
