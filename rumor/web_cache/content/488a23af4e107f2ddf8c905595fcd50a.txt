Oct 2024

Mark Esper, who served as secretary of Defense under Donald Trump, backed former colleague John Kelly’s recent remarks that he believes Trump meets the definition of a fascist.

Esper told CNN Wednesday that he has no reason to doubt Kelly’s “honesty or integrity” in relaying Trump’s previous comments. He encouraged the audience to look up the definition of fascism, as Kelly did, and ask whether Trump falls “into those categories.”

“It’s hard to say that he doesn’t, when you kind of look at those terms. But, he certainly has those inclinations,” Esper said. “And I think it’s something we should be wary about.”

Kelly, who served as Trump’s chief of staff and secretary of Homeland Security, said in interviews with The New York Times and The Atlantic published Tuesday that Trump is “certainly an authoritarian” and that the former president once told Kelly he wished he behaved more like the “German generals,” and specifically “Hitler’s generals.” Trump’s recent statements about using the military against his political opponents compelled Kelly to speak out, he told the Times.

The Trump campaign said Kelly’s statements were “debunked” and “fabricated” in a statement to POLITICO Tuesday.

While Esper told CNN he had not experienced the type of incidents Kelly and others described, he recalled Trump being “very troubled by the fact that we would have America’s wounded warriors out in public, and he did not like that.”

“[Trump] just thought we should conceal them. And our view — my view, the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff — was that these were America’s heroes and it was important to show the American people and their colleagues in the service that when our heroes come home wounded, that we take care of them,” Esper said.

When asked whether checks and balances exist to prevent Trump from using the military to go after his political rivals, the former Defense secretary emphasized his trust in the military.

“I fully believe that the uniform officers will live up to that oath to obey the Constitution and will not swear a loyalty oath to the president of the United States. And so my concern, of course, is to the institution,” Esper said.

Oct 2024

**Kamala Harris painted a dark picture of life under a second Donald Trump term at a CNN town hall Wednesday.**

The vice president reminded the audience of undecided voters that Trump has said he wants to terminate the Constitution, has expressed admiration for dictators, and — according to his longest-serving chief of staff, John Kelly — has a positive opinion about Hitler’s generals. Trump and his spokespeople have denied Kelly’s claims.

The town hall underscored that Harris’ closing pitch to voters is that Trump poses a danger to America.

She even went so far as to say Trump is a fascist.

CNN’s Anderson Cooper asked if, like Kelly, Harris believes Trump is a fascist.

“Yes, I do,” the vice president replied.

Harris argued that Trump would be even more extreme than in his first term because career public servants won’t be there to restrain him. “We must take very seriously those folks who knew him best and who are career people are not going to be there to hold him back,” she said.

**Harris also dodged several questions.**

The town hall, held in the Philadelphia suburbs where Harris must drive up turnout to win the critical battleground state of Pennsylvania, featured a number of undecided voters with often pointed questions for Harris. She didn’t always answer them directly.

Asked by one voter if Trump or Biden was to blame for the high cost of groceries, she expressed empathy for Americans dealing with inflation and laid out her plans to deal with price gouging. She did say that “both parties, Democrats and Republicans, haven’t done enough to deal with the issue of housing.”

She also muddled through a response to a question that’s a feature of many job interviews: What are your weaknesses?

“I am certainly not perfect, so let’s start there,” adding that “perhaps a weakness — some would say that, I actually think it’s a strength — is I really value having a team of very smart people around me” who offer “different perspectives.”

**Harris got personal.**

The vice president discussed some intimate issues, including grieving the death of her mother, making mistakes as a parent, and her faith.

Harris, who is Christian, talked about her pastor by name and said that she prays every day. She also said that faith informs her work as vice president.

“Faith is a verb,” she said. “Your life’s work should be to think about how you can serve in a way that is uplifting other people.”

Kamala Harris has approached the final sprint of her campaign needing to do at least two things that are occasionally in conflict: Raise the stakes of a second Donald Trump term by talking about the past, and also talk to voters about their futures in a way that brings in new constituencies.

On Wednesday, an exogenous event gave the vice president the chance to do the former while new polling showed she was achieving the latter.

Echoing Trump’s former chief of staff John Kelly’s misgivings, a phalanx of former Trump officials sounded alarms about what they described as the former president’s fascistic impulses and fixation with Hilter and his generals.

But perhaps more importantly, Harris showed Wednesday her message about the future may be breaking through.

“On every economic issue surveyed, Kamala Harris has closed the trust gap or even surpassed Trump as the candidate that swing-state voters trust more,” according to a new Bloomberg News/Morning Consult poll of the battleground states.

The editorials editor at the Los Angeles Times resigned after the paper’s billionaire owner blocked the editorial board from endorsing a candidate for president, the Columbia Journalism Review first reported today.

Soon-Shiong defended his decision in an X post after Garza’s resignation became public. He said the editorial board declined an alternative opportunity to draft a “clear and non-partisan side-by-side” comparison of Harris’ and former President Donald Trump’s policies.

One of the most prominent pro-Israel groups is making its return for the general election in an under-the-radar race.

United Democracy Project, the American Israel Public Affairs’ Committee’s super PAC, has placed more than $500,000 on television ads in the Democrat-vs.-Democrat race for California’s 34th District, according to ad tracker AdImpact. The ads are set to start tomorrow and run through Election Day.

Democratic Rep. Jimmy Gomez is fending off a challenge from progressive Democrat David Kim for a third time in the deep-blue, Los Angeles-area district. The showdowns between the two have gotten increasingly competitive: In 2020, Gomez beat Kim by 6 points, and in 2022, that margin shrunk to around 2 points. The two Democrats had advanced to the general election under California’s top-two primary system.

UDP has also dropped five figures on digital ads boosting Gomez and attacking Kim for “switching his party registration multiple times” (a broadside that Kim has pushed back on) and has spent more than $300,000 on mailers, starting earlier this month.

Kim has received the backing of the Muslim American political group Emgage Action, as well as some progressive groups like the Sunrise Movement Los Angeles and IfNotNow Los Angeles, according to his website. Kim’s campaign website describes him as calling for the withholding of all aid to Israel until a cease-fire is reached in its conflict with Hamas, backing a permanent cease-fire, and supporting the Boycott, Divestment and Sanctions movement. He also advocated for the creation of an international criminal court through the U.N. Security Council to “prosecute illegal settlers in the West Bank and deliver justice to Palestinian families.”

UDP spent heavily in primaries throughout the cycle — more than $36 million — targeting candidates on both sides of the aisle it deemed as insufficiently pro-Israel. Prior to that spending, AIPAC vowed to spend $100 million across its political entities in 2024.

The last spending tracked for UDP was in August, when the group was spending against Rep. Cori Bush (D-Mo.), who ultimately lost her primary. The group contributed to a handful of other candidates’ losses in the primaries as well, though it wasn’t successful in every race it spent in.

A spokesperson for UDP declined to comment on the spending and pointed to Kim’s social media posts.

Oct 2024

A political action committee running Elon Musk’s controversial sweepstakes offering million-dollar prizes to registered voters in swing states has received a warning from the Justice Department that the unusual incentive may violate federal law, a person familiar with the message said.

The head of DOJ’s Election Crimes Branch, Robert Heberle, sent a letter Monday to an attorney for America PAC cautioning that the giveaway could violate a federal law against paying anyone to vote or register to vote, said the person, who was granted anonymity to discuss a letter that has not been publicly released.

The warning was first reported by 24sight News.

A Justice Department spokesperson declined to comment. A lawyer for America PAC, Chris Gober, did not respond to messages Wednesday seeking comment.

America PAC said that as of Tuesday it had awarded four prizes of $1 million each to people who signed a petition supporting First Amendment and Second Amendment rights. So far, three winners have been Pennsylvania residents and one from North Carolina. The giveaways are to continue daily through Election Day, the PAC said.

While Musk initially framed the offer as an incentive to residents of seven swing states to register to vote, the PAC is now describing the winners as being paid to be spokespeople for the group.

Musk, founder of Tesla and Space-X and owner of the social media platform X, has endorsed former President Donald Trump’s bid to return to the White House.

Some legal experts have said Musk’s offer violates a federal law that makes it a felony to offer “to pay or [accept] payment either for registration to vote or for voting” in a federal election. Each violation carries a potential penalty of up to five years in prison or a $10,000 fine.

Other lawyers say Musk’s scheme doesn’t run afoul of the law because it doesn’t directly pay people for registering, and is limited to people who have registered by the time they sign the petition.

In addition, the criminal statute applies only to those who “knowingly or willfully” disobey the law. Legal advice Musk may have received blessing the arrangement could be enough to head off any prosecution.

Justice Department investigations of election-related crimes also typically take months or years, so it seems highly unlikely the issue will be resolved before polls close.

A new poll that includes large oversamples of young voters of color shows Donald Trump has massively increased his support among groups that he previously performed poorly with in 2020.

It underscores some of the challenges Kamala Harris has had connecting with younger male voters — a demographic that typically breaks for Democrats, but is showing signs of shifting away from the party this election cycle.

The GenForward survey, which ran from Sept. 26 to Oct. 6 by the University of Chicago and included 2,359 eligible voters 18 to 40 years old, included some fascinating findings:

*   A quarter of young Black men are supporting Trump. (Black men overall backed President Joe Biden nearly nine to one in 2020.)
*   44 percent of young Latino men said they’d back Trump, an improvement over the roughly 38 percent who backed him in 2020. 
*   For her part, Harris is at 58 percent with Black men, 37 percent with Latinos and 57 percent with Asian American and Pacific Islander men.
*   Kamala Harris is doing incredibly well among women of color. A majority of Black (63 percent), Asian American and Pacific Islander (60 percent) and Latina (55 percent) women say they will vote for Harris.
*   White women are nearly evenly split between Harris (44 percent) and Trump (40 percent). 

Harris and Trump have both been doing late-breaking outreach to voters of color, with Harris and Trump both taping Univision town halls.

Harris recently taped interviews with Black media personalities Charlamagne tha God, Roland Martin and Justin Carter to reach majority Black audiences. Last week her campaign unveiled her economic agenda aimed at Black men, and Harris’ interview with Telemundo will air tonight at 7 p.m.

Trump, meanwhile, rolled out a slew of endorsements from a slew of former athletes and entertainers this week including Detroit rapper Trick Trick, boxing legend Tommy “Hitman” Hearns and former NFL standouts Antonio Brown and Le’Veon Bell. He also did a business roundtable with Latino community leaders in Doral, Florida on Tuesday.

House Democrats are turning to unlikely saviors to help their nominees win in difficult districts: third-party candidates.

In Montana, Ohio and Alaska, the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee is signaling to its allies that they should boost Libertarians or independent candidates in three targeted House races. All three seats were won by former President Donald Trump in 2020 and it will be difficult for Democrats to win them without siphoning off Trump’s voters from the GOP congressional candidate.

The DCCC previewed the strategy on messaging websites often referred to as “red boxes” — a public and legal way for them to signal outside groups under campaign finance law. The House GOP campaign arm is also asking its allies to get involved in Alaska, where the top four vote-getters advanced from the all-party primary. If no candidate gets more than 50 percent in the general election, the race proceeds to ranked-choice voting.

In Alaska, a super PAC allied with House Democrats has already answered the DCCC request.Another Democratic-aligned group has waded into Montana.

Here’s a breakdown of the machinations in each district:

**Alaska:**The DCCC posted a red box urging allies to run digital ads touting John Wayne Howe, the Alaska Independence Party candidate. The committee suggested advertisers highlight Howe’s support for the Second Amendment and expanded oil production and his skepticism of man-made climate change. The intended audience: “White men under the age of 60 without a college degree, who are either very likely to be Republican or slightly more likely to be Republican than Democrat.”

Vote Alaska Before Party, a group primarily funded by House Majority PAC — the top super PAC for House Democrats — did just that in a digital ad that urges voters to rank Howe first.

The goal: pull GOP voters away from Republican Nick Begich to keep him hitting 50 percent and win outright in the first round. That helps Democratic Rep. Mary Peltola.

The National Republican Campaign Committee, meanwhile, signaled in October that its allies should help Democrat Eric Hafner, the fourth candidate in the race, by touting his support for Medicare-for-all and defunding ICE to “Liberal Democrats with a college degree under 35.”

Republicans’ intention is to pull Democratic voters away from Peltola so she can’t hit 50 percent and win outright in round one. Hafner is in prison in New York — Democrats tried unsuccessfully to get him off the ballot.

**Ohio:**The DCCC also suggested that its allies boost a third-party candidate to help Democratic Rep. Marcy Kaptur win another term in her Toledo-based seat. Their red box calls for digital and streaming ads to target “Voters who identify as Republican and are open to persuasive information about the difference between Libertarian Tom Pruss” and Republican nominee Derek Merrin.

The website suggests touting Pruss as “a conservative outsider who is focused on draining the corrupt Washington swamp” who will stand “up to the radical left.” Merrin should be cast as “Washington-swamp politician” beholden to special interests.

It’s not clear if Democrats have aired any ads promoting Pruss.

**Montana:**Democrats are targeting Republican Rep. Ryan Zinke in the Western Montana seat that Trump carried by 7 points in 2020. The Democratic nominee is Monica Tranel.

The DCCC’s red box suggests mail, streaming and digital ads to boost Libertarian Dennis Hayes with “Voters under the age of 60 who identify as Republican and are open to persuasive information about the difference” between Hayes and Zinke. It urges allies to cast Hayes as a “conservative outsider” who wants to eliminate “the government overreach of the Bureau of Land Management.”

It also warns: “It is critical that voters in this district, which Donald Trump carried by 7 points in 2020, DO NOT see images of Ryan Zinke and Donald Trump together.”

Voter Protection Project, a group that describes itself as dedicated to stopping “Republicans’ attacks on our right to vote,” reported spending nearly $92,000 in late October on direct mail to support Hayes.

The race is still tight in Michigan and Wisconsin.

With under two weeks to go until Election Day, Kamala Harris is slightly ahead of Donald Trump in Michigan, while the race is tied in Wisconsin, according to a pair of new Quinnipiac University polls released Wednesday.

In Michigan, Harris has 49 percent of support from likely voters while Trump has 46 percent of support, according to the poll. In Wisconsin, the race is tied with each candidate maintaining 48 percent of support from likely voters.

Earlier this month, Trump had a 3-point lead over Harris in Michigan and a 2-point edge in Wisconsin.

The polls come as both campaigns have spent significant time in both states working to win over swing state voters. Last week, Harris held several events in Wisconsin and Michigan, and on Tuesday, her running mate Tim Walz campaigned in the Badger state with Barack Obama.

Trump has events planned in Michigan on Friday and Saturday.

The Quinnipiac University polls were conducted Oct. 17-21, surveying 1,136 likely voters in Michigan and 1,108 likely voters in Wisconsin. Both samples have margins of error of plus or minus 2.9 percentage points.

An indictment rocked New York’s 3rd Congressional District — and the country — not long ago, ultimately costing the House GOP the seat. Republicans are now hoping another legal fight can help them flip the district back.

The Congressional Leadership Fund, House Republicans’ main super PAC, is wading into the 3rd District with an attack ad against Rep. Tom Suozzi. The Democrat won the seat earlier this year in a special election to replace then-Republican Rep. George Santos, who was indicted for a wide range of financial crimes and then kicked out of Congress.

The spot opens with Democratic New York City Mayor Eric Adams’ federal indictment, which dropped last month and alleges that he engaged in a yearslong conspiracy to receive illegal foreign campaign contributions, wire fraud and bribery. (Adams has pleaded not guilty.) The ad then links Adams to Suozzi, calling the mayor “Suozzi’s partner in crime” and hitting them both for raising taxes.

“Some politicians steal the spotlight,” the narrator says. “Others, like Tom Suozzi and Eric Adams, steal straight from your pocket.”

After Adams was indicted, Suozzi said it’s “sad that yet another public official has been indicted,” but did not call for his resignation. Suozzi’s opponent, former Republican state Assemblymember Mike LiPetri, has called for Adams to step down.

CLF’s spending is the first notable outside investment from any of the main House groups in the race for the full term, signaling that Republicans are feeling bullish in the final stretch of the election. President Joe Biden won this seat by 11 points in 2020, and Suozzi prevailed in the special election by around 8 points.

Even before Adams was indicted, Republicans have been using him — and Democratic Gov. Kathy Hochul — as boogeymen in advertising to criticize battleground Democrats on crime and immigration, hoping that their unpopularity would drag them down.

**Candidates:**Sen. Deb Fischer (R) vs. former union leader Dan Osborn (I)

**Ad spending since Labor Day:** $13.88 million from Republicans, $18.99 million in support of Osborn

**Past results:**Fischer beat Democratic candidate Jane Raybould by nearly 20 percentage points.

**2020 presidential result:**Donald Trump beat Joe Biden 58.5 percent to 39.4 percent

**Cook Political Report rating:**Lean R

**Some background:** Fischer’s race wasn’t on many campaign experts’ radars in the larger battle for the Senate. But it has turned into one of most closely watched races in the final weeks of the 2024 campaign.

Fischer is running for her third term after winning easily in 2018, and the state’s Senate delegation has been dominated by Republicans for more than a decade. But outside money is pouring into the state to bolster Osborn, who is trying to appeal to a broad coalition of voters and has pledged he won’t caucus with either party if elected.

**The state of play:**There’s been a notable lack of outside polling in the race, despite sizable national attention in recent months, making it hard to get a good read on who’s actually ahead.

Fischer is describing Osborn, a registered nonpartisan voter, as a “Democrat in disguise.” Former President Donald Trump, who is expected to win most of Nebraska’s electoral college votes, recently cut an ad criticizing Osborn.

But Osborn’s internal polls have shown him constantly with a narrow lead, and Fischer has acknowledged that the race has tightened. National Republicans have stepped up their outside spending, saying they are confident Fischer will be able to hold onto her seat as they try to close the fundraising gap. Fischer’s own internal polling has her with a slight lead.

**Why you should care:** If Osborn pulls out a win, it has ramifications for the larger Senate map. Republicans need a net of two pickups to have an outright majority, so an Osborn win would provide an additional hurdle.

An Osborn victory could also have larger implications for how to run for federal office in a state typically dominated by one party. And Republicans know the money they are putting into Nebraska right now is miniscule compared to the amount they will have to spend in six years if Osborn wins.

**The candidates:**Osborn, a steamfitter and Navy veteran, has not previously run for political office. He first gained national attention for leading a strike against Kellogg’s in 2021.

Fischer has served in the Senate since 2013, and is poised to lead the Senate Rules Committee if Republicans flip the chamber. Before joining the Senate, she was a member of the Nebraska Legislature.

**The issues:**Fischer and Osborn won’t have a face-to-face debate before the election. (Osborn has made that an issue, including showing up at events in Nebraska with signs questioning why Fischer won’t debate him.)

Fischer, meanwhile, is putting her work in the Senate at the center of her reelection campaign, arguing that Osborn is out of step with Nebraska on issues like immigration.

Osborn has tried to avoid being boxed in with either party, instead trying to tap into a populist frustration with Washington that his supporters are hoping will allow him to flip Trump voters in the red state.

Giffords PAC, founded by former congresswoman Gabby Giffords, announced a new $1.8 million swing state campaign to back Kamala Harris on Wednesday, targeting women and Latino voters in Michigan and Nevada, according to plans first shared with POLITICO. And the gun control group used a gun owner to deliver its message.

The group’s latest batch of television and digital ads feature Steve K., a gun owner and a veteran. In the spot, he talks about why it’s important to make sure these weapons “don’t end up in the wrong hands” and hits Donald Trump for refusing to “close gun loopholes“ while in office. The latest ad comes after the gun safety group launched Gun Owners for Harris, which consisted of more than 400 members across 45 states, including Washington, D.C.

The ad blitz, which is the latest component of the group’s more than $15 million campaign to support the vice president and House candidates in battleground districts who favor tougher gun laws, echoes Harris’ strategy in talking about guns on the trail. The vice president has mentioned owning a gun several times, while also pushing for gun restrictions, including an assault weapons ban, universal background checks and red-flag laws. Highlighting her gun ownership serves as another way for Harris to project strength and expand her support in rural and small-town America, as well as among the independent and Republican-leaning voters her campaign is targeting.

Oct 2024 | EXCLUSIVE

Republicans are sounding the alarm that they need to close the money gap with Democrats in the battle for state legislatures, calling the “financial disparity this cycle … more significant than ever.”

The Republican State Leadership Committee announced in a donor memo, first shared with POLITICO, that it increased its investment in state legislative races this year to $44 million. That’s up from its previously announced $38 million, which it said was its largest investment ever in these contests.

“Despite the RSLC’s historic $44 million investment in 2024, we will still be outpaced by the $175 million the [Democratic Legislative Campaign Committee], the States Project, and Forward Majority PAC have pledged to spend alone,” RSLC President Dee Duncan wrote in the memo.

“Since the three aforementioned national liberal outside groups have already combined to spend at least $69 million of traceable money across the country, that means they are on the road to dump at least another $100 million into key races between now and November,” the memo says.

The memo references a recent plea from the RSLC’s Democratic counterpart, the DLCC, saying that it needs to raise millions of dollars to close the gap for its $60 million election budget.

“They are clearly seeking to replicate their 2022 playbook where they overwhelmed us on the airwaves in the final weeks,” Duncan writes. “We don’t expect to fully close the fundraising gap we face, but additional resources are still immediately needed to counter the opposition’s massive spending advantage in these final weeks to ensure we remain on course to defend our majorities and seize any and all opportunities to flip chambers still within reach.”

Duncan also notes that the committee’s early spending has led to “increasing our margins on early-vote and vote-by-mail” — a practice that Republicans have lagged behind Democrats on.

He noted that it is “imperative that we continue to emphasize the importance of absentee, early-vote, and vote-by-mail, during the final stretch of this election cycle,” and points to stats in Michigan and Pennsylvania, including more GOP requests for mail voting in those states at this point in the cycle compared to the whole 2022 cycle. The committee has lauded its absentee ballot and early voting program, which it invested millions into in 2023.

Republicans are defending their majorities in both chambers in Arizona, Wisconsin — where there are new, more competitive maps this year that wiped out an effective permanent Republican majority in both chambers — and New Hampshire, as well as the Pennsylvania Senate. They’re looking to flip the state House in Michigan, Minnesota and Pennsylvania. And as Democrats more forcefully go after Republicans’ supermajorities, the GOP is also looking to protect its leads in both chambers in Florida, Kansas, North Carolina and Ohio, as well as the Iowa Senate.

Democrats have long struggled to catch up with Republicans at the state legislative level, where the right has historically focused more on these races. But that’s changed in recent cycles, where national Democratic players have ramped up their spending. Last month, Harris’ campaign and the Democratic National Committee sent a historic $2.5 million to the DLCC.

Oct 2024

Democrats seeking to expand their ranks in state legislatures are zeroing in on a closing campaign message: We are the firewall against efforts to subvert democracy.

The party writ large has campaigned on a message about the threat posed by Donald Trump after his effort to overturn the 2020 election, but state legislative candidates are emphasizing their specific role in the process. As a result, these races, often overlooked, have been injected with record amounts of cash and attention.

The Democratic Legislative Campaign Committee intends to make this part of their final argument to shore up a legislative majority in Pennsylvania and make inroads in other swing states where chambers could be in play, such as Arizona and Michigan. Democrats also hope to erode Republicans’ supermajority in the North Carolina legislature.

“Electing Democrats to statehouses next month will have immediate and long-term impacts on upholding our democracy and ensuring a safe and stable transfer of power,” DLCC President Heather Williams wrote in an Oct. 23 memo shared exclusively with POLITICO.

Anxiety over the membership of state legislatures is rooted in a Trump-backed theory that these bodies have unilateral power to offer their own slates of presidential electors even if they conflict with the popular vote results. In a scenario where Republicans dispute the election results, state legislatures could, in theory, send these alternate slates to Congress and force a Jan. 6, 2025 conflict. It’s an approach that got little traction and ultimately failed in 2020 — but is still one that’s keeping Democrats up at night.

In a related push, Democratic group The States Project is out with new research hammering election deniers in state legislative races and also shaming Republicans without a clear position on whether state legislatures have the ability to overturn the results of an election, according to an analysis shared exclusively with POLITICO.

Ninety-three percent of GOP state legislative candidates in “winnable” districts across the battleground states of Arizona, Michigan, Nevada, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin have not indicated how they would handle an attempt to overturn the results of the 2024 presidential election, according to The States Project, which is focused on winning Democratic majorities in those states.

In Arizona, the hotbed of election denialism, 43 candidates in the state House and Senate have “anti-democracy beliefs,” a label they earned through actions like attending the Jan. 6 insurrection or calling for decertification of the 2020 results, according to the analysis. Another 12 “have not expressed a clear view on the issue to voters.” Just one Republican, Rep. T.J. Shope, stands out for calling President Joe Biden the legitimate winner.

“The importance of these state legislative elections cannot be overstated,” said Simone Leiro, chief communications officer for The States Project. “Majorities elected this November could be in office when their state’s electors are sent to DC and having majorities that will respect the will of the voters and stay out of their ability to elect their next president is critical to ensuring free and fair elections for every voter this fall.”
