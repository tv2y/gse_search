Red Dead Redemption 2’s single-player campaign is designed to be fully replayable and many players do play it more than once, but whether it feels “too long” depends on what you want from a second run.

What replayability looks like

*   Full replay: You can start a new save and play the entire story again from the beginning, including all main missions, side quests, random encounters, and Honor-related outcomes. The game does not lock story content behind online progress.
*   Chapter structure: The campaign is divided into chapters; you can replay specific segments by loading manual saves or using multiple save slots to jump to a chapter you want to revisit.
*   Different approaches: A second run lets you change playstyle — lawful vs. outlaw (Honor), role-play choices, different weapon/loadout strategies, moral choices in key scenes, and hunting/crafting focus.
*   Completionists: If your aim is 100% completion, expect to replay selectively (missed stranger missions, challenges, or collectibles) rather than the entire 60–80+ hour main campaign.

Length and pacing considerations

*   Typical single-play length: Main story ~40–60 hours; main + many side activities ~60–120 hours depending on play style and thoroughness. Average second playthrough will be shorter if you skip optional content.
*   Perception of “too long”: Many players find the first playthrough feels long due to pacing and setting-up of characters. A second run often feels tighter because you know narrative beats, allowing you to focus on favorite missions or outcomes.
*   Time-savers for repeat plays:

    *   Use save-scumming: keep saves at chapter starts to jump to desired moments.
    *   Skip optional activities and hunting unless collecting is your goal.
    *   Use Dead Eye, weapons, and horse upgrades saved from prior playthrough if you keep any system-specific carryover (note: story progress does not carry over across different characters; only some players’ personal strategies or knowledge carry).
    *   Follow mission lists or guides to replay only specific chapters or missions.

When a full second playthrough makes sense

*   You want to explore alternative moral choices, dialogue/scene variations, and character nuance.
*   You want to experience different gameplay (stealth vs. guns-blazing), or to focus on collectibles/challenges missed the first time.
*   You simply love the world, characters, and atmosphere and enjoy re-living the story.

When it may feel too long

*   You want only a few key missions or endings — replaying the whole campaign will feel repetitive unless you skip content.
*   Time-limited players who prefer shorter, bite-sized replay experiences; consider chapter-select saves or targeted guides instead of a full run.

Summary

Red Dead Redemption 2 absolutely supports replaying the story, and many players do a second playthrough to change choices, improve completion, or savor the world with different playstyles. The campaign is long on first play, but a second run can be much shorter and more focused if you use saves and skip optional content; whether it’s “too long” depends on how much of the game you intend to repeat.

When it comes to open world games and story, the reality is not about length, it’s about perceived length. I’ll give you a few examples.

FALLOUT 3: Fallout three took me nearly 100 hours to beat. That is not including DLC, I’m talking bout the main quest; most of that is becuase traveling in vanilla Fallout 3 is a lethal prospect unless you a) use cheats or b) have leveled up so hard that most places aren't a challenge. Here are the steps to following the main quest line checkpoints:

Go to megaton and talk to Moriarty.

Go to Galaxy News Radio and do Three Dog’s quest.

Got to Rivet City and talk to Dr. Lee.

Got to the Jefferson Memorial.

Go to Vault 12.

Go to Rivet City.

Go to Jefferson Memorial.

Escort Dr. Lee and survivors to The Citadel.

Get the location of Vault 87.

Go to Little Lamplight.

Go through Murder Pass (or hack the terminal) and enter Vault 87.

Get the GECK.

Leave Vault 87 and get knocked out.

Meet President Eaton.

Leave Raven Rock.

Return to the Citadel.

Join the Lion’s Pride and fight your way to the purifier.

Start the purifier.

Game Over.

That’s 18 steps. There are minor points in between, but those are the major ones. Why did it take me so song to finish? because between every one of those steps there’s a living world full of violence, terror, hope and joy. there are people to talk to, bandits to fight (or hide from), radiation spawned monsters, a nice old lady who plays the violin for you, a community of children where people who turn 16 are turned out into the wastes, an entire race of creatures hell bent on furthering their species at all costs. There are hidden communites and secret paths and treasures and hostile radiation environments to be discovered.

Compared to that, most modern open world games feel very flat because they seem to only concentrate on the design of the world, leaving the story absent from the game world, which is odd, but there it is.

GTA V: Here’s a game with a massive and highly detailed open world and a story that’s easily the most forgettable of the past ten years. The main story follows three unlikable people and focuses on…I’m not exactly sure. There doesn't seem to be a real reason behind it, only a way to meander through the world in order to reach the point where you can just go off and do your own thing and ignore everything else. I find this to be quite sad because the world is truly one of the most detailed and beautiful I’ve ever seen. Now, I’ve not finished GTA V, and probably won’t because the world is far more interesting than the story and at this point, I couldn’t tell you what was going on in any case.

They say the story of GTA V can be completed in 25–30 hours, but the problem, at least for me, was that there was too much story and not enough investment. I played GTA V for about 6 hours, and in that time I couldn’t tell you about anything that happened narrative wise. I’ve watched videos online about the campaign, but with the exception of the alien cult (ala Scientology) I still don’t remember much, and that’s just sad. They say that GTA IV’s campaign was about 40–45 hours which was obviously too long, but is it any better if the story is so forgettable that not having it improves the game-play? After all GTA Online still makes a ton of money.

In Summary: The length of an open world title’s campaign doesn’t really matter as much as the way that story is told. If the story is an intricate part of the world, and is woven through it, then the campaign can be fairly long, provided there is something meaningful to do while traveling from one story beat to the next.

The Witcher 3 is famous for it’s length, taking between 25 and 200 hours depending on how you go about it. If you only want to focus on the main story, you can get it done in between 25–40 hours depending on your level of skill and your tolerance for level-grinding to meet a goal. Like Fallout 3, The Witcher 3’s story is interlaced with the world, making the whole thing feel organic and natural while still being strange and fantastic.

That, I believe is what so many of the newer open world games are missing; not content as much as meaningful content. When the world feels like a checklist, you’re doing it wrong.

Yes and no.

The bad sides are the controls, which are clunky and unintuitive and because the same buttons are needed for different actions, you can often end up accidentally shooting people in the face.

The speed at which our protagonist moves around camp is ridiculously slow. You'd be forgiven for thinking Arthur has Arthritis.

The omission of fast travel is a good and a bad thing. It certainly lends, nay forces a more realistic take on traversing the world but, it can jar a little when you have a long ride back somewhere (and some of those rides are looooong). That being said, fast travel is there but, it’s not obvious nor easy to discover and when you do, it doesn’t work in the way you’ll expect. There are restrictions and it’s often better to take a coach or train.

Bumping into people no matter how softly earns you a wanted status. It’s ludicrous.

The opening chapter is monotonous beyond belief and will have many walking away from it before even giving it the chance to draw them in.

Now, there are many more niggling detractors besides these but, these are my main issues with the game. Do they affect my enjoyment of it? Sometimes, but not enough to stop me from saying that it is still the standout game of the year.

* * *

The high points of this glorious game - and believe me, there are far too many to list - turn what could have been a shameless and boring cash-in into something absolutely exceptional.

Yes, the controls are pants but, they work once you get the hang of them. Furthermore, once you get past that laborious first chapter and out of the mountains, the game seriously opens up and grows in every direction.

The diversity of the world is astonishing. It’s completely organic and you genuinely feel part of a living and breathing wild west. People go about their business entirely naturally. Horses crap on the floor whilst waiting around. Animals you hunt and place on your horse flop about when you’re riding with them to the tanner. You get held up by bandits. You get ambushed without warning regularly. You can kill with wanton abandon or be a good guy. You can be whatever you want to be and whatever choices you make affect the world and its inhabitants around you.

RDR2 is hugely ambitious and it works almost flawlessly and what really seals this games legacy is the small details hiding from plain view. It encourages exploration away from the main story and when you do go off the beaten track, you start to discover all the really interesting stuff this world has to offer. People, sects, hunting, missions - you name it.

* * *

I get that some people will not like the pacing of this game because it is not GTA. It is not an out and out western action game. It is almost a simulation of being a cowboy in the wild west. You have to care for yourself and your horse all the while tolerating issues that would ruin a lesser game.

Stay with it, however, and the game evolves into something truly extraordinary and does indeed live up to the praise bestowed upon it.

I did, to be honest.

I liked that I could play at my own pace with no detriment to the story. I don't mean that I'm slow at it, I just mean that I love to explore. I liked to find every nook and cranny, every secret, every little detail, both of the world and the characters from stories or little discoverable things (letters, clippings, etc) in camp. I'm the sort of person who will collect everything and do every sidequest. The game didn't penslise me for that. It didn't nag at me to stop spending four days drawing landmarks, finding cigarette cards or finding crafting materials for an eccentric hatmaker. It didn't go “Oi, this character needs you, stop taking photos with that man and filling out your compendium, we have a bank to rob.” it let me do what I wanted.

The story was bolstered by the characters. They're fabulous. It's a great cast. Even the villain has his sympathetic moments and enough optional background can be found for some of the characters that even the bad guys (from this game or those that survive to villainy in RDR1) have understandable viewpoints, behaviour patterns or opinions.

A few things, though …

One, I felt Guarma was unnecessary. It was like I was suddenly in Assassin's Creed: Black Flag, but with sweaty outlaws and open-shirted Micah which I will never unsee. I'd have preferred a sojourn to Mexico, or even just New Austin. It felt out of place and was really restricted. Did not enjoy.

Two, it's definitely worth playing High Honour. For one, it's way harder to keep your honour low than high, because you have to go out of your way to consistently be a raging butthole and cause mayhem all the time, where as high honour just requires throwing back a few fish and feeding your horse. I'd replay it all again if they released an extended edition for the PS5.
