A newly written epic poem does not strictly need to be in blank verse; looser forms, including free verse, can be acceptable, though challenging. 

Key points from the discussion:
1. **Blank verse** (unrhymed iambic pentameter) is traditional for epics because it avoids the repetitive "arrival" of rhyme, using other poetic devices like alliteration, assonance, and meter to maintain structure.
2. **Free verse** can work for contemporary epics but requires exceptional skill (e.g., Margaret Ann Griffiths) and a strong performer to sustain narrative and rhythm without meter.
3. **Rhyme and structure** help in memorability and flow. Removing meter risks a disjointed effect unless replaced with equally compelling techniques.
4. **Prose poems** are deemed unsuitable for epics due to their lack of mnemonic and rhythmic support.
5. Some argue that **learning traditional forms** first enhances free verse, as understanding rhythm and structure improves intuitive poetic flow.

Notable opinions:
- Colin: Free verse epics are possible but rare and demand mastery.
- Tzvi Gross: Learning rules before breaking them ensures rhythmic quality even in free verse.
- Eliot Sorde: Slant rhymes can be effective if used deliberately, not as a fallback for imperfect rhymes.

In short, while blank verse is the classic choice, modern epics can experiment with looser forms if executed with skill and awareness of poetic conventions.
