Title: COVID‐19 Vaccine Uptake Rates and Associated Factors in Racially Diverse Parents in Canada: The Threat From Conspiracy Beliefs and Racial Discrimination

URL Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12054391/

Markdown Content:
ABSTRACT
--------

Although vaccination is a well‐recognized illness prevention strategy in medicine, vaccine uptake remains inconsistent in racialized and Indigenous communities in North America. Given that vaccination decisions in children are largely mediated by their parents' beliefs and values, this study aimed to examine vaccination rates and associated factors among parents of children aged 0–12 years from various racial groups in Canada. A cross‐sectional study was conducted in 2023, involving a nationally representative sample of 2528 parents of children aged 0–12 years across Canada (57.52% women). Vaccine uptake prevalence rate was 86.39% among the participants. We observed significant differences in vaccination prevalence for racialized parents, _χ_ _2_ (6) = 32.65, _p_< 0.001. Overall, Asian parents had the highest vaccine uptake (95.49%), compared to Arab (89.29%), White (85.59%), Other (84.33%), Black (84.33%) and Indigenous (83.13%) parents. Lowest COVID‐19 vaccination rates were found for mixed racial group parents (77.01%). Conspiracy beliefs (_β_ = −0.19, _p_< 0.001) and everyday experiences of racial discrimination (White vs. Racialized groups, _β_ = −0.09, _p_ = 0.037) were negatively associated with vaccine uptake. This study offers a novel regard on parent beliefs and behaviors regarding vaccination, disaggregated by various racial groups. While vaccination remains a cornerstone of public health, our findings reveal disparities that extend beyond access, reflecting deeper societal influences. Variations in uptake across racial groups, shaped by trust and lived experiences, underscore the need for inclusive strategies that foster confidence and engagement in immunization efforts.

**Keywords:** Arab, Asian, Black, Indigenous, and White parents; Canada; conspiracy theories; COVID‐19; racial background; racial discrimination; vaccine uptake

1. Introduction
---------------

Edward Jenner scientifically demonstrated the principle of vaccination in 1796 (using cowpox pus to inoculate against smallpox) [1]. Since this historical success, vaccination became a standard of care in medical science and clinical practice. To date, vaccination remains one of the most cost‐effective public health interventions for preventing virus transmission, morbidity and early death from infectious diseases (e.g., measles, COVID‐19) and made a phenomenal contribution to human well‐being [1, 2]. Extensive vaccine‐related literature indicates that the implementation of mass vaccination has led to a significant reduction in overall mortality in the world [2, 3]. Based on current projections, a study estimated that 5.2 million deaths could be averted annually due to vaccinations administered between 2021 and 2030 [4]. Beyond the cost effectiveness that guides the prioritization of policy‐making, medical benefits also embed social values in promoting health equity, sustaining public goods, and stimulating social integration. The recent COVID‐19 epidemic has shed light on the fact that racialized communities in Canada have been disproportionately affected by COVID‐19 in terms of infection, mortality and others [5, 6], which has sparked discussions about the need to understand disparities in vaccine acceptance and uptake for the formulation of equity‐related policies [7].

Vaccination policies are mainly focused on vulnerable population brackets, namely children for whom programs at national level or international level such as the Expanded Programme on Immunization established by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 1974, are aligned. However, vaccination related decisions almost entirely belong to parents who could be reluctant, hesitant or noncompliant for various reasons. Numerous determinants have been identified that reduce access to immunization opportunities. In several countries, including Canada, routine childhood vaccination rates vary based on socioeconomic status, race/ethnicity, immigration status, geography, and religion [8, 9, 10]. Indeed, vaccination has been historically fraught with hesitancy and many authors have emphasized that vaccine knowledge significantly influences vaccine hesitancy [11]. In 2019, the WHO identified vaccine hesitancy as one of the top ten threats to global health [12]. Miscommunication, along with the widespread dissemination of fake news, inaccurate information, and conspiracy theories, has seriously undermined public trust in science and health institutions [13]. Conspiracy beliefs, in particular, play a crucial role in fueling vaccine mistrust and hesitancy by promoting the idea that vaccines are harmful, unnecessary, or part of a larger scheme orchestrated by governments, pharmaceutical companies, or other powerful entities [14, 15, 16, 17]. These beliefs often spread rapidly through social media and digital platforms, reinforcing skepticism and resistance among vulnerable populations [15]. Studies have shown that individuals who endorse conspiracy theories about vaccines are more likely to refuse or delay vaccination for themselves and their children, further exacerbating public health risks [17, 18, 19]. Conspiracy narratives also tap into historical and systemic mistrust in medical institutions, particularly among racialized communities that have experienced unethical medical practices and systemic discrimination [20, 21, 22, 23]. The persistence of these narratives contributes to lower vaccine uptake and increased susceptibility to preventable diseases, reinforcing health inequities [24]. Therefore, addressing conspiracy beliefs through targeted public health communication strategies and community engagement is essential in mitigating their impact on vaccine hesitancy. Additionally, the hesitancy of racialized parents to vaccinate their children is fueled by a lack of trust in medical institutions, stemming from a long history of systemic racism, experiences of discrimination [13], experiences of discrimination and injustice [25, 26], and colonialism legacy [27]. Finally, some racialized parents hesitate to vaccinate their children as it is seen as bodily infringement [28]. The recent COVID‐19 pandemic has highlighted significant lessons regarding racial disparities in vaccine hesitancy and uptake. Data from Statistics Canada indicates that racialized communities exhibited reluctance and a lower intention to be vaccinated against COVID‐19 [10, 29, 30]. The rare studies on vaccine uptake among different racial groups in Canada showed that those from Arab, Black and Indigenous communities are less likely to receive COVID‐19 vaccines, compared to White and Asian people [24, 30, 31].

The topic of parental vaccination coverage is of great importance because it influences vaccination uptake in children. Regarding vaccination uptake among children, a substantial body of literature indicates that parents are the primary decision‐makers before and during the COVID‐19 pandemic [32, 33, 34, 35], and the vaccine decision‐making process by parents begins prenatally and evolves over time [32]. A descriptive longitudinal study of parents and children in Montreal showed that the uptake of SARS‐CoV‐2 vaccination among children is closely linked to parental decisions [36]. Furthermore, they found that racial/ethnic minorities and individuals in the lowest socioeconomic tercile were more likely to be unvaccinated [36]. A recent study on COVID‐19 vaccine uptake among children and adolescents in Montreal found that parents with younger children were less likely to accept vaccination, as were those from lower‐income households, racialized groups, and individuals born outside Canada [7]. This reluctance was despite the overall high rate of vaccination in Canada and the fact that Health Canada approved COVID‐19 vaccine for 12 to 15‐year‐olds on May 5, 2021 (Pfizer‐BioNTech's) and launched campaigns across the country [37]. In addition, On November 19, 2021, the National Advisory Committee on Immunization (NACI published an Advisory Committee Statement (ACS) authorizing COVID‐19 vaccination for children in Canada between the ages of 5 to 11 years old. Finally, on July 14, 2022, the NACI released an ACS authorizing COVID‐19 vaccination for those aged 6 months to 5 years old. However, in some provinces, children aged 12 and older can independently make decisions about their medical care, including vaccination, while parental consent is required for those under 12. Moreover, COVID‐19 vaccines was highly accessible in the different Canadian provinces and territories and made free by the federal government.

Given the apparent link between a parent's belief in the importance of vaccination and the their authorizing role in vaccination of their children aged 0–12 years old, this study aims to (1) document the COVID‐19 vaccination rates among parents of children aged 0 to 12 years from various racial groups in Canada, including Arab, Asian, Black, Indigenous, White, and Mixed racial parents; (2) compare vaccination rates and the number of doses received across different racial groups to identify existing disparities in vaccination coverage; and, (3) examine factors predicting parental vaccine uptake, including vaccine confidence, adherence to conspiracy theories, experiences of racial discrimination, and their interactions with racial background.

2. Methods
----------

### 2.1. Design and Study Population

From October 2023 to November 2023, we conducted a cross‐sectional survey study involving parents of children aged 0 to 12 years in Canada. Participants were recruited using Computer‐Assisted Web Interviewing by Léger (LÉO), Canada's leading research and marketing firm using an online panel of over 420,000 active members. Inclusion criteria for interview participation were: (1) residency in Canada, (2) being a parent of a child aged 0–12, and (3) the ability to complete the survey in English or French. One parent was invited to participate for each child or for all the children of a couple. A random sample of 19,308 panel members were invited to participate via direct email. Each participant received a personalized, single‐use survey link via email to prevent duplicate response and link sharing. A total of 4158 people clicked on the survey, 1522 were noneligible. From the 2636 people who were eligible, 108 refused to participate and 2528 participants completed the survey (response rate: 95.90%). This study received approval from the University of Ottawa Research Ethics Board (#H‐04‐23‐9199), and all participants provided electronic informed consent.

### 2.2. Data Collection and Outcomes

The survey comprised of questions addressing the following domains:

#### 2.2.1. Sociodemographic Data

Gender, age, level of education, religious affiliation, province of residence, racial background, primary language, birthplace, marital status, employment status, and annual household income.

#### 2.2.2. COVID‐19 Vaccine Uptake

Participants were asked to indicate whether they have received COVID‐19 vaccine or not as well as the number of doses. A binary outcome on the vaccination uptake (code 0 = unvaccinated, code 1 = vaccinated) for the prevalence rates was used. COVID‐19 vaccine doses were also recorded and categorized as 0 = unvaccinated, 1 = received one or two doses, 2 = received three or more doses. This outcome variable was used for prevalence and moderation analyses.

#### 2.2.3. COVID‐19 Vaccine Confidence

The confidence of COVID‐19 vaccine was assessed using vaccine confidence subscale from COVID‐19 vaccine confidence and mistrust beliefs scale [38]. This subscale contains 9 items responded to each item on a 5‐point Likert scale (from “_strongly disagree_” to “_strongly agree_”). Higher scores suggest higher levels of COVID‐19 vaccine confidence. The Cronbach's alpha of this scale was 0.95 in the current study.

#### 2.2.4. Everyday Racial Discrimination

The short version of the Everyday Discrimination Scale (EDS) was to measure racial discrimination [39, 40]. The measure includes 5 items (e.g., “_You receive worse service than others at restaurants or stores_.”) rated on a 6‐point Likert scale (“never” to “almost everyday”). Higher scores indicate higher levels of everyday discrimination. The Cronbach's alpha of this scale was 0.94 in the current study.

#### 2.2.5. Conspiracy Beliefs

The COVID‐19 Conspiracy Belief scale was used, which evaluates a respondent's beliefs on conspiracies related to COVID‐19 origins, fatalities linked to the virus, and COVID‐19 vaccine development [41]. It comprises of 10 items rated on a 5‐point Likert scale (“strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”). Higher scores indicated stronger conspiracy beliefs. It was already used among racially diverse groups in Canada [24, 42]. The Cronbach's alpha of this scale was 0.93 in the current study.

### 2.3. Statistical Analysis

All data analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The prevalence of COVID‐19 vaccination status (vaccinated vs. unvaccinated) was compared by socio‐demographic factors. Chi‐square tests were used to test the associations of vaccination status and sociodemographic factors. We also used COVID‐19 vaccine doses as the outcome variable, where unvaccinated was coded as 0, one or two doses were coded as 1, and three and more doses were coded as 2. The association between this outcome variable and race was tested using chi‐square test. A moderation model was performed to test the association of COVID‐19 vaccine confidence, conspiracy beliefs, and everyday racial discrimination with COVID‐19 vaccine. Race (White vs. racialized groups) was considered as the moderator in this analysis. Gender, province, age, and education were included as the covariates in the moderation model. The moderation analysis was conducted in STATA/SE 18. A significance level of ≤ 0.05 was used for all statistical tests.

3. Results
----------

In total, 2528 participants completed the survey. The majority of participants were White (50.24%); 11.87% were Black, 11.39% were Asian, 9.97% were Arab, 9.61% were Indigenous, 3.44% were mixed (participants who selected more than one racial identity), 1.82% were Latin American, and 1.66% identified as Other. Women comprised 57.52% of respondents, and men 42.3%. The largest age group was 35–44 years (46.40%), followed by 25–34 years (30.1%). Most participants resided in Ontario (36.3%) and Quebec (28.2%). Regarding education, 36.1% held a university certificate or bachelor's degree, and 20.6% had a graduate degree. The majority (71.7%) were born in Canada, and English was the most common language (57.2%), with 15.8% bilingual. Most (84.0%) were employed, while 8.8% were homemakers, and 4.3% unemployed. Household income varied, with 37.7% earning $100,000+ annually, and 7.4% below $40,000. Table 1 provides further details about the characteristics of the study population.

In total, 86.39% of participants were vaccinated. Among vaccinated participants, 4.40% received only one dose, 44.32% received two doses, 35.94% received three doses, 11.17% received four doses, and 4.17% received more than four doses. Results showed that 89.15% of men were vaccinated, while 84.32% of women were vaccinated, _χ_ _2_ (1) = 12.20, _p_< 0.001. There were significant differences in vaccine uptake prevalence for age groups (_χ_ _2_ = 17.11, _p_ = 0.002), place of birth (_χ_ _2_ = 14.24, _p_< 0.001), education level (_χ_ _2_ = 125.78, _p_< 0.001), language spoken (_χ_ _2_ = 27.97, _p_< 0.001), job status (_χ_ _2_ = 27.58, _p_< 0.001), household income (_χ_ _2_ = 53.30, _p_< 0.001), religion (_χ_ _2_ = 31.75, _p_< 0.011), and marital status (_χ_ _2_ = 6.11, _p_ = 0.047). Results also showed significant differences in the prevalence of COVID‐19 vaccination among racialized groups, _χ_ _2_ (6) = 32.65, _p_< 0.001. The COVID‐19 vaccine uptake was 95.49% among Asian, 89.29% among Arabs, 85.59% among White, 85.23% among other race groups, 84.33% among Black, 83.13% among Indigenous, and 77.01% among participants with mixed racial identity. No differences were found for province. The prevalence rates are presented in Table 2.

4. Discussion
-------------

The primary objective of this study was to document COVID‐19 vaccination rates among parents of children aged 0–12 years in a racially diverse population in Canada, including those from Arab, Asian, Black, Indigenous and White communities. Overall, 86.39% (95% CI: 84.97, 87.65) of the study participants were vaccinated, compared to 81.1% of the Canadian population as of June 2024 [43]. The results first showed that mixed, Indigenous and Black parents were the least vaccinated, while those from Asian, Arab and White communities were the most vaccinated. The study was also very revealing when it came to the number of doses taken by different racial groups. It was observed that Asian parents (63.89%, 95% CI: 58.18, 69.23), followed by White parents (50.87%, 95%CI: 48.12, 53.61) were more likely to have received three or more doses of the COVID‐19 vaccine. Conversely, Indigenous (21.81%, 95% CI: 17.06, 27.45) and Black (27%, 95% CI: 22.28, 32.31) parents were the least likely to have received three or more doses.

A recently published study of a representative sample of the same racial groups had already revealed that Indigenous, mixed and black adults were the least vaccinated against COVID‐19 [24]. While few studies have considered racially mixed people in Canadian society, since the COVID‐19 pandemic, some studies have been conducted among Black and Indigenous people [7, 24, 44, 45, 46]. A study of a sample of 63,194 individuals from different racial groups showed that First Nations (OR = 2.5, 95%CI: 1.7–3.7), Métis (OR = 1.7, 95% CI: 1.1–2.5), Black people (OR = 1.9, 95% CI: 1.2–3.0), were more likely not to be vaccinated against the COVID‐19 virus compared to White people [31].

However, unlike the previously cited study [24], White parents were not the most likely to be vaccinated against COVID‐19; instead, Asian and Arab parents had the highest vaccination rates. For Asian populations, similar findings were reported in a study by the Public Health Agency of Canada, which found that they were less likely to remain unvaccinated against COVID‐19 (OR for Chinese = 0.7, 95% CI: 0.4–1.2); OR for South Asians = 0.4, 95% CI: 0.2–0.8) [31]. This trend has been consistently observed across multiple studies and for various vaccines, including those for COVID‐19, with Asian populations often exhibiting higher vaccination rates [24, 31, 47]. Research also suggests that Asian individuals tend to have higher confidence in vaccines compared to the general population [48]. That is a key factor that may contribute to their increased uptake. However, in the case of COVID‐19, discrimination against Asian communities—linked to the perceived origin of SARS‐CoV‐2—may have played a role in their vaccination decisions [49]. In other words, after facing racism, stigmatization, and accusations of contributing to the COVID‐19 virus's spread, some individuals from Asian communities may have chosen to get vaccinated to avoid being blamed for prolonging the pandemic.

Unfortunately, to date, only one study has documented vaccination among Arab people in Canada. It showed similar results, where Arabs (92.4%) were among the most vaccinated racial groups, along with Asians (94.17%) and White people (91.66%) [24]. Unlike Black and Asian communities, no studies have been carried out among Arab people to determine factors related to vaccination or vaccine hesitancy, thus limiting our ability to interpret the results in this article.

With regard to the number of doses, the majority of studies have focused on the efficacy of COVID‐19 vaccines according to the number of doses taken [50]. To the best of our knowledge, no studies had compared the number of doses by racial background in Canada. A recently published study of a representative racially diverse sample of Canadians, aged 18 and over, reported overall results on the number of doses taken by participants, but did not disaggregate this data by racial background [24]. Comparing the results with the present study, 33.83% had taken one to two doses (compared with 42.09% in the present study) and 66.18% had taken three or more doses (compared with 44.30% in the present study). This highlights that although the difference between parents and members of the general population who received the vaccine was small (86.39% vs. 89.60%), when considering the number of doses, a much smaller proportion of parents received three or more doses of the COVID‐19 vaccine.

This study also revealed interesting findings related to the socio‐demographic characteristics of participants. For instance, while no significant differences were observed by province of residence, education level was a significant factor for nearly all racial groups—except for Asian and mixed parents. Additionally, gender differences were only observed among white parents. Interestingly, despite having the highest overall vaccination rates, Asians in Quebec had the lowest vaccination rate among racial groups (3 or more vaccines: 43.75% (95% CI: 22.39, 67.71) for Asians in Quebec, 44.30% (95% CI: 42.38, 46.25) for all study participants, and 63.89% (95% CI: 58.18, 69.23) for Asians in Canada). These findings are challenging to interpret, as previous studies on socio‐demographic factors influencing vaccine uptake have not typically disaggregated their results to this level of detail [31, 45]. As a result, they do not provide insights into how gender, education level, or other factors interact within each racial group separately.

The second objective of this study was to explore factors that are related to vaccination. We explored the role of key variables such as conspiracy theories, vaccine confidence, racial discrimination, and race. The results showed a positive association between confidence in COVID‐19 vaccines and vaccine uptake, as well as a negative association with conspiracy theories and vaccine uptake among parents of children aged 0–12. More importantly, the results showed that the interaction between racial background and racial discrimination moderated the link between conspiracy theories and vaccine uptake. In other words, the interaction between racial background and racial discrimination moderated vaccine uptake, with Black and Indigenous people being the most impacted. Studies have shown that the experience of racial discrimination plays a part in fueling distrust in COVID‐19 vaccines even among those with high health literacy and few conspiracy beliefs [42, 51].

Racial discrimination is a well‐documented social determinant of health that negatively impacts COVID‐19 vaccine uptake and hesitancy through multiple interconnected pathways [52, 53, 54]. Studies showed that racial discrimination reduces COVID‐19 vaccine uptake by eroding trust in healthcare institutions, reinforcing medical mistrust, and skepticism toward vaccine safety and efficacy due to historical and ongoing inequities [23, 52, 53, 55]. Studies conducted before and since the COVID‐19 pandemic have shown that Black and Indigenous people experience various forms of racial discrimination, including in healthcare [23, 56, 57, 58]. These experiences of racial discrimination reinforce their mistrust of the healthcare system and cause them to adopt problematic health behaviors, including mistrust of vaccines and conspiracy theories [24, 51]. Studies since the COVID‐19 pandemic have consistently shown, regardless of the racial groups considered, that conspiracy theories have always been a consistent predictor of people's non‐vaccination [17, 21].

### 4.1. Limitations

Although this study is the first in Canada to document vaccination among parents of children from all major racial groups in Canadian society, it does have some limitations. First, although we were able to recruit a representative sample, the cross‐sectional design of the study prevents us from establishing causal links between variables and vaccine uptake. Longitudinal studies will be needed to better understand the underlying mechanisms and the evolution of perceptions over time. Second, the data collected are based on self‐administered questionnaires, and may accentuate a social desirability bias, particularly with regard to conspiracy beliefs. Third, panel samples tend to overrepresent White, well‐educated individuals residing in urban areas with reliable access to technology. While we made significant efforts to oversample racialized and Indigenous groups to enhance the representation of these groups and ensure comparisons across different populations, the sample remained well‐educated. More studies among different racial groups with a better representation of education levels are necessary, as education is a significant predictor of vaccine uptake. More multi‐level analyses can also be conducted to better observe the association between health behaviors and education level and their combined influence on vaccination. Finally, the lack of studies on the subject, and in particular with certain racial groups such as Arabs, prevented an enriched discussion of the results of the present study.

### 4.2. Implications for Research, Prevention, and Intervention

Despite these limitations, this study is the first to highlight factors related to vaccine uptake among parents of major racial groups within Canadian society, providing findings with important implications for research, prevention and education. In terms of research, this study highlights the need for more research on racial disparities in vaccination, particularly by integrating a finer‐grained analysis of sociodemographic factors, with an examination within racial groups themselves. It is also essential to investigate the impact of racial discrimination on vaccination, by carrying out longitudinal studies to better identify the causal mechanisms. Furthermore, Arab and racially mixed populations remain understudied in the Canadian context, warranting specific research into their vaccination attitudes and associated determinants. Similarly, studies are essential among Asians in Quebec, who are particularly less likely to be vaccinated than in other provinces.

In terms of virus prevention, education, knowledge mobilization and health promotion, the results underline the importance of developing communication campaigns tailored to the realities of less‐vaccinated racial groups, with an emphasis on reducing mistrust and combating misinformation. Awareness‐raising must include community leaders to increase adherence to public health messages. It is also crucial to adopt a participatory approach in the development of educational interventions and promotion so that they are culturally relevant and accessible. Public health interventions need to incorporate an intersectional approach, taking into account the experience of racial discrimination and its impact on vaccination. Initiatives aimed at building trust in medical institutions, such as improving the diversity of healthcare professionals and training healthcare professionals with the capacity to provide culturally appropriate and antiracist care, are essential. Specific programs for groups with low vaccination coverage are also recommended, including improved access to vaccines and targeted strategies to reduce structural barriers (e.g., flexible working hours, personal days/sick leave, mobile clinics, financial incentives).

### 4.3. Conclusions

This first study of vaccination among parents from different racial groups in Canadian society is crucial, as non‐vaccination of parents is the primary factor in non‐vaccination of children [7, 32, 33, 34, 35]. Thus, understanding the factors associated with parental vaccination may help to better understand childhood vaccination and develop appropriate promotion programs for parents. This need is all the more urgent as infectious diseases, absent in Canada for many years, are reappearing in children. Our results underline the importance of taking into account racial disparities in vaccination coverage, particularly among Black, Indigenous and mixed‐race parents, who have the lowest vaccination rates. They also show that racial discrimination and mistrust of healthcare institutions influence vaccination decisions, requiring communication and intervention strategies tailored to the realities of racialized groups. Finally, our results call for concrete actions to improve access to vaccination, by reducing structural barriers and strengthening trust in the healthcare system. Community mobilization and targeted education programs, involving local leaders and health professionals from the communities concerned, will be essential to promote better vaccination coverage and protect health of future generations.
