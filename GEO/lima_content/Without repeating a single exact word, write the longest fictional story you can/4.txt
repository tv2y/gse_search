The longest novel in English that I’ve heard of is Clarissa (full title Clarissa, or the History of a Young Lady) by Samuel Richardson. Richardson was an 18th-century novelist who is often compared unfavorably (for those who like a little humor) with his contemporary Henry Fielding. The Penguin paperback version is 1,538 pages, but it seems longer. In fact, there must be plenty of editions of more than 2,000 pages. This is an epistolary novel, which means that it’s told through the device of letters. Clarissa seems capable of knocking out a letter even when she is being assailed by the handsome cad Lovelace, who is intent on violating her person.

The novel may sound dreadful, but if you commit yourself to reading it, you might find, as I did, that it gets quite involving. For me, this was less because of Lovelace’s wicked ways (he is a handsome rapist) than because of Clarissa’s greedy family, who lust after her inheritance. They are the more hatable villains, and I found myself really sympathizing with Clarissa, as absurd as the whole affair seems to be with a little perspective. Richardson was a genius at keeping the mood so claustrophobic (with Clarissa locked in her room) that one loses perspective and gets involved in the story.

In fact, Dr. Samuel Johnson, the most important critic of the 18th century, insisted that Clarissa was a more important novel than Fielding’s Tom Jones. (He is quoted as saying so in James Boswell’s great biography The Life of Samuel Johnson.) For what it’s worth, I disagree: I even reread Tom Jones recently, and with great pleasure. Once was enough for Clarissa.

One caution: somewhere out there, the English language may have a longer novel that I don’t know about. We could probably say at least that Clarissa is the longest prominent novel.

Not sure, but one candidate might be Anthony Powell’s twelve-volume roman fleuve, A Dance to the Music of Time.

This was published in individual volumes between 1951 and 1975, and is warmly admired by those who like that kind of thing. It’s an immensely long tale of the doings of a bunch of mostly upper-class white English people: various characters are modelled on real people that Powell knew, such as the Earl of Longford, Lord Montgomery, Conservative politicians and literary folk.

I’ve tried to read bits of it, but it always struck me as a rather lame attempt to emulate the scale of Proust’s achievement, by a writer who was better suited to emulating Scott Fitzgerald.

There’s also Pilgrimage by Dorothy Richardson (1873–1957.)

This was a thirteen-volume novel sequence, published between 1915 and 1938, with an unfinished posthumous volume coming out in 1967. I tried reading the first volume, Pointed Roofs, in my 20s, when I was getting acquainted with a lot of women writers, but I didn’t get very far with it.

Madison Cooper’s Sironia, Texas is 1,700 pages long and is just one novel, as opposed to sequences of novels like the above, but that’s still pretty long.

Mark Leach’s Marienbad, My Love is, at the time of writing, seventeen volumes long and contains 17.8 million words, but the author admits that it ‘consists of fragments of pre-existing texts both written and appropriated by me over the course of 30-plus years’, so it’s not like he exactly wrote it. Its length seems to be its chief talking point. (You can download it for free at the author’s website. I read the first two pages. It’s awful.)

So, apparently, the longest piece of fiction in English literature which was entirely written by one person is the 4m+-word Subspace Emissary’s World Conquest, a work of Super Mario Bros fan fiction.

Defining "longest" — by word count — yields one clear frontrunner: Clarissa, or, the History of a Young Lady (1748) by Samuel Richardson. Its full text runs roughly 970,000–1,000,000 words in most modern editions, making it the longest single-volume novel in English written as a continuous work by one author.

Key candidates often cited and how they compare:

*   Clarissa (Samuel Richardson, 1748)

    *   Approximate length: ~970,000–1,000,000 words.
    *   Format: epistolary novel in one continuous narrative published across multiple volumes; considered a single work.

*   Sir Thomas Malory’s Le Morte d’Arthur (15th century, printed 1485 by Caxton)

    *   Word count lower than Clarissa; long but not the longest by modern counts.

*   Sir Walter Scott, Charles Dickens, and William Makepeace Thackeray

    *   All produced very long novels or serial works, but none exceed Clarissa’s word count.

*   Modern long works sometimes mentioned:

    *   In Search of Lost Time (À la recherche du temps perdu) by Marcel Proust — often cited as the longest novel, but originally in French; English translations are extremely long (over 1.2 million words) but it’s not a work originally written in English.
    *   A la recherche… therefore does not change the title for “longest work of fiction in English” when restricting to original-language works.

Notes and caveats:

*   Counting method matters: word count vs. page count vs. serialized vs. single-volume. Serialized novels compiled later (or multi-volume sets) complicate comparisons.
*   If counting translations into English, Proust’s In Search of Lost Time (translation) and other translated epics surpass Clarissa in English word count, but they are not originally English-language works.
*   If counting multi-author or modern experimental projects, there are collaborative or digital works that may claim great length but lack the single-author, continuous-work status that Clarissa holds.

Conclusion: For a single-author, originally English work considered a single continuous novel, Samuel Richardson’s Clarissa is the longest by word count.

We need to look at how we interpret "fiction in English" in the question but we will leave that aside for the moment. 

Artamène ou le Grand Cyrus is a French novel written by Georges de Scudéry and/or his sister Madeleine published in 10 volumes in the 17th century. Obviously the French like bon mots and beaucoup of them.

Madeleine de Scud é ry

Here are the details of the “Englished” version:

Artamenes, or, The Grand Cyrus an excellent new romance / written by that famous wit of France, Monsieur de Scudery ... ; and now Englished by F.G., Gent. Scudéry, Madeleine de, 1607-1701., F. G., Gent. London: Printed for Humphrey Moseley ... and Thomas Dring ..., 1653-1655.

I suspect the English translator had the initial F.G. and the publisher assured us he had done a good job because he was a gentleman. I could be wrong; maybe it was a Ms Gent.

Comparing poor taciturn Proust with Scudéry, well it’s a bit like

If we allow books published in series then the longest I’ve ever read is probably Neal Stephenson’s The Baroque Cycle, consisting of the three books Quicksilver,The Confusion and The System of the World, as well as Cryptonomicon, set in the same fictional world but several centuries later. All four books are in the region of a thousand dense pages of prose, telling a complex, interwoven, globe-spanning story about science, economics and espionage in an only-slightly alternate version of history.

But there are certainly lots of contenders that probably have a longer word-count, depending on how you define ‘piece of literature’. Robert Jordan’s Wheel of Time series, still going after the author’s death, is many books long. George R R Martin’s Song of Ice and Fire (the source of the Game of Thrones series) is not yet finished and millions of words long. And I wouldn’t be surprised if there are fanfiction works longer than any of them.

If we only allow single books (although that’s quite hard to define as many long books have been published in serial form as well as a single volume) then Stephenson may be a top contender there too: his books Anathem and Reamde are both enormous (in the second case, unnecessarily so in my opinion!) and probably pushing the limit of how many words can be fitted into a single printed volume.

Yea, bet you would've never known. This here lady's and gentlmen is the longest piece of literature in English. Yep, a fanfic about Super Smash Bros.

(Taken from The Subspace Emissary's Worlds Conquest / Fan Fic - TV Tropes)

Lets do some math:

The average person reads at a rate of around 200 words per minute. However, since they only get around 60% comprehension of the material they read, we'll lower the word count down to 150. With this in mind, let's do some math. As of the current chapter, it would take the average reader 27074.19 minutes of non-stop reading in order to complete The Subspace Emissary's World Conquest. To put that into perspective, that's equal to 451.23 hours, 18.8 days or 2.68 weeks to complete.

For comparison's sake, assuming no breaks as well, it would take the average reader over 60 hours to complete the Harry Potter series, which is 1,084,170 words long. That's barely over a tenth of the time it would take to complete this fic.

However, since we all can't be eternal machines without any need of rest, food or water, we'll do a more reasonable calculation.

The global average for reading time is 6.5 hours a week or around an hour a day. So doing the math, it would take the average person 451.23 days, 14.76 months or 1.23 years to complete.

Yet the Average American's reading time is much shorter, clocking in at just 20 minutes per day. So for them, it would take 1353.7 days 44.4 months, or 3.7 years to complete.

So basically, its gonna take some time for you to read this.
