It should come from your knowledge of human behavior, and when playing a character, you should be acting out a part. Fleshing them out, giving them pieces of wisdom, pieces of folly…

A character should be fleshed out like that. So, if you really want to write good characters, you should be a good actor. Learn how to play different roles. If you played games as a kid, where you and your friends took on roles, you should incorporate that into the novel. Like, I was steeped in childsplay until I was about sixteen. I played Role Play all my life, so building characters was natural for me. I had played characters my whole life.

But, if you don’t have this, you should take acting classes, and learn how to really reprise your role and become a Character for the instances of their dialogue. It should come spontaneously, if you have any Providential blessing to do it.

So, you think you’ve drafted a tweet, an email, a short story, or even a novel. These are different forms of communication, but the process of bringing them to fruition has a necessary, sometimes overlooked step: editing! Unless you’re a professional writer, it’s unlikely that you have an editor who can review your writing regularly. Here are some tips to help you review your own work.

1.   Give your writing some space. Have you ever felt a mix of pure relief and joy when you’ve finished a draft of something? Don’t downplay that feeling and the ability to walk away from your work before you start editing it. You may need minutes, hours, or days, but once you sit back down with what you originally had on the page, you’ll have the thrill of looking at it with fresh eyes. You’ll notice errors you may not have seen the first time. You’ll come to new realizations about its overall tone and structure. If it’s a text or email, maybe you only need a few minutes away from it. If it’s a story or essay, perhaps you’ll need longer. Regardless of what type of work it is, it will help your writing tremendously.
2.   Don’t use overachieving synonyms. Looking at your work for the second, third, or fourth time around may inspire you to spice up your language with longer, more uncommon words. There’s nothing wrong with having a thesaurus nearby, but try to limit the repetition of long, pretentious-feeling words so your work flows well and doesn’t feel too bogged down. At the end of the day, you want it to feel true to you and the message you’re conveying.
3.   Remember who the reader is. Don’t forget your own voice as the writer—but don’t forget who your reader is. Many writers get too close to their work; editing is a chance to try to get out of your own head. Who is your ideal reader? What do you want them to take away from the writing? It’s a unique time to step in their shoes, to make sure your communication is as effective as you’d like it to be.
4.   Kill your darlings. Don’t be scared to remove chunks of your work, even if it feels precious to you. If it’s a passage that’s really tough to part with, try saving it somewhere else, so you can return to it later in your piece or for another work.

Most writers pay close attention to other people - to their ideas, the way they express themselves, mannerisms, etc. Many writers also are fascinated by what other people do. Sometimes, if I’m stuck with a stranger, and have to pass the time, I just ask them to talk about what they do - for a living, as a hobby, anything. A lot of the time it’s interesting, and I walk away with a little more “material.”

Writing about unfamiliar experiences and perspectives can require some research. When you know what a character does (or did), it’s often much easier to imagine their perspective. A character’s perspective is usually shaped by their priorities, and those - in turn - are shaped by what they need or want. Writing from a different perspective can actually be quite fun. In historical fiction (which is what I write) you have to do it all the time, as the past is fundamentally different from the present.

While a lot of writers draw on their own experiences, etc., it’s not really necessary if you know your craft. It can actually be interesting to learn about something you’ve never done. Today, of course, there are all sorts of resources you can use to do this. Videos can be a big help if you can drum up a good one. You can also do the thing yourself (or go to the place) if you have the chance.

Oh this one's easy. Have them repeatedly do things that seemingly make no sense with no explanation or apology. Then, when the time comes to do the reveal, explain it.

The first example of this that came to mind was the second episode of Matt Smith's run on Doctor Who. They land on a ship and the Doctor is doing what he usually does, gawking at everything around him and generally being excitable. And then he picks up a glass, sets it on the floor, and stares at it for a few seconds before hopping up and running off to the next distraction without missing a beat. Then, later in the episode, he does the same thing again.

In context it's easy to dismiss this behavior as just another bit of irrational exuberance from a character who is frequently more than a little odd. But then, later in the episode, he points out what he's actually looking at: the water. Specifically he's looking for the ripples one would expect to be created by the ship's engines transferring vibration to the rest of the structure as they run. But there are no ripples, which means the “ship" isn't actually a ship. And you suddenly realize that the “irrational exuberance" wasn't just the character being weird, but him immediately catching on to something being wrong and doing his best to investigate without panicking his companion.

The trick to actually pulling this off is maintaining the proper balance. The example wouldn't have worked if the Doctor had only done it once, because the detail wouldn't have stuck in our minds. You need to repeat the action at least two or three times to establish it as a significant detail. However it also wouldn't have worked if it was over-exposed. If the only thing that we'd seen the character do upon arriving was checking the glass, the audience would not have initially dismissed it. They'd have been primed to expect the explanation, and when one wasn't immediately offered they'd know that there was a twist incoming. You need to figure out how to bury these clues as to the character's motivations within other actions that are perhaps more appropriate for a given context.

How you approach this will depend mostly on the kind of character you're writing. The Doctor is an easy one because anyone who's seen the show knows that they're rather goofy and even occasionally childish, so random behavior is normalized. But not every character can pull off random acts of whimsy. You may need to establish other rituals for them early on, or combine the idiosyncratic acts with ones that are more immediately explicable, such as actually drinking the water.

Imagination and some empathy. You don't have to agree with every other person in the world. In fact, it isn't possible. You live with that fact. Everyone does.

But you can try to comprehend their attitudes and treat them with politeness and possibly sympathy. Even if some of their beliefs strike you as dead wrong, it's unlikely you have to change their mind. Many disagreements aren't worth a fight, so you only argue when it matters enough to you.

Having said all that, you imagine the thought process and beliefs of your character. Maybe they're an atheist and you're religious. They work for a villain or are the villain and you can't approve of their behavior or ideology. You don't have to believe the way they do. You try to understand and to express their thoughts as they would.

Some things would probably demand research, too. I can't imagine writing a character from a culture I'm unfamiliar with and working from clichés. The odds are I'd insult somebody without wanting to. The same can apply to anything you haven't experienced. Not being devout, I'd want to understand a religion's mindset and rules before depicting anyone who lived that faith. I'm not married, never have been, not gay or a survivor of abuse. Some research would at least give me an intellectual framework to imagine the character who's experienced what I'm trying to imagine.

The latest, for me, was realizing no one had figured out ‘posting’. This is where you rise one stride and sit the next. It makes both the trot AND the canter/gallop much easier on the rider and the horse. Even more than standing in the stirrups, which puts uniform pressure on the horse’s back.

Now, almost no one posts the canter except in polo. It's a weird thing, because most people travel by horseback at the trot. That's the most efficient ‘normal’ equine gait. So I keep my mouth shut and move on.

It's not worth triggering the ‘Centipede’s Dilemma’ in authors I enjoy.

As the other answers indicate, it takes some research. It helps if you’re interested in lots of subjects.

The thing is, readers don’t want research and data, they want a story. You don’t need to become a technology whiz to write about Steve Jobs (although that wouldn’t hurt). However, you do need to learn enough to know what is within the realm of the possible, or you can ask someone who would know.

With unfamiliar subjects, look for the Telling Detail. This is a writing technique that brings your story to life. Rather than impress your reader with a blizzard of indecipherable technobabble, boil it down to one thing that marries your narrative to the field under discussion. Continuing the Jobs example, suppose you’re writing about a small-business owner who is considering an offer of purchase by Apple. What product does that company make? Why is Apple interested in it? What does the product do? If the product sounds believable, then the company you build around it will sound believable.

In the end, though, a story is about people. Give your audience authentic characters with authentic problems, and the technical stuff will fade into the background. Get it right, but don’t obsess over it.

First order of business is to craft a compelling character— villain, anti-hero, or otherwise. At the end of the day, your entire premise (at least, from what you’ve given us) is riding on the “cool-factor” that comes with writing a story about a villain. The thing is, nobody is going to care about reading a book from the villain’s perspective if the villain is bland as my mother’s cooking (sorry mom).

Figure out what type of villain you’re writing about. A superhero-esque villain (Joker, Magneto, Green Goblin, etc.)? A fantasy-type villain (Sauron, Voldemort, Hades etc.)? A gothic villain? Victorian era? Horror? Science fiction? Thriller? It’s all for you to choose. Pick one, and use as much about that type of villain as you can to your advantage. Try immersing yourself into the sort of genre that your villain hails from, and really getting a feel for it.

Also, make sure that your villain has layers. Make them feel relatable, and likable. Don’t get me wrong— they can be the most horrid being imaginable. But, you should probably make the villain have some likable elements, if we’re going to be with them for the entirety of a novel (sharp wit, clever thinking, sense of humor, etc.). Give them a strong background too. Nothing too sappy (they’re irrationally evil, so try keeping that element consistent), but try making them have a compelling story.

My advice is that, if you wish to write a story purely about a villain, don’t insert whites. We see the “misunderstood villain” cliche all the time in fiction, who end up getting a happy ending (or a “tragic” ending), and I hate it. Why? Because, while the writer might think that they’re writing about a villain, they’re actually writing about an anti-hero. Writing about an anti-hero is fine. But, I’d prefer for the writer to be honest to themselves when they’re writing about an anti-hero. (Note: Don’t get me confused here. A villain can have a tragic backstory, and element s of humanity. It’s just— when a villain is “misunderstood, it typically means that their intent is pure. While a villain always thinks that they’re right, their intentions are rarely ever “pure”— many villains even know this, and unabashedly accept it.)

You can use tinges of grays, but I would only use those to humanize the villain, and make them feel real. I’d avoid trying to make the villain act heroic, unless it’s a fake-out that plays with the reader’s emotions (and the hero’s), or a “what could’ve been” type of thing. You can make the villain consider changing his sides— sure. But, I’d even be hesitant to do that (if this character is truly a villain, then they probably think that being evil is the right thing, in their own twisted sort of way).

I think that your idea could be an incredibly interesting and an intelligent way to explore what the psyche of a classic villain might be. We all see the classic villain on the screen, or on the page, and we always wonder why they do some of the seemingly irrational things that they do— long-winded monologues, over-complicated plans, an overabundance of hate, and an obsession with the hero (if they hate the hero, why are they always so hesitant to kill the hero). For once, this is your chance to tell us why— to really show us how the villains tick. In a sense, villains represent humanity’s worst parts— there’s always something compelling to explaining the mind of the villain, since their mind oftentimes contains a fragment of our own minds.

The one thing that I will say: an entire novel might be hard to maintain. Normally, with this sort of story, I’d advise for you to write it in first person… Though, for a type character as one-note as this, that format typically is only rich for a short-story(see, “Tell-Tale Heart”). Constantly hearing the evil-villain think evilly will lose its novelty over the course of a novel. Instead, I suggest that you write the book in third person limited (please don’t use third-person omniscient), or first person perspective from a third party (maybe the villain’s protege). Even then, though, I still think you should try keeping the novel sparse.

Throughout the book, it would be great to see the villain make interesting choices, and then you give us blurbs of what they’re thinking— how they’re feeling. Try to keep what they’re thinking minimal enough, but not so minimal that the character becomes distant and cardboard. The thing about villains is that they often work best with a certain veil of mystery. If you can manage to keep that sense of mystery with your villain, the villain should come across as all the more intimidating, menacing, and intriguing.

Finally: I say that you should make the villain win and lose, at the same time. If there’s anything that we’ve always wanted, it’s to see the villain win— just once. Honestly, villains have the worst luck out of all the characters in fiction. Is karma really that effective? I wish karma was as attentive to me as it is to villains…

The thing is, though, even when a villain wins, they lose. Why? Because, what they want is irrational. Kill the hero! All your talent, and that’s the best that you could come up with? Take over the world! Then what happens when the world revolts, Einstein? Ultimate knowledge! The very fact that you think there’s such a thing as “ultimate knowledge shows me that you’re undeserving of the pursuit of knowledge… Rob banks to a mass a lot of wealth! Good luck escaping the IRS… Immortality! Come back to me in 14 trillion years, and tell me how immortality is going for you…

At the end of the day, the villain’s goal is always hollow. In a sense, they reflect the aspirations of humanity. once we accomplish our goals, what have we really done? The universe remains indifferent to our actions… And so, the villain, with all of his sharp wit and malice, will eventually have to see that it gains him nothing. The villain will never admit it though… They’d probably shoot themselves with their own death-ray before ever doing something like that.

Good luck on your novel. Hope to see you on the bestseller’s list!

Research and education provides novelists with the background to write about things they don’t fully understand. Many scientists do not understand the full repercussions of their work on society; they might know what it proves or disproves or how it fits into the support of an idea, but not necessarily how it affects a community who uses it, or for those who don’t use it. For instance, there are scientists who said if they had known what the effect on the world of nuclear weapons meant, they would have never worked on the problem.
