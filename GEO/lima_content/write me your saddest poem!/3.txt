Before I share it: I really hate writing poetry, because of the vicious cycle of “I write - I read - I hate”.

I am like kids when they mess up their plate: they deeply enjoy mixing the gravy with the peas, the peas with lingonberry jam, the jam with crumbles of bread and mashed potatoes… But then they don’t want to eat it because it’s gross. I do the same: I have fun but I realize that what comes out of it is cloddish.

Poetry needs more in-depth study than any other form of art, and I am still a beginner. Someday my mish-mash will turn into a ratatouille, but the day I wrote what I am about to share was not that day.

OK it sounds HORRIBLE in English. Sigh. Anyway.

I wrote this for my former best friend. We were like Chip ’n’ Dale, always together, drifting through life and attending poetry readings, literature classes and writing essays together. He was the light of my life, often the only thinking side of my intoxicated brain. And I was like a wave of red and violet paint thrown on grey wanna-be-academic life. We were the perfect match.

Then somehow, in a week’s time, everything collapsed.

He broke up with his girlfriend of 10 years out of the blue, and developed a weird rebound crush on me. Instead of coming clean, he just stopped talking to me and left. Meanwhile, I was dealing with eating disorders, self-harm and attempts at poetry in English (three dangerous pastimes) after my teachers told me that they didn’t see any future for me in academia. Too random, incapable to network.

A month after, he successfully applied for a master degree and a job at the Italian Embassy.

He contacted me to share the good news, he apologized for his coldness and invited me out to dinner. I accepted, but I knew things weren’t going to be the same ever again. So I cried my eyes out and wrote this half-assed piece to have some closure (I guess). Went to dinner, talked, small-talked. Yeah. As I had imagined.

Now we talk from time to time. It’s nice, but I still feel part of my heart is with the shy-but-cheeky Teo I met in the library’s basement.

So, you think you’ve drafted a tweet, an email, a short story, or even a novel. These are different forms of communication, but the process of bringing them to fruition has a necessary, sometimes overlooked step: editing! Unless you’re a professional writer, it’s unlikely that you have an editor who can review your writing regularly. Here are some tips to help you review your own work.

1.   Give your writing some space. Have you ever felt a mix of pure relief and joy when you’ve finished a draft of something? Don’t downplay that feeling and the ability to walk away from your work before you start editing it. You may need minutes, hours, or days, but once you sit back down with what you originally had on the page, you’ll have the thrill of looking at it with fresh eyes. You’ll notice errors you may not have seen the first time. You’ll come to new realizations about its overall tone and structure. If it’s a text or email, maybe you only need a few minutes away from it. If it’s a story or essay, perhaps you’ll need longer. Regardless of what type of work it is, it will help your writing tremendously.
2.   Don’t use overachieving synonyms. Looking at your work for the second, third, or fourth time around may inspire you to spice up your language with longer, more uncommon words. There’s nothing wrong with having a thesaurus nearby, but try to limit the repetition of long, pretentious-feeling words so your work flows well and doesn’t feel too bogged down. At the end of the day, you want it to feel true to you and the message you’re conveying.
3.   Remember who the reader is. Don’t forget your own voice as the writer—but don’t forget who your reader is. Many writers get too close to their work; editing is a chance to try to get out of your own head. Who is your ideal reader? What do you want them to take away from the writing? It’s a unique time to step in their shoes, to make sure your communication is as effective as you’d like it to be.
4.   Kill your darlings. Don’t be scared to remove chunks of your work, even if it feels precious to you. If it’s a passage that’s really tough to part with, try saving it somewhere else, so you can return to it later in your piece or for another work.
5.   Use Grammarly. Last but not least, Grammarly has countless resources for editing your work. Our writing assistant helps you find areas of your writing that are unclear or too wordy, as well as help you find mistakes you might not have caught.

Editing may feel tedious, but it’s just as important as writing itself. For an extra pair of editing eyes on everything you write, download the free Grammarly for Windows and Mac today.

The Wandering Outcast

I am lost in the dark shadows,

I tread deep into the shady meadows,

My mind is plagued with deep thoughts,

Difficult to ingest this paradox.

Trapped in my soul’s decadence,

I am disturbed as a consequence,

Possessed by a never-ending haunting,

My spirit is breaking down from within.

I wish for this heaviness to end,

But this sorrow is difficult to mend.

The uneasiness is growing in my blood,

Can anyone save me from this flood?

Trapped in this cage of nothingness,

I am slowly descending into the darkness,

This anxiety fills up my skull,

Everything around me seems to dull.

Sunken eyes, deafening silence, and dried lips,

Signs of tears lie upon my cheeks.

The void in my heart begins to expand,

Is salvation too much to demand?

Stuck in this time loop of distress,

And the turbulent notions difficult to suppress,

Aloof, I approach eternal slumber,

No echoes, no whispers left to remember

But somewhere faint, a flicker glows,

A breath of warmth the darkness knows.

No promises, no sudden light—

Just strength to rise and face the night.
