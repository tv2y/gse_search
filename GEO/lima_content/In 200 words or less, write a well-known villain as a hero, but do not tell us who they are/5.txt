My favorite villain of all time, Euron Greyjoy.

Ironically, George R. R. Martin, who probably popularized the idea that the best villains are the heroes of their own stories, created one of the most loathsome characters in fiction. Even in the world of Westeros, Euron is a monster. He is under no illusions about his villainy, seeing himself as a ubermensch, antichristlike figure, the breaker of the world, the bringer of a new, dark age. He positively revels in the destruction he causes, as can be seen with his most infamous line: “From Ibb to Asshai, when men see my sails, they pray.” He murdered and molested his brothers just to prove to himself that he is above the Drowned God. He seduced a teenage girl just to sacrifice his unborn child with her. He revels in the torment and fear he inspires. He doesn't see himself as a hero; he is long past caring for trifling matters such as heroism and villainy.

And yet, in a series with such masterfully crafted villains as Tywin Lannister, Littlefinger, Cersei, and Joffrey, all of whom see themselves as the hero, I find Euron Greyjoy the most compelling villain, not just written by Martin, but ever. Why?

Two reasons. First, a controversial statement: everyone who doesn't see at least a little bit of themselves in Euron is lying to themselves. Euron represents the evil every one of us has within us taken to the most absurd extremes. The fundamental reason for his evil is that being evil makes him feel powerful. That informs everything he does, from his murder of his brothers to his ravages as a pirate. He loves feeling above the Gods. He loves feeling feared.

I don’t know about you, but I’ve certainly felt that way. As a kid, stomping on anthills, I felt powerful watching the ants run away. Really, the only difference between that and what Euron does is that Euron doesn't make an exception for humans. That’s a line I would never cross… or is it, the back of my mind whispers. The evil of Twin Lannister, the evil of Joffrey Baratheon, the evil of Littlefinger- these evils were born of the specific circumstances of these characters. That makes them more interesting, but less relatable. The darkness in Euron Greyjoy is the darkness in all of us, and that’s why I love him.

Secondly: Euron Greyjoy is, for lack of a better term, the best written Fascist ever. He lives in a society where the conditions for Fascism do not exist, and yet ultimately he gets to the core of what the Fascist sentiment is better than any other character I’ve read. Palpatine, Saruman, and especially not Voldemort- no one comes close.

Euron is King of the Iron Islands, but he didn’t inherit his throne, he had to win it in an election. The Ironborn, his people, used to be powerful and respected, ruling over the Riverlands. That died when the Targaryens came. Now all they rule are some crappy, infertile rocks. They are the worst place to live in Westeros, bar none. And Euron comes along promising to change that. He promises that the Ironborn will be great again, that under him they will conquer all of Westeros. It’s a lie, of course. Euron just needs an army to get him to Oldtown, before he can take whatever he needs. But the Ironborn believe him because what other option do they have? They cling to the one man offering them hope.

Euron is the only Fascist I’ve read where I empathize with his followers. Voldemort is served by rich, racist assholes, Palpatine by faceless Stormtroopers, but Euron is served by the men who make all dictatorships: desperate fools, grasping at straws. If you read one chapter from ASOIAF, make it The Forsaken(also featuring Euron) but if you read two, make the second the chapter where Euron is elected King of the Iron Islands. The speech Martin wrote for him made me empathize with the Ironborn because I myself felt swayed by it. Euron shows, more than any other villain I’ve read, why people follow evil leaders.

Euron Greyjoy scares me. He scares me when I find myself cheering for him as he commits his atrocities. He scares me as I wonder if I would have followed him as easily as the Ironborn did. It’s what this irredeemably, unashamedly evil villain reveals about ourselves that to me makes him so compelling.

Why do we like villains more than heroes?

As a kid, I found myself frequently loving the villains.

For probably these reasons:

1.   They were outcasts. Society rejected them, people hated them, but despite all this, they were still able to impose their will on the world. In real life, outcasts are usually powerless, but these villains subverted that expectation. You’ve got to be pretty brilliant to succeed in a world that hates you.
2.   I had a default soft spot for outcasts, since I felt like one myself. The more others hated them, the more I liked them. In hindsight it’s probably because I wanted to prove to myself that no matter how hated you are by the world, someone will find a way to love you. *shrugs*
3.   For my favourite villains, it’s because their motivations are very “pure” and rooted in ideals, things like trying to destroy an unjust world. Underlying their villainy is a fallen idealist. At one point, they expected better from human nature but became so completely disillusioned that they turned dark. 

Or they want revenge for a loved one being unjustly taken away from them. In this case, their extremely deep love manifested into a lifelong quest for vengeance. Most people can just forget about it and move on, but their love is so deep that they literally can’t let it go, to the point of dysfunction. The ones I found most compelling are the ones who lost their family at an early age. Or mothers who will go ape shit for their kids (looking at you Cersei).

Or some of them just want to fuck with people for the lols. The complete irreverence towards a life which many of us take too seriously, is kind of refreshing.
4.   They are intelligent. They understand the systems they are navigating to attain their aims, and they understand the people who they’re manipulating to do their bidding. Brutes who impulsively bust into buildings shooting willy nilly are much less interesting than masterminds who are calculating every step of the way. I liked the fact that they were in control of their chosen sphere.
5.   When they ultimately get defeated, their flaws are made even more apparent. We get to see actual weakness. Seeing vulnerability in a supposedly powerful and in-control character is really endearing. It’s kind of like seeing a tough cookie finally crack a genuine smile. This one is just a personal preference. ;)

Why? To give the villain the edge. To create a sense of dread and suspense. A feeling of

“Oh no. How will Thor beat this villain now?”

The victory can’t be a straightforward one for Thor. He needs to do something extra.

Thor was forced to think outside the box and use Surtur to defeat Hela.

Which sounds more interesting than Thor beating Hela without any difficulty, if you ask me.

* * *

And when a hero beats the villain after being on the verge of losing, the pay-off is much greater.

Like in the Guardians of the Galaxy: Vol 2, when all hope was lost

Peter gets angry enough to summon his true power and helps stop Ego.

That was a great moment imo.

* * *

Imagine if the villain was not good enough to beat the hero…

End of the story. Roll credits.

* * *

For a good story/fight, there has to a low and a high. It can’t be all high. Then the story is just predictable and boring for most people.

Thor one-shots a Kronan. That was anti-climactic if you ask me.

If we know the hero can’t be beaten, what’s the fun in watching them crush all the villains with ease. Where’s the tension? Where’s the uncertainty? There is none. It can get boring after a while.

That’s why it is better for the story/movie for the villain to be “better” than the hero in some way.
