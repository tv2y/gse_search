For context - I wrote an award winning novel that was funny, light-hearted and somewhat realistic. So I speak from experience.

Reality is hysterically funny. So keeping things realistic is the easy part.

It helps do know what the core strengths of your comedy style is. Do you write funny dialogue? Can you create side-splitting scenes? Do you pun like a nun?

Know what isn't funny. Sure, humour is subjective. But some things are just cringe worthy and not funny at all. Baby in a blender jokes for example.

Comedy can have a serious message to it. Sir Terry Pratchett's books were realistic fantasy humour. Sure the Discworld was flat, and had gods and stuff. But it was a recognisable facsimile of Britain in many of its character archetypes and scenes. While delivering some very funny stories, Pratchett also managed to deliver important social messages too.

You don't have to preach social awareness, you can just make jokes.

Mix up the comedy. I use all kinds of jokes. Some are silly names (my protagonist was called Charlotte Pudding) interesting ideas (a religion based on Quantum Physics) acceptable but odd realities (Charlotte Pudding was a computer psychologist - she worked with people to help them achieve better relationships with their computer equipment).

Things that are bizarre - Charlottte's world ran on empathic energy - which meant that if you said kind words to your car, it would run better and your toaster might have a psychotic break and destroy your breakfast.

Keeping it realistic means taking a Monty Python style absurdity and applying it to a recognisable framework (yes there are cars, telephones, and the internet in the world - but they don't use fossil fuels, they use empathic energy as a form of electricity).

Things that are funny may be seen as completely normal to people in the story - Charlotte wasn't fazed by seeing a person taking their television for a walk. Even when the TV's leash had gotten tangled around a lamp post and everyone involved were constantly apologising to each other.

View the world through a cracked lens. Alice in Wonderland - completely madcap. The world follows a clear set of rules (gravity works, cars drive (or fly), animals talk (or don't talk) people go to work, pay taxes, drink coffee, but the barista at their local coffee shop is a conjoined twin.

For further reading - Jasper Fforde's books are great examples of comedy that has realistic elements.

And you can check Amazon for Engines of Empathy for my attempt.

Edit: Golden rule. If you find yourself giggling, laughing, or smiling at what you write, then it's probably good.

Write your first draft however you need to get it down. Seriously, if you can avoid backspacing to fix typos, do that, too.

You’re going to change the tone in the revisions…once you have had at least a night’s sleep between the first draft and the revising. This is arbitrary but helpful, since the space of time allows you and your brain to approach it better “from outside the author’s POV”—even though you are the author, the you that wrote it is now a different writer, because you’re now reading what “serious-first-draft-you” has completed the job.

As you read through, embrace the passages that make you uncomfortable because they feel too serious, stuffy, self-involved, or simply are too descriptive and tone-setting while departing from action. Underline them, bracket them, whatever. These are specific passages you want to rethink because you’ve gone too far with them, and that is a contributor to why your tone “gains weight.”

PLEASE, PLEASE, PLEASE: don’t criticize first-draft-writer-you. That writer is not responsible for the final product, and FDWY is the “miner,” finding and producing content from the convolutions in your mind. THAT IS FDWY’s only job, and if you try to make FDWY do the refining processes that will take the raw materials and turn them into gems and treasures, you’re asking your most loyal dog to meow. And then drive you to the Pulitzers.

Now is the job for the punch-up team. This is where you try out what you have written on different points of view. If you have other characters—they don’t even need to be in the same body of work!—“ask” them to share their take on the ideas in the passages.

If you find your characters all tend to agree with you, that means you’re actually an essayist…I’m kidding. That’s funny to me because I write a LOT of essays, and they all tend to be self-serving because they are essentially monologues: my own, unfiltered, unshared moods. And I know FDWY needs to be protected from your self criticism because I already yelled at FDW(me) for a long time, and that is why I have so much unedited, monotonous work yet to revisit. We have since been to therapy together, and it has been highly productive.

Seriously—if your characters agree with you, it may just mean the topic is too close to you, and you need to let go of it. In literature it is ONLY an idea, because you are just writing. Your words, thanks to the way of the world, are going to be interpreted a bunch of ways you didn’t intend, because your words now stand on their own.

This said, “2DWY” is the researcher/interviewer/analyst. 2DWY and 3DWY tend to work together (which 3DWY doesn’t mind at all, because it is not a separate entity but a separate step. Everybody wins!). Send those crazy kids out to:

* surf a little to see what others think about the same topic (like I read other people’s Quora responses before wasting either of our time writing the same things with the same POV and tone). Making fiction funny and lighthearted while retaining realism requires controlling tone, pacing, character truth, and stakes. The goal is to let humor arise naturally from believable people and situations rather than forcing jokes. Below are focused strategies and practical techniques you can apply immediately.

1.   Anchor humor in character

- Give characters clear desires, flaws, and consistent perspectives—humor springs from how real characters respond to things, not from gag lines.
2.   Keep stakes real but manageable

- Low-to-medium stakes (social embarrassment, ruined plans, workplace mishaps) allow sustained lightness. High stakes can have comic moments but need grounding with genuine consequences.
3.   Favor situational and character-based humor over gag-after-gag

- Situational comedy: the humor arises from situation dynamics—miscommunication, timing, physical awkwardness. Build scenes where the situation itself produces laughs.
4.   Use contrast and tone control

- Mix normalcy and absurdity: present ordinary details sincerely, then introduce a slightly offbeat element. The contrast creates humor without breaking realism.
5.   Dialogue as comedy engine

- Short beats, interruptions, and subtext produce comic timing. Characters saying the opposite of what they mean (sarcasm, understatement) can be very funny when rooted in real motives.
6.   Timing, rhythm, and sentencecraft

- Vary sentence length for comic beats: quick short sentences for punchlines, longer sentences for setup.
7.   Show, don’t tell the funny

- Demonstrate absurdity through concrete sensory detail and action rather than labeling something “ridiculous.” Concrete images stick and read as more realistic.
8.   Use irony, understatement, and misdirection

- Irony: allow outcomes to contradict expectations in believable ways.
9.   Keep empathy at the center

- Characters should remain sympathetic even when foolish. Readers laugh with them, not at them. Avoid humiliation that feels mean-spirited unless you intend darker satire.
10.   Layer realism into comic elements

- Research details that ground scenes (workplace procedures, family dynamics, small cultural specifics). Real textures make comic departures more believable.
11.   Edit for comedic economy

- Cut anything that dilutes the setup or repeats the joke.
12.   Read and imitate with analysis

- Study lighthearted writers (P.G. Wodehouse for structural comedy; Terry Pratchett for satirical warmth; Ottessa Moshfegh or Helen Fielding for different comic voices) and dissect how they build setup, timing, and character-based payoff. Copy rhythms in practice exercises, then adapt to your voice.
13.   Practical exercises

- Rewrite a dramatic scene with one character reacting comically but realistically—retain stakes, change tone.
14.   When to allow broader absurdity

- If the premise permits (magical realism, heightened farce), maintain internal rules: once you establish a world logic, let humor exploit it consistently so it still feels “real” within that world.

Summary checklist before final pass:

*   Are jokes rooted in character and desire? 
*   Do consequences exist and feel earned? 
*   Is the tone consistent and not announcing humor? 
*   Are setups specific and payoffs economical?

Applying these controls produces writing that is light, genuinely funny, and credible—humor emerges organically because the characters and situations behave like people and worlds readers recognize.

The problem isn’t that you don’t have any ideas. The problem is that you are trying to come up with an idea that is original. Unfortunately, there is no such thing as an original idea for a book. Every idea is based in some way on something that has come before it. We all stand on the shoulders of our predecessors.

Instead, write about what makes you curious, what makes you ask, “What if?” Then, even if it is something that has been written about before, you will bring your own unique perspective to it because you bring with you a unique life full of unique experiences and perspectives with which to color your story. Your perspective is therefore unique.

The next step is to harvest those ideas until one jumps out at you. Keep an idea journal. Use it to write down anything and everything that inspires you. Free-write, with no concern of grammar or style, or even quality. Just write anything that comes to mind. Write down news stories that pique your curiosity. Write down snippets of conversations you overhear, or about a particular painting you saw that made you feel something.

Write about your life. Keep a diary as well.

Do this religiously. In time, you will go through it and find things that start your mind churning. If you can’t stop thinking about an idea, start free-writing about it and see where it leads you.

Often, writers fall victim to the Paradox of Choice, where there are so many different things to write about that you instead don’t write at all. If this is your problem, use writing prompts to get you started. You don’t necessarily have to turn that into a story, but it may inspire you to write another one. Sometimes it is as easy as just starting to write anything. Or, choose a subject at random (i.e., robots) and free-write about it for a bit. If you restrict your choices, you’ll find that your brain starts to try to think outside the box it has been given.

There are loads of other ways to get ideas to write about. Good luck.

Write on.

I’m just now reading one of P.J. Wodehouse’s Jeeves and Wooster novels, and it is hilarious. Nobody really cracks any jokes, but the situations are somewhat absurd while remaining believable, and everyone is utterly serious. Bertie Wooster is the narrator and evidently holds himself in high regard, while Jeeves the butler, while subservient to him, is clearly the real brains. Wooster’s misestimation of his own competence, combined with his stodgy views on master/servant dynamics, cause him to get himself into seemingly inextricable situations that just grow worse. I highly recommend checking out the books. I downloaded a collection in one thick volume from Amazon; I believe the first book in the set is Right Ho, Jeeves. (They’re very English, as one might well guess, don’t you know.)

An example of something that’s supposed to be funny that falls flat would be (and I hope fans of Rodney Dangerfield will forgive me) the movie Back to School. Potentially funny premise, and Kurt Vonnegut’s two seconds on-screen is probably the best part (I won’t spoil it for you) - but there are bits where Dangerfield starts ad-libbing. What he’s saying would be hilarious if he were on-stage. But he does this at times when other characters are supposed to be annoyed with him - and they start grinning and laughing. They follow up with their complaints about what he’s doing (as far as the story goes); but clearly, they enjoyed his comedy riffs. I felt like I was watching the outtakes.

Essentially, comedy should be played straight. Robin Williams was good at ad-libbing, and Oscar Levant (great pianist and raconteur) did spectacular ad-libbing in movies that’s so deadpan you’d never notice except that it’s so much funnier than everything else. Note, though, that the characters don’t laugh at what they say or do - the audience does. So should the reader.

Also, it helps if the stakes are quite low. One can write a comedy about the end of the world, but it’s hard to keep that lighthearted. Avoid death and illness unless it’s handled with utmost discretion. We need to care about the protagonist, but not so much that we’re devastated when things don’t work out. Look up, for example, the short story “Love Is a Fallacy” by Max Schulman. We follow his protagonist’s earnest efforts to get the girl, but in the end (and I won’t spoil it), it’s his own conceited attitude, and a slight miscalculation, that bring about his downfall.

There’s “realism” and there’s “authenticity”. And they are very different in fictional worldbuilding.

Realistic means the place in your story might be fictional, but could exist in reality. It’s economy, science, society, culture, etc. are all based on our own world. The characters and settings feel “real” as in, you could visit this place and meet these people.

Authentic worldbuilding means your world might not exist in reality, but is well thought out with its own consistent internal logic. The key is to figure out the internal logic of your world and make sure it is consistent throughout. In this case, the characters and world feel “real” as in, if you accept the principles of the world, everything makes sense.

Say, your world has intelligent animals on the same level as humans.

Then you need to figure out the internal logic for these smart animals’ behavior with each other and with humans.

*   Do they wear clothing? Why? They already have fur to protect them from elements. If they wear clothing, what’s the purpose of such clothing? Is it shame of their own body parts that drove them to cover it up? Why is the human concept of “shame” a thing for these animals?
*   Do they use utensils? How? They have paws. Did they invent “paw” specific utensils? Suppose they didn’t use utensils. In their dealings with humans, would humans looked down upon them because eating with your snout looks “uncivilized”?
*   How do different animal species treat each other? Are they equally intelligent, or are some species smarter than others?

These (amongst many others) are the questions you need to ask yourself and provide answers to. And you need to make these answers consistent with your worldbuilding logic.

And it is these consistencies that make your world, no matter how ridiculous, authentic. It creates the feeling that this world could exist, based on the principles you provided.

For example, say you want to write a story about humans riding dinosaurs while shooting AK-47s. In order for this setting to work, you would need to have an explanation for how dinosaurs could co-exist with AK-47s. The reason doesn’t matter really, it could be scientific experimentation (like Jurassic Park), time travel, or magic. But once you’ve decided which reason you’re going with, you must stick with it, and apply it to the rest of your world consistently.

Suppose your explanation is: “People used magic to revive dinosaurs from bones.”

Ok, once revived, do dinosaurs behave like living creatures with their own minds or like zombies? Does this magic work on other types of bones? Does it work on humans? If it works on humans, it would come to reason that the death rate in your world would be relatively low because people can revive their dead loved ones with magic. And it would come to reason that your world wouldn’t have enough resources to feed all these humans.

Now why would the people revive dinosaurs? They aren’t particularly smart, they’re not domesticated, and there are better animals to revive.

All that is internal consistency. This is something you need to figure out before you start your story. Because internal consistency often drives your story; it creates obstacles and solutions.

When you have a world with internal consistency, it might be unrealistic, but it is authentic.

If you don't have enough to write about, then you either don't have enough conflict, or you are telling instead of showing your story.

Great stories take your protagonist and put them through something that will cause them to be uncomfortable, grow and change for the better because of that conflict. Conflict can be internal or external, or even better: both! For example, your story could be a couple going on a blind date. Okay. But what if only *one* of the people on the date actually wants a relationship? Maybe the man is still hurt from being dumped by his ex and has vowed to never fall for a woman again (Internal conflict). You can layer an external conflict: maybe he's attracted to her, despite his determination to avoid a relationship, and he needs the date to go well because he needs a date for his sister's wedding. In fact, maybe he already told his parents he was engaged, just to get them off his back. The possibilities are endless when you just keep adding conflict.

What's the worst thing that could happen to your character, because of the way he, specifically, is? Make that thing happen, and make your character stronger for having gone through it.

The other thing you may be doing, is you may be *telling* the reader what happens (as I did above, in a summary fashion) instead of *showing* us. "Show, don't tell" when you're writing a story to make it feel as if the reader is experiencing what is happening, instead of just hearing about what happened after the fact.

Example of telling: "She drank a cup of coffee with her breakfast, then walked out the door."

Example of showing: "The bitter taste of her coffee was a welcome contrast to the sweet pancake on her fork. She'd probably need to cut down on the pancakes if she wanted to fit in to the same swimsuit she bought last summer, though. She took another bite, licking the maple syrup off her fork. Mmmm. Maybe a few extra pounds didn't matter, after all. Who could give up the breakfast of champions?"

I can write a synopsis of a 75,000 word book in only one page (and have done so many times—not fun, but sometimes necessary for marketing teams or publishers) by telling in summary what happens, which is the opposite of what you do when you are writing the actual book and showing what happens as it unfolds.

If you add conflict and show, don't tell, you should have no problem writing for as long as you need to.

By the way – 3000 words in one sitting is great. I often call it a day after I write 2K-4K. It's perfectly reasonable to set your story aside after you get 3K in, and start again the next day where you left off. Some people find it helpful to write an outline before they start writing their's book (or story) so they know what should come next.

Good luck with your writing!

I do not see a contradiction between funny style and serious matters. Terry Pratchett would be my prime example of how to take the world and explain some of its most serious mechanisms in a way that the reader cannot stop laughing. Basic human nature, ethnic clashes, changing societies, philosophy and tolerance all written in a “funny style”.

Your style is part of what you are. If you try to erase your personal note, why should anybody read your text instead of that of an other person? But actively trying to copy the stile of other people can be quite educating too. Just don’t get so used to it that you do it by mistake and don’t underestimate your personal note - an original piece of crap has the potential of being way more interesting than a death-serious enumeration of facts and conclusions. If you loose interest yourself, why shouldn’t your reader?

But if you believe that the “funny” part of your style is somewhat a hinderance to the quality of the lecture, you can attempt to do what I do abot the weaknesses in my writings:

When I write I do stages. First putting all down that comes to my mind. Then I take a break and later I revision it, rework it if needed and polish it somewhat. But often the rework sucks in terms of atmosphere, sharpness and depth, while the original fails in logic, has too many typos and sometimes breaks my characters for situations sake. So I go back to the original, compare it with the rework and create another version. That way I sometimes create a piece of text that I am satisfied with.

tldr: split up the work into creative production and quality assurance.

The best answer is the simplest. Look around you. Observe people as they go about their daily routines. Detectives become aware of human behaviors because they pay attention to even the smallest detail.

A portly man buys a cup of coffee at a coffee shop around late afternoon and has a seat. You have already learned two things. He is having a late coffee which means he may be working late and this is probably his second of third cup today. He sat down which means he has nowhere he has to be or he is waiting for some one.

Observe further. He is dressed in an oversized jacket zipped up. The shop has mild air conditioning and outside it is comfortably warm. He bought a hot coffee but did not need the cardboard protector. What you can garner is his man is not comfortable in his appearance today. He feels the need to cover up instead of making himself comfortable to have his coffee. He also may have ordered his coffee with too much milk, making it easy to carry.

As the man walked to his chair, he glanced to the snacks. He sat down with a down expression and sighed. He raised the cup slightly and stared at it in his hand before setting it down again. His expression is unchanged. I surmise this man is on a diet and but he still wants to enjoy a simple coffee without a sweet. So why did his expression remain unchanged. I suspect he is unhappy with his choice because of the extra milk he used to ease his sweet tooth.

He brings the coffee to his lips, stops for a moment and then drinks. He appears pleased for a moment, but soon returns to his saddened disposition. He turns to face away from the crowd, hunches forward slightly folding his arms between the table and himself. This man knows he made a bad decision. His trepidation before he took that gulp of milk filled coffee. The signs of mild worry as he tries to shrink into his own space says he wants to be unnoticed.

Final evaluation: This man is lactose intolerant and on a low sugar diet to lose the weight he is not comfortable in showing. His sad expression is because this is a guilty pleasure and he is experiencing shame which is only being exacerbated by the knowledge of what is about to happen in his belly.

I just did that about a stranger sitting on the other side of this coffee shop. I will hopefully leave before the air fills with SBD, but know this before I go. Even if I'm wrong, I just came up with an entire behavioral makeup by just being casually conscious of the world around me and you can too.
