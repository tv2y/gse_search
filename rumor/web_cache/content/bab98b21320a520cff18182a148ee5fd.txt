Calvin Coolidge, without a doubt.

Much like Reagan would do six decades later, Coolidge used the bully pulpit to change the perception of how government should work. In an era of big government tax-and-spend Presidents like Teddy Roosevelt, Woodrow Wilson, Herbert Hoover and FDR, Coolidge stood apart.

He cut taxes substantially on at least three occasions and appointed laissez-faire businessmen to government agencies like the Federal Trade Commission and Interstate Commerce Commission. The federal debt was reduced by one-fourth as state and local governments grew substantially and surpassed the federal budget.

Coolidge also vetoed farm subsidies, disdained federal intervention in flood control, spoke in favor of civil rights for blacks and Catholics and was wary of foreign entanglements.

Combining notes from libertarians Ivan Eland (author of Recarving Rushmore) and Brion McClanahan (author of 9 President who screwed up America and 4 who tried to save her) you get a list looking like this (most lists I have found seem to have a relative consensus on the first four in both constitutionality and policy).

1.   John Tyler: ranked as number 1 by both. Firm believer in states rights. Continually vetoed unconstitutional legislation. Avoided war with the British. Fought against the banks. Overall the historians have largely ranked him one of the worst due to the fact that he wasn’t an authoritarian war-monger and that he wasn’t harsh on slavery (he owned slaves himself and thought Liberia was a possible solution).
2.   Grover Cleveland: according to BM he was the last true conservative President. He was super anti-corruption, helped set our monetary policy in order, and was very anti-imperialistic. He was also Ron Paul’s favorite President.
3.   Martin Van Buren: according to IE allowed the market to readjust fairly quickly after the Panic of 1837, reduced public spending, balanced the budget, and avoided potential wars with Canada and Mexico.
4.   Calvin Coolidge: the last true President we ever had. He understood that the American Presidency really shouldn’t have all that much effect over an average person’s daily life. The more I read about him from his avoiding war with Mexico, to helping lower lynchings of African Americans, and to being one of the only Presidents who actually weakened the power of the Presidency, it’s hard to conclude this guy wasn’t the one of the best we ever had if not the best.
5.   Franklin Pierce: while given far too much blame for the Kansas Nebraska act, this has caused people to overlook the immense good he did. He had one of the least corrupt cabinets of any President, demolished the debt by over 80%, avoided war with Britain, and continully vetoed unconstitutional legislation.

Another top contender I’d say is Zachary Taylor with many lists having him ranked much higher than the Virginian DRs and is also considered one of the more constitutional Presidents by BM. However his Presidency was fairly short and his accomplishments were arguably not as lasting as the Virginia DRs.

James Monroe was also excellent and the most Jeffersonian of the Virginia dynast.

Republicans Grant, Hayes, Arthur, and Harding are also worth considering even if they didn’t have the staunch constitutionality the other aforementioned Presidents had.

For the worst we have

1.   Woodrow Wilson: authoritarian war-monger who got us the Federal reserve, introduced the national income tax, brought about multiple recessions, and started the rhetoric that viewed the constitution as an enemy of progress.
2.   Harry Truman: again an authoritarian war-monger who specifically said we were going to put international principles over American principles. To give him a little credit he was more or less following the path laid out by FDR but he nonetheless put America in a continual state of “being at war” that has existed to this day and even attempts at restrained foreign policy from Eisenhower, Carter, and Trump haven’t been enough to stop that.
3.   Abraham Lincoln: while ending slavery was indeed good, his actions for getting us to that point set a bad precedent of authoritarian power that Presidents since have followed.
4.   LBJ: unconstitutional war on poverty, wars in Vietnam and Laos, created the welfare state, and undid JFK’s work to get rid of the Federal Reserve before his body was even cold.
5.   FDR: attempted to pack the courts, expanded spending in a massive way, and lead us down the path we are today of being the world police (although Truman was the one who continued it).

Other top contenders are Bush Senior, Clinton, Bush Junior, and Obama. All were unconstitutional and each was more unconstitutional than the last, all got us into wars, and all expanded the power of the executive.

Another huge contender is John Tyler (rated number 1 in Ivan Eland’s ranking and considered the most constitutional by historical and constitutional scholars Brion McClanahan and Kevin Gutzman).

One name you’ll probably never here circulated but I personally think was one of the best was Franklin Pierce and you can see my rationale bellow.

BDM · 2y What do you think of Franklin Pierce? Very underrated and misunderstood President. Before serving he was actually extremely popular in New Hampshire and was known as a true Jeffersonian. His biographer even noted that he had the least corrupt cabinet of the era. Part of the demonization process of Pierce is both political and the fact that writing a biography on him is difficult due to limited material since he never really wrote a memoir or anything. Overall, he was a staunch constitutionalist (rated as one of the best Presidents in regards to the constitution by scholars Kevin Gutzman and Brion McClanahan), signed a reciprocity treaty with Canada, reduced the national debt by 83%, continually vetoed internal improvement bills that were unconstitutional, got them lowest tariff at that time passed, had a fairly benign foreign policy despite a British aggressions while also opposing filibustering, supported some expansion and desired to expand further to have the Jefferson’s vision of the “Empire of liberty,” and he was more of a statesman than a politician who acted on principle not popularity. He gave an eloquent and sophisticated speech rooted in founding principles that was over 3000 words and spoken all from memory. One of the quotes was: “But these are not the only points to which you look for vigilant watchfulness. The dangers of a concentration of all power in the general government of a confederacy so vast as ours are too obvious to be disregarded. You have a right, therefore, to expect your agents in every department to regard strictly the limits imposed upon them by the Constitution of the United States. The great scheme of our constitutional liberty rests upon a proper distribution of power between the State and Federal authorities, and experience has shown that the harmony and happiness of our people must depend upon a just discrimination between the separate rights and responsibilities of the States and your common rights and obligations under the General Government; and here, in my opinion, are the considerations which should form the true basis of future concord in regard to the questions which have most seriously disturbed public tranquillity. If the Federal Government will confine itself to the exercise of powers clearly granted by the Constitution, it can hardly happen that its action upon any question should endanger the institutions of the States or interfere with their right to manage matters strictly domestic according to the will of their own people.” He also shared the vision of Jefferson’s Empire of liberty, and wanted to expand the US not just to Cuba, but potentially to Canada (some scholars say this was his intention in regards to expanding trade there), Hawaii, and a portion of the Dominican Republic. Claims he was a white supremacist also don’t hold up well considering the treaties he negotiated with the Dominicans and Hawaii would’ve had their people be given full and equal citizenship as Americans. The reason however he has been so maligned (other than not being an authoritarian war-monger and actually valuing states rights) was bleeding Kansas. Here’s something that needs to be understood about Pierce. He HATED slavery. He thought it was immoral and rightly wanted it gone. “He had never seen a slave without being sick at heart. Slavery was contrary to the Constitution in some respects, and a blot upon the nation.” He even disdained the fugitive slave law not just on moral grounds but because it overrode state liberties. However he also strongly opposed the radical abolitionists. Why would he do something so seemingly contradictory? In his own words, “I am no advocate of slavery. I wish it had no existence upon the face of the Earth, but as a public man, I am called upon to act in relation to an existing state of things.... [T]he violent course of the Abolitionists at the North has postponed the emancipation of the coloured population in Maryland, Kentucky & Virginia Many & many a long year.... It should be remembered, that there are but two methods by which domestic slavery at the South can possibly be abolished. It can only be accomplished by the consent and agency of the Southern people themselves or by revolution. Would you recommend overturning the Constitution by a civil war, which carry ruin & desolation to every portion of this Country and probably result in the extermination of the coloured population upon this Continent?” Source: Gilder Lehrman Institute The thing is there was a difference back then between being anti-slavery and full on abolitionist. Quakers for example were staunchly anti-slavery and took many actions to eliminate it such as “owning” slaves in the South where it was basically known to everyone that their “slaves” lived as free individuals. Quakers however were greatly alienated by the abolitionists primarily because of their tendencies toward violence, power, and continual demonization of southerners. Pierce partly blamed the extreme abolitionists for the continuation of slavery. He felt that if they had not demonized southerners so much continually causing them to double down and keep slavery and also not propose any solutions to the end of slavery that wouldn’t cause massive socio-economic problems for the south (problems the abolitionists would never have to deal with) then slavery could’ve ended sooner and cleaner. In fact, as of 1827, there were more than four times as many anti-slavery societies in the South as in the North. The abolitionist movement, in peppering their message with belligerent and vitriolic anti-Southern rhetoric, made it all but impossible for Southern anti-slavery activists not to be viewed with suspicion. This video helps elaborate what I mean (of course slavery is disgusting and evil but that doesn’t change that it was a complicated issue). With bleeding Kansas as historian Brion McClanahan put it, Pierce didn’t foresee the mass violence that would occur from Northern and Southern forces. Pierce’s legal argument was following the Louisiana purchase which allowed for slavery in those territories. He also believed in sovereignty as the people being able to form their own states as they saw fit just as Zachary Taylor treated the western states who were anti-slavery. This is a stark contrast to Buchanan (who is often lumped in with Pierce when people talk about bad Presidents) who accepted a pro-slavery state constitution that was made by a minority of people and not the will of the majority. The other thing Pierce is criticized for is for his criticism of Lincoln because he felt many of Lincoln’s actions were unconstitutional. The thing is constitutionality matters. When one President violates their oath, it opens up the door for the next guy to violate his oath. We went from the Presidency being a fairly minor office with the primary roles being the handling of treaties and vetoing unconstitutional legislation, to basically being an elected monarch who can declare wars, bail out corporations, and walk all over the states like it’s nothing. Because of this Franklin Pierce should be remembered as a man politicians should strive to emulate. A man who valued freedom, state sovereignty, and principle over politics.
