China-Taiwan Weekly Update, October 24, 2025
============================================

Toplines
--------

**Chinese Communist Party (CCP) General Secretary Xi Jinping has expanded his military purges, expelling nine of the seniormost generals from the CCP and referring them to military procuratorates for criminal prosecution. The purge is part of a major restructuring of the People’s Liberation Army (PLA) leadership and reflects Xi’s continued doubt about the political loyalty of the military elite.**The PRC Ministry of National Defense announced the expulsion of the generals from the party and military on October 17.[1] The generals are listed here with their most recent titles before removal. Some or all were removed from their posts before the announcement on October 17.

*   General He Weidong, second-ranked Central Military Commission (CMC) Vice Chairman
*   General Miao Hua, CMC Political Work Department director
*   General He Hongjun, CMC Political Work Department executive deputy director
*   General Wang Xiubin, CMC Joint Operations Command Center executive deputy director
*   General Lin Xiangyang, Eastern Theater Command commander
*   General Qin Shutong, PLA Ground Forces political commissar
*   Admiral Yuan Huazhi, PLA Navy political commissar
*   General Wang Houbin, PLA Rocket Force commander
*   General Wang Chunning, People’s Armed Police (PAP) commander

PRC officials and state media claimed that these nine individuals “seriously violated party discipline and were suspected of serious duty-related crimes involving an extremely large amount of money, of extremely serious nature, and with extremely detrimental consequences.”[2] The Ministry of National Defense spokesperson called the expulsion a “significant achievement in the party and military’s anti-corruption campaign” and claimed that it would lead to a more cohesive and combat-ready PLA.

Eight of the nine generals were members of the CCP Central Committee and were replaced during the Central Committee’s Fourth Plenum from October 20 to 23, though the PRC has not announced replacements for most of them in their military roles.[3] The Fourth Plenum elevated General Zhang Shengmin to replace He Weidong as CMC second-ranked vice chairman, however. Zhang is already on the CMC as its Commission for Discipline Inspection (CDI) secretary. The Fourth Plenum did not announce that Zhang would be leaving his role as CMC CDI secretary and did not add any new members to the CMC, suggesting that he may hold both positions at least temporarily. The Fourth Plenum also announced the expulsion of alternate Central Committee member Lieutenant General Zhang Fengzhong—the PLA Rocket Force’s Political Office director—who was not previously confirmed to be under investigation.

The expulsion of the nine generals marks one of the largest purges of senior military officials in Xi Jinping’s longstanding “anti-corruption” campaign. He Weidong is the first sitting CMC vice chairman to be purged since Zhao Ziyang in 1989 and the first uniformed vice chairman to be purged since He Long in 1967.[4] The removal of He Weidong and Miao Hua, together with former Defense Minister Li Shangfu in 2023, has reduced the CMC from seven members to four in just two years, possibly narrowing the inner circle on which Xi relies for military decision making.

The generals expelled on October 17 are part of a larger group of senior officers across all parts of the military whom the CCP has investigated or punished since late 2024. Taiwanese National Security Bureau (NSB) Director Tsai Ming-yen reported on October 15 that over half of the 35 PLA generals (including three lieutenant generals acting as full generals) have been absent from public view for the past six months and 16 of them since December 2024.[5] The generals purged on October 17 included top leaders in all PLA services except the Air Force, but leaders in every service and theater command have been targeted by anti-corruption authorities since late 2024. This wave of purges appears to be separate from anti-corruption investigations that targeted the PLA Rocket Force and equipment procurement in 2023, removing the PLA Rocket Force commander and two defense ministers.

**Connections between the nine purged generals suggest that most or all were part of a clique likely centered around He Weidong and Miao Hua.**All of the generals, except He Hongjun and Wang Chunning, served in senior roles in the ETC or its predecessor—the Nanjing Military Region—the military districts responsible for the Taiwan Strait.[6] He Weidong, Miao Hua, Wang Xiubin, Qin Shutong, and Lin Xiangyang all served in senior command or political commissar roles in 31st Group Army (now called the 73rd Group Army)—an elite Taiwan-facing frontline unit of the PLA Ground Forces.[7] The 31st Group Army is known for its connections to Xi Jinping, who spent 17 years of his political career working in its home province of Fujian. Xi’s connections from Fujian, including He Weidong and Miao Hua, became one of his core personal networks when he ascended to CCP general secretary in 2012. Many of the purged generals were subordinates of Miao Hua or otherwise promoted by him.[8] The recent round of purges has effectively eliminated the influence of the “Fujian Clique” in the PLA. The CMC’s first-ranked Vice Chairman General Zhang Youxia has remained untouched in the purges, which some commentators have assessed indicates that he is the victor in a factional power struggle.[9] Zhang and newly promoted CMC Vice Chairman Zhang Shengmin are both part of the “Shaanxi Gang”—one of several CCP factions with close ties to Xi Jinping, possibly indicating the expanding influence of that group within the PLA.[10]

The concurrent timing of investigations and other disciplinary action against the generals further indicates connections between their cases, as disciplinary investigations often begin with one official and expand to investigate that official’s network. Qin Shutong, Yuan Huazhi, and Wang Chunning were all absent from a promotion ceremony in December 2024—one month after Miao Hua’s suspension.[11] He Weidong, Lin Xiangyang, Wang Houbin, and Wang Xiubin all apparently disappeared from public view in March 2025 amid unconfirmed reports that they had been arrested.[12]

**The near-total purge of the Fujian Clique from the PLA has removed many of the seniormost officers with experience focused on Taiwan.**It is unclear what effect that will have on PLA planning and readiness, however, as the CCP may yet replace the generals with others with experience focused on Taiwan. It is possible that disagreement over Taiwan policy was a factor in the recent purges, though CDOT-ISW has not observed direct evidence of that.

**The purge of the generals is likely connected to Xi Jinping’s continued push to centralize control of the military under his personal leadership and enforce ideological orthodoxy.**A frontpage editorial in the official PLA newspaper _PLA Daily_ on October 18 accused the purged generals of undermining CCP leadership of the PLA, the CMC Chairman Responsibility System, and the “political and ideological foundation for unity and progress” within the PLA.[13] It specifically named He Weidong, Miao Hua, and He Hongjun and stated that their corruption is a “mutated form of the pernicious influence of Guo Boxiong and Xu Caihou.”[14] The Chairman Responsibility system is a concept the CCP introduced in 2014—in contrast to a previous “Vice Chairman Responsibility System”—referring to the ultimate authority of CMC Chairman Xi Jinping over the military.[15] Guo Boxiong and Xu Caihou were CMC vice chairmen under Xi’s predecessor Hu Jintao. They were arrested and expelled from the party in 2014 for bribery and corruption, having already retired from the CMC.[16] Retired PLA officers noted at the time that Xi likely introduced the Chairman Responsibility System based on his impression that Xu and Guo had usurped Hu Jintao’s power. The officers said that Xu and Guo had served as proxies for Jiang Zemin—the CCP general secretary before Hu—thus undermining Hu while he was in office.[17] _PLA Daily’s_ comparison of He Weidong, Miao Hua, and He Hongjun to Xu and Guo suggests that Xi was concerned that the recently purged generals could similarly undermine his authority.

Recent PRC directives and publications have shown that Xi and CCP leaders are trying to defend the Chairman Responsibility System against not only corruption and self-interest, but possibly also ideological challengers. The PRC issued directives in February 2025 that strengthened oversight of military-related publications after multiple PLA-affiliated organizations published articles that emphasized tenets of collective leadership and intra-party democracy—principles that challenge the Chairman Responsibility System.[18] _PLA Daily_ published a series of commentary articles in July 2025 stressing the military’s subservience to the party with Xi Jinping at the core.[19] The newspaper also announced new regulations, which were not published in full, that likewise emphasized strengthening party control over the military and promoting personnel based on merit rather than personal relationships.[20] Four of the generals purged on October 17—Miao Hua, He Hongjun, Qin Shutong, and Yuan Huazhi—were top political commissars, responsible for enforcing political discipline and loyalty to the CCP within the military. Their removal could be due to ideological disagreements with Xi’s directives or failure to enforce them to Xi’s satisfaction.

Several of the purged generals were promoted as replacements for other purged top officials, indicating that Xi’s earlier rounds of purges did not successfully remove disloyalty or corruption in the military. Miao Hua replaced Zhang Yang as the CMC top political commissar in 2017 after Zhang committed suicide while facing an anti-corruption investigation. Wang Houbin replaced Li Yuchao as PLA Rocket Force commander in 2023, after Li was removed as part of a wave of anti-corruption investigations targeting the PLA Rocket Force and equipment procurement departments.

**Taiwan’s Executive Yuan (EY) approved a large investment in domestic drone production amid efforts to prepare an “asymmetric” defense of Taiwan.** The EY approved a plan on October 16 to invest 44.2 billion New Taiwan Dollars (NTD) (about 1.4 billion US dollars) in the domestic production of unmanned systems over the next six years.[21] The plan also provides for the government to purchase 100,000 drones in the next three years, of which 48,750 are intended for the Ministry of National Defense.[22] The special budget for asymmetric warfare and operational resilience, separate from the approved investment plan, is intended to fund the Ministry of National Defense drone procurement and is still under review in the EY.[23] An asymmetric defense of Taiwan would involve using cost-effective systems that can act as force multipliers for the Taiwanese military.[24] Ukrainian forces have demonstrated how one can use large quantities of inexpensive systems to defend on land and sea against a larger and better-resourced adversary.[25]

The drone procurement plan also bolsters Taiwan’s efforts to become self-sufficient in the defense industry. The plan provides funding for domestic drone research and development as well as drone testing centers.[26] Taiwan continues to work toward self-sufficiency in the defense sector to ensure its national defense is not reliant on overseas supply chains.[27]

Taiwanese Deputy Minister of Economic Affairs Ho Chin-tsang said that the plan provided funds for research and development in the drone industry that aims to position Taiwan as a “non-red” drone supplier.[28] The PRC currently dominates the global drone industry, with PRC drone manufacturer DJI accounting for 90 percent of the US commercial drone market and 80 percent of the worldwide market.[29] Taiwan’s drone industry has recently increased its exports to Poland through the “Taiwan Excellence Drone International Business Opportunities Alliance” initiative.[30]

United States-Taiwan Business Council President Rupert Hammond-Chambers said on October 20 that US arms sales to Taiwan will likely surge if the special budget for asymmetric warfare and operational resilience passes Legislative Yuan (LY) scrutiny.[31] Former US Assistant Secretary of Defense for Indo-Pacific Security Affairs Randall Schriver likewise said on October 14 that US military aid will hinge on the success of the special budget.[32] Taiwanese media have reported that the LY will review the special budget in late October.[33]

**The LY passed a different special budget proposal on October 17 with tripartisan support that enhances preparations to withstand a PRC blockade**. Taiwanese President Lai Ching-te signed the bill into law on October 23.[34] The bill appropriated 107 billion NTD (3.4 billion US dollars) to the Ministry of National Defense.[35] The funds are allocated for purchasing “war readiness” fuel and expanding wartime reserves of ammunition and water to last 120 days without resupply.[36] The bill also provides funds for safer, dispersed storage of critical supplies and modernized communication networks to increase resilience against sabotage or a first strike.[37]

The Kuomintang (KMT) led opposition efforts to cut defense spending from the bill, amounting to only 5 billion NTD (162.4 million US dollars, or less than five percent of the budget) in relatively noncritical areas. KMT legislator and co-convener of the LY Foreign Affairs and National Defense Committee, Ma Wen-chun, criticized the bill for excessive spending on water procurement from abroad.[38] The Ministry of National Defense agreed to find domestic suppliers for the emergency water procurement to cut costs.[39] The LY also cut 3.2 billion NTD allocated for “improving information and communications operating environment and equipment.”[40] The final bill included 64.1 billion NT D for strengthening communications resilience, however.[41]

Taiwan’s energy sector likely remains highly vulnerable to PRC interdiction despite improvements to overall resilience. Taiwan imports approximately 97 percent of its energy.[42] Only three percent of the bill’s defense funding was allocated to enhance resilience in this sector, however.[43] Taiwan’s energy imports would likely be a key target for a PRC blockade or quarantine of the island. The PRC could also pressure Taiwan’s trade partners, such as Qatar, to reduce or cease energy exports to the island during a contingency.[44]

Key Takeaways
-------------

1.   **PLA purges:**CCP General Secretary Xi Jinping has expanded his military purges, expelling nine of the seniormost generals from the CCP and referring them to criminal prosecution. The most recent purges appear to target a distinct human network and are part of Xi’s continued efforts to assert his personal control over the PLA.
2.   **Taiwanese military modernization:** The Taiwanese EY approved a large investment in domestic drone production and procurement to enhance Taiwanese defenses against PRC aggression. These moves reflect the Taiwanese commitment to integrating advanced technologies, such as drones, into its defensive concept.
3.   **Taiwanese resilience:** The Taiwanese LY approved a special budget to support preparations to withstand a PLA blockade. The budget provides funds to stockpile fuel and other critical supplies, among other things. The LY passed the budget in a rare instance of tri-partisan cooperation among the three major political parties.

Cross-Strait Relations
----------------------

### Taiwan

**Taiwanese Defense Minister Wellington Koo expressed doubt on October 21 that Taiwan would receive the 66 F-16V fighter jets that it purchased from Lockheed Martin by 2026, as originally agreed. Delayed acquisition of US systems will likely contribute to greater “America skepticism” within Taiwan and contribute to existing narratives surrounding US “exploitation” of Taiwan.**Koo said that the jet delivery program had encountered delays, which may prevent the purchased jets from arriving on time.[45] Taiwan purchased the jets for approximately 245.38 billion NTD (8 billion US dollars) in August 2020, with final delivery expected by 2026.[46] Taiwan has received none of the jets at the time of this reporting.[47] This incident is part of a series of delays in US weapons deliveries to Taiwan. The Taiwan Security Monitor estimated that the backlog of US weapons sold but not delivered to Taiwan as of September 2025 is approximately 21.5 billion US dollars.[48] Delays have impacted Taiwan’s acquisitions of munitions, traditional platforms, and asymmetric capabilities from the United States, which harms Taiwan’s ability to enhance its military capabilities and defend against PRC aggression.

The PRC and some Taiwanese politicians can use delayed deliveries of US equipment to bolster narratives that weaken US-Taiwanese relations. Taiwanese politicians frequently issue complaints when US weapons delays are announced, criticizing the United States for pushing Taiwan to purchase expensive weapons systems that take a long time to receive. This discourse contributes to broader trends of “America skepticism” within Taiwan, which questions the likelihood of US assistance in a Taiwan contingency. KMT legislator Ma Wen-chun questioned the decision to purchase the F-16s on October 21 and called the delay “unacceptable.” [49] The spread of such narratives within the KMT, which has effective control over the LY, could lead to Taiwan becoming less likely to purchase new weapons systems from the United States and harm US-Taiwanese relations overall.

**The Taiwan High Court sentenced former independent LY candidate Ma Chih-wei to 32 months in prison for accepting campaign funds from the PRC, highlighting PRC efforts to influence the 2024 Taiwanese election and infiltrate Taiwanese politics.**The court found on October 16 that Ma accepted 1.05 million NTD (34,260 US dollars) in cryptocurrency from PRC agents in violation of the Anti-Infiltration Act, overturning the previous ruling that found that there was insufficient evidence.[50] She provided contact information and business cards of Taiwanese government officials, such as national security and central government agency personnel, to individuals affiliated with the PRC government.[51] Ma was formerly the TPP spokesperson for the Taoyuan chapter, but the party expelled her on January 6, 2024, after she was detained on suspicion of receiving PRC campaign funding.[52] The PRC allegedly instructed Ma to run for office, though her campaign was unsuccessful.[53]

National Security Bureau (NSB) Director General Tsai Ming-yen said on October 15 during an LY interpellation that the PRC is increasing espionage efforts in Taiwan. The PRC is known to have tried to influence political parties across Taiwan’s political spectrum, including but not limited to funding the fringe China Unification Promotion Party, recruiting former DPP aides for espionage, funding a hometown association trip targeting KMT wardens, and more. The NSB statements reflect Taiwan’s growing awareness and expanding efforts to counter such PRC efforts.

### China

**The CCP held the fourth plenary session of its 20 th Central Committee from October 20 to 23 and released its 15 th Five Year Plan (FYP) that will guide PRC economic, technological, and military development through 2030. The 15 th FYP signals a continuation of PRC modernization efforts.**The CCP Constitution requires an annual plenum to convene the CCP Central Committee—the highest CCP authority—to discuss policy issues. In a communiqué, this plenary session outlined the 15 th FYP, which seeks to improve PRC economic strength, scientific and technological advancement, national defense capabilities, comprehensive national power, quality of life for the Chinese people, and international influence. A more detailed proposal for achieving the 15 th FYP’s goals is expected to be released in a week and will be passed during the two 2026 sessions.

The communique underscored the importance of continued economic development amid “profound challenges” presented by the “complex international landscape,” likely referring to ongoing trade tension with the United States and its allies. The communique also underscored the importance of national security and creating a modern military by the PLA’s 2027 centenary, “as scheduled.” The communique also mentioned Xi’s “three-step” strategy for modernization, indicating that the internal deadlines of achieving national defense modernization by 2035 and developing a “world-class military” by 2049 remain. The communique briefly mentioned Taiwan—typical for high-level announcements—stressing the importance of peaceful cross-strait relations and “national reunification.”

**Top PRC officials met with US business leaders to discuss bilateral economic cooperation with the US private sector. The PRC likely seeks to find an alternative method of influence within the Trump administration amid US-PRC trade tensions.**PRC Vice Premier He Lifeng and Commerce Minister Wang Wentao met with Apple CEO Tim Cook on October 16.[54] They met separately with Blackstone Group CEO Stephen Schwarzman the same day.[55] Official PRC press releases emphasized that US-PRC cooperation is mutually beneficial and that US actions have severely damaged trade relations between the two countries.

The PRC may try to use meetings with US industry leaders as an alternative avenue of influence with the Trump administration. The PRC has invited top industry leaders to speak with senior officials before; CCP General Secretary Xi Jinping met with heads of major global firms after the China Development Forum business conference in March 2024 and 2025, and the US-China Business Council invited US business leaders to the PRC in July 2025 as trade negotiations were ongoing in Sweden.[56] Some CEOs notably had seats at Xi’s table at a dinner hosted by the USCBC and the National Committee on US-China Relations during his November 2023 visit to San Francisco.[57] Schwarzman was part of the group of business executives that met with Xi in March 2024 and 2025.[58]

The National Foreign Trade Council—a lobbying group with 74 firms on its board, such as Amazon, Coca-Cola, FedEx, and Walmart—addressed a letter on October 20 to US President Donald Trump to suspend the Affiliates Rule administered by the Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security.[59] The Affiliates Rule requires that companies whose majority owner is listed on the Entity List will also be added to that list.[60] The PRC has strongly opposed the Affiliates Rule for “harming the rights and interests of its firms.”[61]

### Japan

**Sanae Takaichi became Japan’s first female prime minister on October 21 after Nippon Ishin no Kai agreed to form a coalition with the ruling Liberal Democratic Party (LDP).[62] Takaichi and her coalition will likely continue to advance Japan’s defense buildup to deter the PRC.** The LDP sought the new coalition after the Komeito party ended its 26-year partnership with the LDP in response to Takaichi’s election as LDP president.[63] The new coalition does not have a majority in either chamber of the National Diet, however.[64] Ishin aligns with the LDP on many foreign and defense issues, including strengthening the US-Japanese alliance, increasing Japan’s defense budget, enhancing Japan’s economic security, and opposing unilateral attempts to change the status quo in Taiwan and the Senkaku Islands by force.[65] Ishin declined to join Takaichi’s cabinet, however.[66]

PRC state media reported on the Takaichi cabinet’s conservative orientation and representation from various factions within the LDP.[67] Takaichi also appointed several officials from previous administrations who had experience engaging with the Trump administration to key positions.[68] PRC Ministry of Foreign Affairs spokesperson Guo Jiakun did not immediately comment on the prime minister election result but reiterated the PRC’s wishes that Takaichi will adhere to previously established agreements.[69] The PRC likely does not approve Takaichi and her Nippon Ishin no Kai ally’s policy views. Guo criticized Takaichi’s statement that declared accelerating Japan’s defense buildup as a priority on October 24, three days after Takaichi became prime minister.[70] Guo claimed that Japan’s increasing defense budget and arms exports call Tokyo’s commitment to an exclusively defense-oriented policy and peaceful development into question and urged Japan to “act prudently in military and security areas.”[71] The PRC imposed sanctions on Nippon Ishin no Kai legislator Hei Seki in September 2025 for his negative remarks about the CPP and support for Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Tibet.[72]

### North Korea

**Ukraine reported the continued presence of North Korean troops and weapons on Russia’s side of the war throughout October 2025, emphasizing troops adapting to use first-person-view (FPV) drones.**UK think tank Conflict Armament Research (CAR) on October 10 documented a submunition with Korean writing saying “Juche 89,” following an attack on a Ukrainian city in September 2025.[73] CAR described the weapon as a modified High-Explosive, Dual-Purpose (HEDP) munition resembling the US-made M42.[74] Ukrainian authorities recovered the munition from an FPV unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV), which had carried the munition attached to a wooden stick. CAR stated that the unmodified version of the munition is designed to be fired by a rocket artillery system. Ukraine released a video showing a North Korean soldier operating multiple FPV quadcopters on October 16, captured through an intercepted communication between Russia and North Korea.[75] Ukraine identified the soldier as a member of a North Korean drone unit active in Russia’s Kursk Oblast.[76] It added that the unit has assisted Russian operations across the border in Ukraine’s Sumy Oblast by operating reconnaissance drones to identify Ukrainian positions and help Russia in adjusting fire on Ukrainian targets.[77]

CAR’s documentation marks the first known use of a North Korean HEDP munition delivered by a FPV UAV in the conflict. Ukraine first recovered an unexploded North Korean HEDP on August 29 but found no indicators at the time suggesting it had been delivered by an FPV UAV.[78] North Korean-made artillery shells and rockets have supported Russia’s war efforts in Ukraine since at least September 2022, according to US intelligence.[79] CAR’s findings on the North Korean submunition are the latest development in Russian-North Korean military cooperation and demonstrate North Korea’s evolving battlefield tactics as it adapts to modern drone warfare. Ukraine’s report of a North Korean drone operator in Kursk also signals continued involvement of North Korean troops in the war. Initial battlefield reports showed North Korean soldiers countering UAVs by using human soldiers as “bait” or attempting to shoot them down with small arms.[80] A Ukrainian official revealed in July 2025 that the Russian military has been training North Korean troops in operating FPV UAVs and other attack drones.[81] The reported drone operation by a North Korean soldier suggests a greater level of integration and tactical adaptation since their initial deployment to Kursk in October 2024.

### South Korea

**The PRC’s Ministry of Commerce imposed sanctions on October 14 on five United States-based subsidiaries of Hanwha Ocean, a South Korean shipbuilding company.[82] The PRC is likely retaliating against South Korea’s strengthening partnership with the United States in the shipbuilding industry.**The sanctions prohibit Hanwha Ocean from conducting any trade with organizations or individuals within the PRC.[83] The PRC cited Hanwha Ocean’s alleged support for the US port fee program as the reason for the sanctions.[84] The port fee program was part of a sanctions package by the Office of the US Trade Representative (USTR) targeting PRC shipping companies and PRC-built ships on October 10. These include a 50 US dollar per net ton fee on PRC vessels entering US ports.[85] These measures are part of an executive order signed by US President Donald Trump on April 10, aimed at bolstering US shipbuilding and countering the PRC’s growing dominance in the industry.[86] Hanwha Ocean acquired US shipbuilder Philly Shipyard to strengthen cooperation with the US Navy in June 2024.[87] Hanwha Ocean announced a 5 billion US dollar investment plan for Philly Shipyard on August 26, following a joint agreement between South Korean President Lee Jae Myung and Trump on August 25 to launch the “Make American Shipbuilding Great Again” (MASGA) initiative.[88]

The PRC sanctions may intend to punish and discourage South Korean cooperation with the United States in sectors where the PRC is a key competitor. The US State Department on October 17 called the sanctions “the latest example in a long pattern of China’s attempts to coerce Korea.”[89] The immediate impact of the sanctions remains unclear, as the company’s US subsidiaries have minimal direct trade with the PRC. Hanwha Ocean could incur up to 60 million US dollars in additional costs over the next two years due to disruptions in its supply chain, however.[90] The PRC may expand sanctions on other South Korean companies with greater dependence on the PRC, pressuring them to scale back cooperation with US shipbuilding out of fear of economic punishment.[91]

**A South Korean court sentenced a PRC national to five years in prison for espionage. The PRC national attempted to obtain classified United States Forces Korea (USFK) information by approaching active-duty South Korean soldiers.[92]**The Seoul Central District Court sentenced a PRC civilian surnamed “Qing” to five years in prison for violating the National Security Act and the Military Secrets Protection Act. Qing recruited active-duty Korean soldiers to spy and disclose classified military information. Qing sought intelligence on the USFK, such as joint exercises, Terminal High Altitude Area Defense (THAAD) operational systems, and recent troop movements. The South Korean Defense Counterintelligence Command arrested Qing in March. The Command revealed that Qing had colluded with an agent from the intelligence office under the Joint Staff Department of the PRC CMC.[93] Qing’s case is the first confirmed case of a PRC national affiliated with a PRC intelligence agent being arrested for espionage activities in South Korea since the establishment of PRC-ROK diplomatic relations in 1992.[94]

Qing’s relatively lenient sentence is due to his conviction under the National Security Act and the Military Secrets Protection Act, rather than the more severe Espionage Act. South Korea’s Espionage Act only applies to activities linked to an indicated “enemy state,” North Korea.[95] The legal examination subcommittee of the National Assembly’s Legislation and Judiciary Committee approved an amendment to expand the scope of the Espionage Law to any “foreign state” in December 2024, which would include cases involving the PRC.[96] The amendment stalled following domestic political turmoil in early 2025, however, and the Lee Jae Myung administration has not revived the bill for consideration. South Korean authorities observed an increase in PRC nationals attempting to obtain intelligence about US forces in South Korea over the last two years.[97] This intelligence could help inform PRC military operations and force allocation in preparation for a Taiwan contingency, likely focused on deterring the involvement of US troops based in South Korea.

Southeast Asia
--------------

### Philippines

**PRC military ships and aircraft harassed a Philippine Coast Guard (PCG) aerial patrol and conducted training exercises near Scarborough Shoal. The PRC appears to be increasing coordination between the PLA, China Coast Guard (CCG), and PRC civilian law enforcement in asserting territorial control over the disputed shoal.**PCG spokesperson Jay Tarriela said that the PCG conducted routine Maritime Domain Awareness (MDA) flights over Scarborough Shoal on October 13 and 15.[98] He said that a PLA Navy helicopter and a PLA Air Force J-16 fighter jet flew dangerously close to the PCG aircraft on October 15.[99] CCG spokesperson Liu Dejun claimed that the CCG “expelled” two Philippine aircraft from the airspace over Scarborough Shoal on October 16.[100]

Satellite imagery released by maritime security researcher and SeaLight director Ray Powell on October 20 shows that the PRC redeployed a floating barrier blocking the entrance to Scarborough Shoal on October 8.[101] The PRC erected similar floating barriers in 2023 and 2024 to prevent Philippine fishing vessels from entering the shoal.[102] The PCG’s MDA flights on October 13 and 15 also observed two PRC buoys deployed at the center and northern tip of the shoal, which may mark the area of ongoing or future PRC operations.[103]

Data from ship tracking software Starboard Maritime Intelligence shows that two PRC ships from the Sansha Comprehensive Law Enforcement (SCLE) body, _San_ _Sha Zhi Fa 101_ and _San Sha Zhi Fa 301_, and several CCG ships circled Scarborough Shoal on October 17. The ships are based in the “city” of Sansha in the Paracel Islands, which the PRC established in 2012 as the administrative center for all PRC-claimed territories in the South China Sea, including Scarborough Shoal.[104 The deployment of the SCLE or any civilian law enforcement ships to Scarborough Shoal is unusual. Data from Starboard Maritime Intelligence, which goes back to May 2020, shows that these SCLE ships typically operate only around the Paracel Islands and that the _301_ has only patrolled near Scarborough Shoal on two occasions in the summers of 2024 and 2025. The same two ships reportedly boarded a Vietnamese fishing boat sailing near Vuladdore Reef in the Paracels and physically attacked the crew in September 2024.[105]

PLA Navy ships and naval aviation units also conducted military training exercises southwest of the shoal on October 17, coinciding with the final day of the US and Philippine-led “Sama Sama” multinational naval training exercise off the coast of Palawan Island, Philippines.[106] PCG aircraft conducting an MDA flight reportedly observed a PLA Navy tanker resupplying a CCG ship.[107]

The PRC’s deployment of PLAAF combat aircraft and SCLE ships to Scarborough Shoal, as well as the use of a military tanker ship to resupply a CCG vessel operating near Philippine territory, may indicate PRC efforts to increase inter-service coordination when conducting coercive activities against the Philippines. CDOT previously assessed that the PLA and CCG’s inexperience in coordinating maneuvers likely contributed to a severe collision between a PLA Navy ship and a CCG ship near Scarborough Shoal on August 11.[108] The PRC is likely blurring the lines between military and law enforcement activities to assert control over the shoal and intimidate the Philippines into accepting PRC encroachment into its exclusive economic zone (EEZ).

Oceania
-------

**A PLA Air Force fighter jet dangerously approached an Australian patrol aircraft and released flares in a close encounter over the Paracel Islands in the South China Sea.[109]**Australia’s defense ministry reported that the jet fired flares near a Royal Australian Air Force (RAAF) P-8A Poseidon maritime patrol aircraft that was conducting a South China Sea surveillance mission on October 19.[110] Australia lodged a diplomatic protest with the PRC and said it would continue to operate in international waters and airspace.[111] PRC Ministry of Foreign Affairs spokesperson Guo Jiakun denied knowledge of the incident in a routine press conference on October 20.[112] PLA Southern Theatre Command Air Force spokesperson Li Jianjian claimed on October 20 that Australia had violated PRC airspace over the Paracel Islands, however.[113]

The PRC, Vietnam, and Taiwan all claim the Paracels, but the PRC alone occupies and controls the archipelago, maintaining an airport, military bases, and administrative facilities on the islands.[114] The PRC claims straight baselines around the Paracels, meaning that it claims all the water between the islands to be its internal waters. The United States and its partners sometimes conduct freedom of navigation operations (FONOPs) to contest these illegal PRC claims, which the PRC considers to be a violation of its territory.[115] A PRC jet previously launched a flare near an Australian jet in February 2025, also over the South China Sea.[116]

The October 19 incident highlights the PRC’s ongoing efforts to defend its illegal or contested territorial claims in the South China Sea, especially against “intrusions” by US coalition partners. Australia’s military activities in the South China Sea reflect increasing cooperation between the United States and its allies to challenge PRC expansionism and malign activities in the region. US President Donald Trump met with Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese on October 20 and expressed support for the Australia-United Kingdom-US (AUKUS) deal to sell Australia US-made nuclear submarines.[117]
