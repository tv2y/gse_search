Story丨On the 4,000-meter-high Tianshan Mountains, they race against the vanishing glaciers

At seven o’clock in Urumqi, Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, China, the sky was still as dark as splashed ink. Frost clung to the car windows, and the thermometer hovered at minus 12 ℃!

A special correspondent from China Meteorological New Press quietly set off with the team of the 2025 International Year of Glaciers’ Preservation Youth Scientific Expedition. Their destination was the No.1 Glacier at the Urumqi River Headwaters in the Tianshan Mountains—a "living archive of glaciers" in China, a white giant standing at an altitude of nearly 4,000 meters, revered by the glaciological community.

Most members of the convoy were young researchers. Instead of travel bags, they carried heavy steam drills, bundles of survey stakes, ice core storage boxes, and more. These instruments would speak louder than words, delivering the profound climate memories hidden deep within the glaciers to the world.

"Our goals for today are crystal clear: extract ice cores and finish deploying the survey stakes." WANG Feiteng, a researcher at the Northwest Institute of Eco-Environment and Resources, Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS), raised his voice above the jolting of the vehicle. Every year, he makes dozens of round trips along this route—all to retrieve the "temporal profiles" written firsthand by the glaciers themselves.

3 hours later, the gravel road came to an abrupt end. The altimeter kept ticking: 3,200 meters, 3,500 meters, 3,800 meters... The higher they climbed, the thinner the air became, squeezing their chests like an invisible hand. Fresh snow from previous days covered the mountain trail; every step sank knee-deep into the snow. When they pulled their feet out, the team’s gasps "crashed" into the icy silence. Trailing deep footprints, they wound their way up the dazzling snow slope.

Trailing deep footprints behind them, the team wound their way up the dazzling snow slope. Photo by LI Qiong

"Slow down. Regulate your breathing," panted Hu Yang, a researcher specializing in alpine ecology, his goggles crusted over with frost. "Truly, these data are carried up here on our feet and backs."

By midday, the team finally reached the glacier terminus. The view suddenly opened up—a vast expanse of ice lay dormant like a giant beast, glinting with a piercing blue light under the sun. The glacier surface was crisscrossed with melt-water channels and erosion marks, resembling cracks etched across the earth.

"This is where China's glacier monitoring and research got its start," WANG Feiteng explained. "Over the past decade, the glacier tongue has retreated more than 50 meters. The elevation where we are standing right now ‘rises’ every year—not because the mountain is growing taller, but because the ice is vanishing."

After selecting the sampling sites, the scientific expedition work kicked off immediately. The steam drill roared as it bored deep into the ice layer. When the drill bit was slowly pulled out, a section of crystal-clear ice core with distinguishable textures was revealed to everyone.

Each segment of ice core is a well-preserved climate chronicle. The ice cores were quickly measured and sealed, and were about to be sent to the laboratory to analyze their gas composition, particle concentration, and other indicators that decode the traces of atmospheric temperature, precipitation, and even human activities over the past thousands of years.

Not far away, the shrill sound of an electric drill pierced the air. Another key task—survey stake measurement—was underway simultaneously. Team members knelt on one knee on the ice, driving metal survey stakes marked with precise scales firmly into the hard ice. Though this work seemed somewhat mechanical, in the alpine, oxygen-depleted environment, every press and adjustment was a test of physical endurance and concentration.

"These survey stakes serve as benchmarks for measuring glacier mass balance," WANG Feiteng stated. By revisiting this site regularly and measuring the length changes of the exposed stakes, researchers can accurately calculate whether the glacier has experienced accumulation or ablation over a given period.

Researchers drilling holes in the ice surface for survey stake measurement. Photo by LI Qiong

From laboratory studies to towering mountain summits, from data simulations to field surveys, glacier conservation and climate change response is a systematic project that demands the concerted efforts of multiple disciplines and cross-departmental collaboration. In Xinjiang, meteorological departments deployed across the vast ice sheets and scientists’ in-situ precision surveys are interwoven with each other—forming an ever-expanding and consolidated protective network for monitoring climate change and safeguarding cryospheric resources.
