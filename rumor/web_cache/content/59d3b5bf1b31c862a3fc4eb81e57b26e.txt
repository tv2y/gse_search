**GROUP:****Hacktivists Launch DDoS Attacks at U.S. Following Iran Bombings**
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

#### OUTLOOK

We assess that based on the current escalation of the Iranian-Israeli conflict (to include, especially, U.S. military strikes on Iranian nuclear infrastructure), Iranian-affiliated cyber actors pose a relatively low direct cyber threat to government entities. In recent months, hacktivists aligned with Iran have engaged in website defacements and leaks of sensitive data obtained from their targets. Additionally, various hacktivist groups have claimed responsibility for Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks against U.S. entities following U.S. airstrikes on Iranian nuclear facilities on June 21. Given these developments, it is likely that these hacktivists will escalate their DDoS campaigns against U.S. and Israeli websites. Furthermore, Iranian-affiliated cyber actors will likely conduct ransomware attacks and data theft, potentially exposing sensitive information online. We have HIGH confidence in this assessment based on reporting from Cyble, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), and other open-source media.

#### OVERVIEW

Pro-Iranian hacktivist groups have likely increased targeting of U.S. entities, likely due to U.S. support of Israel within the broader Iranian-Israeli conflict coupled with the recent U.S. strikes on Iranian nuclear infrastructure. Various groups such as Mr. Hamza, Team 313, Cyber Jihad, and Keymous+ have targeted U.S. Air Force domains, major aerospace and defense companies, as well as banks and financial services. These cyberattacks are part of a larger campaign against Israeli (and Israeli-aligned) targets that intensified after Israel’s strikes on Iranian nuclear and military sites on June 13. The conflict has escalated with missile and drone exchanges between Israel and Iran, including Iran’s missile launch at a U.S. military base in Qatar on June 23, likely in direct response to U.S. military strikes.

#### MITIGATION

Organizations can enhance their cyber defenses against Iranian-affiliated cyber actors by taking several key steps:

1) Identify and disconnect Operational Technology (OT) and Industrial Control Systems (ICS) assets—frequent targets of pro-Iranian threat groups—from the public internet.

2) Focus on securing remote access technologies such as virtual network computing (VNC), remote desktop protocol (RDP), Secure Shell Protocol (SSH), and web management interfaces, whether as part of a Human Machine Interface (HMI), virtual private network (VPN), or otherwise.

3) Adopt a deny-by-default allowlist policy to help prevent unauthorized access if an asset’s remote access cannot be removed.

4) Ensure that devices and accounts are protected with strong, unique passwords or multifactor authentication (MFA) and immediately replace weak or default passwords.

5) Utilize Role-Based Access Controls (RBAC) and conditional access policies for cloud service or managed service providers.

6) Consider strategically requiring MFA for changes to high-value controllers that are difficult to replace or could have a significant impact if compromised.

7) Apply the manufacturer’s latest software patches for internet-facing systems to ensure protection against known vulnerabilities.

8) Ensure business continuity and incident response plans are in place for a swift recovery, including implementing full system and data backups to facilitate any recovery efforts—essential for overall cybersecurity resilience.

#### ANALYSIS

##### **Initial Access**

Iranian-affiliated cyber actors and aligned hacktivist groups exploit vulnerabilities by targeting unpatched or outdated software with known Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVEs) and using default or common passwords on internet-connected accounts and devices. Their techniques include automated password guessing, cracking password hashes using online resources, and exploiting default manufacturer passwords. When focusing on OT, these actors utilize system engineering and diagnostic tools to target engineering and operator devices, performance and security systems, as well as vendor and third-party maintenance and monitoring systems.

##### **U.S. Involvement Draws Hacktivist Attack**

Following U.S. military action, hacktivist groups have retaliated with claims of attacks, some of which vary in credibility. The Department of Homeland Security (DHS) issued a warning on June 22, stating that “Low-level cyber-attacks against U.S. networks by pro-Iranian hacktivists are likely, and cyber actors affiliated with the Iranian government may conduct attacks against U.S. networks.” DHS has also warned about the potential for violent reprisals, especially if Iranian leadership issues a religious ruling calling for retaliatory violence against targets within the U.S.

##### **Hacktivist Activity**

Cyble dark web researchers have observed multiple hacktivist groups aligned with Iran claiming DDoS attacks against U.S. targets following the bombings on June 21. For instance, the hacktivist group, Mr. Hamza, claimed targeting various domains of the U.S. Air Force and U.S. Aerospace & Defense companies under the hashtag #Op_USA. These claims were substantiated with check-host.net reports showing website downtime over a ten-hour period on June 22. Another hacktivist group, Keymous+, claimed that they had attacked multiple U.S. financial entities and shared check-host.net links displaying website disruptions for an hour on June 22. Team 313 also claimed to have targeted Truth Social, the personal social media platform of the U.S. President Donald Trump, but failed to provide substantial evidence to support their claim. In a related development, the Cyber Jihad Movement announced plans to launch cyberattacks against U.S. entities, initiating the campaign with the hashtag #OpUSA.

Cyber Jihad Movement

##### **U.S. Hacktivist Activity Small Compared to Middle East**

So far, the number of hacktivist attacks targeting U.S. entities has been relatively low in comparison to the significant volume of attacks and threat groups operating in the Middle East region. Cyble has identified 88 hacktivist groups active in the Middle East since hostilities commenced on June 13, with 81 of these groups believed to have affiliations with Iran. Iranian-aligned groups have also targeted countries such as Jordan, Egypt, the United Arab Emirates, and Saudi Arabia in their cyber campaigns. These nations seem to have been viewed as too neutral by the groups involved in the conflict.

Emerging Threat

**MALWARE:****The Evolution of Linux Binaries in Targeted Cloud Operations**
----------------------------------------------------------------------------

#### OUTLOOK

We assess that the development of Linux Executable and Linkable Format (ELF) files by threat actors to target cloud infrastructure poses an elevated risk to government targets. A recent report on cloud threats by Palo Alto’s Unit 42 researchers revealed a significant rise in cloud-based alerts, with an average increase of 388% in 2024. Furthermore, 45% of organizations have reported a surge in advanced persistent threat (APT) attacks. It is worth noting that researchers estimate that a substantial percentage, ranging from 70% to 90% of computational instances in cloud environments are based on various flavors of the Linux operating system (OS). While ELF-based malware is not a new phenomenon, these malware families and associated attack techniques are expected to evolve towards targeting cloud infrastructure in the future. We have HIGH confidence in this assessment based on reporting by Palo Alto Networks, and open-source media we deem to be mostly credible.

#### OVERVIEW

Unit 42 has identified a growing threat in cloud security involving the development of Linux ELF files by threat actors to target cloud infrastructure. They anticipate that threat actors focusing on cloud environments will increasingly employ sophisticated tools in their attacks. This shift will involve enhancing and customizing existing tools that traditionally target Linux OS. The ELF malware samples utilized by threat actors will likely leverage various malicious functionalities such as backdoors, droppers, remote access Trojans (RATs), data wipers, and binaries exploiting vulnerabilities. Unit 42 conducted an analysis on five distinct ELF-based malware families that threat groups have used to target cloud environments in their operations. These activities include malicious operations directed at cloud environments and the direct exploitation of cloud infrastructure through the deployment of these ELF binaries. The findings suggest that attackers will likely persist in using ELF binaries to target cloud infrastructure.

#### MITIGATION

In safeguarding cloud environments against malicious Linux binaries, it is critical for organizations to leverage endpoint security agents on cloud computing instances. This measure ensures the detection of any malicious runtime processing, network traffic, and suspicious behavioral operations. Also, machine-learning detection methods can be used to flag malicious binaries. Additionally, defenders can enhance their security posture by proactively hunting for common ELF malware executions within cloud endpoints. This can be achieved through cloud detection and response (CDR), a comprehensive cloud security solution that integrates endpoint detection and response (EDR) capabilities, detection and prevention of executable processes on cloud endpoints, as well as auditing and logging functionalities inherent within the cloud service platform.

#### ANALYSIS

##### **Evolving ELF Binaries**

Commonly known Linux malware families have shown the ability to adapt and target cloud resources. Unit 42 has identified evolving strains of ELF-based malware, including NoodleRAT, Winnti, SSHdInjector, Pygmy Goat, and AcidPour. These malicious ELF binaries employ techniques like dynamic linker hijacking, leveraging the LD_PRELOAD environment variable to:

*   Inject malicious code into legitimate system processes
*   Hook into critical Linux services like the SSH daemon (sshd)
*   Exploit vulnerabilities or misconfigurations in containerized environments.

This allows threat actors to establish persistence, maintain covert command and control channels, exfiltrate data, and disrupt operations through data wiping.

##### **NoodleRAT**

The malware allows threat actors to conduct Command and Control (C2) operations on a targeted endpoint, including:

*   Access through reverse shell
*   Socket Secure (SOCKS) proxy tunneling
*   Encryption of communications
*   Scheduled code execution
*   Uploading and downloading of files
*   Spoofing of process names

NoodleRAT exists in both Windows and Linux variants, with the Linux version being an ELF-based backdoor. While sharing similarities with other Linux backdoor malware like Rekoobe and Tiny SHell, NoodleRAT is recognized as its own distinct malware family. It has been identified in cybercriminal and cyberespionage activities, with Chinese-speaking threat actors such as Rocke and suspected nation-state actors linked to the Cloud Snooper campaign using the malware. The Linux variant of NoodleRAT has targeted organizations in various countries across the Asia-Pacific region, including Thailand, India, Japan, Malaysia, and Taiwan.

##### **Winnti**

Winnti has versions for both Windows and Linux OS. This malware establishes persistence by exploiting the LD_PRELOAD environment variable, allowing it to be loaded into memory without changing any legitimate system binaries.

The backdoor includes the following functionalities:

*   Remote command execution
*   File exfiltration
*   SOCKS5 proxying for facilitating C2 communication

The Linux variant of the Winnti malware serves as a backdoor utilized by various threat actors associated with China, including groups such as Starchy Taurus (also known as Winnti Group and BARIUM) and Nuclear Taurus (also known as Tumbleweed Typhoon, THORIUM, Bronze Vapor). This backdoor comprises two key files: a primary ELF executable named libxselinux and an accompanying dynamic library named libxselinux.so.

##### **SSHdInjector**

The SSHdInjector is a Linux SSH backdoor that injects malicious code into the sshd during runtime. This injected code enables threat actors to maintain persistent access and carry out various malicious activities, including credential theft, remote command execution, malware ingress, unauthorized file and directory access, opening a remote shell, and data exfiltration.

The SSHdInjector has been identified as being utilized by multiple threat actors with ties to China, including a group known as Digging Taurus (also referred to as Daggerfly, Evasive Panda). The targets of these attacks are typically related to cyberespionage and have encompassed individuals, government entities, and telecommunications organizations.

##### **Pygmy Goat**

Pygmy Goat is a Linux backdoor initially found on Sophos XG firewall devices that has the capability to target other Linux-based systems. This malware establishes initial access and persistence by utilizing rootkit functionality, exploiting the vulnerable libsophos.so library file, which is susceptible to authentication bypass (CVE-2022-1040).

The executable injects itself into the sshd by leveraging the LD_PRELOAD environment variable on the targeted device, thereby intercepting SSH communications. The threat actor can communicate with the malware by sending specially crafted ICMP packets (which is referred to as “port knocking”) or embedding a series of magic bytes in SSH traffic.

Its functionalities include:

*   Establishing remote shells
*   Capturing network packets
*   Creating cron jobs
*   Tunneling through a reverse SOCKS5 proxy

Reported targets of this malware include government agencies, suppliers, non-governmental organizations (NGOs), as well as entities in the healthcare and transportation sectors within the Asia-Pacific region.

##### **AcidPour/AcidRain**

AcidRain and its newer variant, AcidPour, are types of destructive Linux wiper malware associated with the Russia-based threat actor Sandworm (a.k.a. Razing Ursa, Voodoo Bear). AcidRain, an ELF binary, specifically targets modems and routers utilizing the MIPS architecture. On the other hand, AcidPour, another ELF binary compiled for x86, has a broader impact range, affecting Linux x86-based storage arrays, network devices, and industrial control systems. Both variants utilize Input/Output Controls (IOCTLs) to carry out data destruction before self-deleting to evade detection. In the event of a threat actor gaining shell access, for instance, through successful web shell deployment or container escape, AcidPour or a potential new iteration could effectively wipe unprotected x86-based cloud systems.

**Iranian-Based Ransomware Crew Reemerges, Promises Big Bucks for Attacks on U.S. or Israel**
---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

An Iranian-based ransomware-as-a-service operation with ties to a government-backed cyber crew has reemerged after a nearly five-year hiatus, and is offering would-be cybercriminals cash to infect organizations in the U.S. and Israel. The malware, an updated version of 2020’s Pay2Key, previously linked to Tehran’s Pioneer Kitten, now uses several of the Mimic ransomware’s capabilities, according to the threat research team at Morphisec, a purveyor of defensive security products. In a June 23 post screenshotted by the security firm and shared in a Tuesday report [PDF], the ransomware crew that now uses the name “Pay2Key.I2P” promised a “favorable percentage (80% instead of 70%) for anyone engaged in an attack against enemies of Iran. This is primarily Israel and the United States. Write in support.” The threat researchers replied, expressing mock support, gained the criminals’ trust, and used the connection to collect information about Pay2Key.I2P’s operations and malware. After analyzing the gang’s updated ransomware and uncovering significant similarities between it and ELENOR-Corp., a Mimic ransomware variant, the Morphisec team concluded that “Pay2Key.I2P appears to partner with or incorporate Mimic’s capabilities.” Researchers first identified Pay2Key ransomware in late 2020 when its masters primarily targeted Israeli companies. Alleged victims included Habana Labs, an Israel-based chip startup that Intel acquired in 2019. In December 2020, the criminals claimed on Twitter to have stolen 53GB of data from the chipmaker and threatened to leak it. At the time, security firms Check Point and Clear Sky linked Pay2Key to the Iranian government-based threat group Pioneer Kitten (aka Fox Kitten aka).

##### **Malicious Chrome Extensions with 1.7M Installs Found on Web Store**

Almost a dozen malicious extensions with 1.7 million downloads in Google’s Chrome Web Store could track users, steal browser activity, and redirect to potentially unsafe web addresses. Most of the add-ons provide the advertised functionality and pose as legitimate tools like color pickers, VPNs, volume boosters, and emoji keyboards. Researchers at Koi Security, a company providing a platform for security self-provisioned software, discovered the malicious extensions in Chrome Web Store and reported them to Google. Some of the extensions are no longer present but many remain available. Several of those extensions are verified, have hundreds of positive reviews, and are featured prominently on the Chrome Web Store, misleading users about their safety. Users should check for the following add-ons in Chrome browser and remove them as soon as possible:

*   Color Picker, Eyedropper — Geco colorpick
*   Emoji keyboard online — copy&paste your emoji
*   Free Weather Forecast
*   Video Speed Controller — Video manager
*   Unlock Discord — VPN Proxy to Unblock Discord Anywhere
*   Dark Theme — Dark Reader for Chrome
*   Volume Max — Ultimate Sound Booster
*   Unblock TikTok — Seamless Access with One-Click Proxy
*   Unlock YouTube VPN
