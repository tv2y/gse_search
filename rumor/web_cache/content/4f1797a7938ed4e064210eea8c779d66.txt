SHELBY, N.C. (WBTV) - Police officers in Shelby, North Carolina, joked in 2015 that a 21-year-old who murdered nine people at a church 250 miles away would come through their small town.

Sergeant Mike Myers, Sergeant Dan Bernat, and officer Scott Hamrick did not anticipate, however, that they’d be the ones to take Dylann Roof into custody.

“Anytime anything goes on, we say, ‘Well, they’ll come through Shelby,” Sgt. Myers said. “I don’t know why, but it’s attractive. They either drive through or come to Shelby.”

Roof, 21, murdered nine Black parishioners at the Mother Emanuel African Methodist Episcopal Church in Charleston, South Carolina on June 17, 2015.

The victims of the June 17, 2015, massacre at Mother Emanuel AME Church in downtown Charleston: Ethel Lance, Tywanza Sanders, Cynthia Graham Hurd, the Rev. DePayne Middleton-Doctor, the Rev. Clementa Pinckney, Myra Thompson, the Rev. Sharonda Coleman-Singleton, and Susie Jackson.(Live 5)

For the first time since the massacre, three of the five officers who detained Roof in Shelby spoke exclusively with WBTV about how an ordinary day turned into one that helped bring a conclusion to a horrific act of hate.

“My heart goes out to the victims and their families,” Sgt. Bernat said. “As someone who could ... walk into the Lord’s house and do what the did ... I don’t have any respect for anybody that can do such a crime.”

### **From Charleston to Shelby**

Roof was nowhere to be found after the deadly mass shooting on Wednesday, June 17, 2015. The following morning, there was a possible sighting in Shelby, North Carolina.

“It all started with one woman who paid attention,” said former Shelby police chief, Jeff Ledford.

That woman was Debra Dills. She was on her way to work when she noticed someone who looked like Dylann Roof driving a black Hyundai Elantra.

Dills called her boss, who called someone at the Kings Mountain Police Department, who then called the Shelby Police Department.

“She could have easily got off her exit and went to work and said, ‘You know, I think I’ve seen this guy.’ But instead she made that phone call,” Ledford said. “We owe her a lot.”

At 10:32 a.m. on that Thursday, a radio call was made to Shelby police officers.

“Black Hyundai, white male, traveling westbound. Advised the [driver] looks like the subject they’re talking about on the news from that Charleston shooting. Says he has a bowl haircut, white male, appears to be the vehicle they see on TV.”

As Sgt. Bernat tried to pull up a picture of Roof on his cell phone, his partner Officer Burris spotted the black Hyundai. They followed the car west down U.S. 74 near Polkville Road. Officer Scott Hamrick and Sgt. Mike Myers joined the pursuit.

“I remember calling Sgt. Bernat on the radio and saying ‘hey, I think we got enough personnel. You know, whenever you’re ready,” Sgt. Myers said. “It just so happened that we wound up at the traffic light at the end of city limits.”

Bernat made the call.

“It had to be done, it had to be effective, it had to be then,” Sgt. Bernat said.

He turned on his blue lights, and the black Hyundai pulled off U.S. 74 at Plato Lee Road into a driveway of an abandoned home. Officer Burris and Sgt. Bernat got out of their car as Hamrick was behind them with his gun drawn, pointed at the driver.

“I could tell pretty well that it was [Roof], because I don’t know hardly anybody else with a hairstyle like that,” officer Hamrick said. “I was thinking like, ‘I can’t believe he came to Shelby. What’s he doing in Shelby?’”

As Sgt. Bernat approached the car, he said one thought went through his mind: the driver appeared to be a child.

“Because of his young appearance, I asked him his name. He told me his name was Dylann Roof,” Sgt. Bernat said. “It’s like I was staring Satan in the face.”

At 10:49 a.m., Roof was put in handcuffs. He complied with officers and admitted what he had done.

“I remember me asking him, ‘What’s your name?’ And he said, ‘Dylann Roof.’ I said, ‘Were you involved in the shooting in Charleston at the church?’ And he said, ‘yes,’” Sgt. Myers said.

Myers then asked Roof if there were any weapons in the car. Roof admitted there was a gun in the back seat. But how Roof referred to the weapon grabbed Sgt. Myer’s attention.

“He referred to it as the gun. It stuck out to me when he said, ‘The gun is in the back seat under the pillow.’ It wasn’t ‘there’s a gun,’ it was ‘the gun,’” Sgt. Myers said.

Officer Hamrick then drove Roof to the Shelby Police Department.

“He just looked beat to me,” officer Hamrick said. “He didn’t say a word ... Not a word.”

Sgt. Myers said Roof didn’t show any remorse.

“It was like he was blank, like he was relieved that it was over with. But you could see, because of the way he was carrying himself, that he was ready for the next chapter of it,” Myers said. “It was almost like he was glad it was over, now he’s ready to tell the story.”

Jeff Ledford said he was being interviewed by a reporter for an unrelated story when he heard what his officers had accomplished.

“My assistant came in and got me out. She just whispered to me, ‘We’ve stopped Dylann Roof,’” Ledford said. “I was like, ‘You’ve got to be kidding me.’”

Ledford said the high point of that day was calling police in Charleston to let them know “we got him.”

### **A cheeseburger, fist bumps and high fives**

Despite their efforts, Shelby police drew criticism online following Roof’s arrest.

Did officers really buy Roof food from Burger King?

“That got blown way out of proportion,” Ledford said.

Ledford said he got a phone call from a reporter who asked the following questions about someone in custody: Did they ask for an attorney? Did they need food? Did they ask for anything to eat? Did you feed them?

Ledford believes his answer was misconstrued.

“I said, ‘I think he’s been offered food … we don’t have a cafe in our headquarters,’” Ledford said. “We’ve had food brought from anywhere … it could have been a burger, I’m not sure ... it’s probably Burger King if it was a burger, I don’t know what he ate.”

Ledford explained that community members had brought food from several different fast-food restaurants to the Shelby Police Department to show their appreciation.

“When you have a person in custody, you have to provide them certain things,” Ledford said. “Food aside, anything [you] do, you’re preparing for trial. You’re not going to give a team something they can hang their hat on and go, ‘oh, you deprived them of this or that.’ You’re crossing every ‘t’ and dotting every ‘i.’”

An article from a local media outlet said: “Earlier in the day, Roof had bought water and chips at a south Charlotte gas station. Now he was hungry. Police bought him food from a nearby Burger King,” Ledford said.

“National outlets picked out that one line from a bigger story and ran with it. Social media helped spread the misinformation faster -- some believed the officers took Roof inside the fast-food restaurant and ate at a booth with him. Others believed the officers took Roof through a the drive-thru.

All of that is false, according to Ledford.

“I can assure that you at no point did any of that ever happen,” Ledford said.

Officer Hamrick backed up his statement.

“I can assure you that I did not take him to Burger King before I took him back to the [police station],” officer Hamrick said.

Sgt. Myers said he and his fellow officers also received criticism for how they reacted after apprehending Roof. In dash camera video, the officers were seen giving each other fist bumps and high fives.

“That’s a gesture that we do to acknowledge the excellent work,” Sgt. Myers said. “After everything calmed down, we were relieved … there [were] so many things that could have happened that didn’t happen, and people can criticize, but I’m pleased 100% with what the officers did and the decisions they made.”

### **Reflecting 1 decade later**

“We were just lucky that [Roof] complied and that everything went so smoothly,” Sgt. Myers said. “Now could it have went bad? Yes, in a split second.”

Sgt. Myers said he and his fellow officers conducted themselves in a way that didn’t cause Roof to react.

“I was thinking about what he might do. I knew what I was going to do if he did something, but fortunately that didn’t happen,” officer Hamrick said.

“You sit down, and you start thinking, ‘What could have happened? Had this been different, where would we be right now?’ And then you get this sick feeling of what could have happened,” said Ledford, who went on to say he’s proud of his officers. “These [officers] went home that night.”

Sgt. Bernat, Sgt. Myers, and officer Hamrick all testified at Roof’s trial. He was convicted of 33 federal hate crime charges and sentenced to death.

“The justice system … it did work here in the flesh. Judgement to me will be in [Roof’s] next life. I think that’s when he’ll receive true judgement,” Sgt. Bernat said.

All the officers agree that witnessing the guilty verdict was satisfying for them and the families of the victims.

“They got to look the devil in the eyes, the families did,” Sgt. Myers said.

The officers who arrested Roof have been called heroes, but each of them said that it’s a title they don’t want.

“We don’t look for publicity … the true light of what happened is in Charleston,” Barnet said. “We were just simply doing our jobs, and we’d do it over again.”

“I’m definitely not a hero. We were just out doing what we would do any other day,” officer Hamrick said. “We don’t really deserve the accolades that we were given.”

The officers -- all downplaying their role in bringing justice to the families of the nine victims -- humbly redirected the focus to Charleston, South Carolina.

“We only had a small part,” said Sgt. Myers.
