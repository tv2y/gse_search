When your argument—or in this case, a photograph—contradicts common and therefore often limited assumptions about a complex subject, the lack of hard evidence can indeed make it difficult to support your position, especially when the prevailing opinions are based on hearsay or small selection of widely known artifacts. Yet in such situations, our best resource remains the judgment of experts. Scholars have identified the photograph in question as Vaslav Nijinsky portraying the "Negro" in the ballet _Le Roi Candaule_, no later than 1907. This attribution is supported by multiple publications.

To reconcile own fixed opinion with information that feels difficult to accept, one must remember that Nijinsky was an artist of extraordinary range _—_ a metamorph, capable of profound and convincing transformation.

Due to his relatively short stage career, the image of Nijinsky was soon shaped more by popular _—_ and therefore more reproduced _—_ photographs and secondhand accounts than by direct experience of those who seen him dance. As Nicolas Nabokov wrote in the early 1940s, Nijinsky had become a myth:

_“In all fairness, one should say that for most of the younger collaborators of the Diaghilev enterprise—like Balanchine, Rietti, Tchelitchew, and myself—Nijinsky was a kind of myth from the 'Golden Age of Ballet.' Most of us had never seen him dance, except in photographs.”_

To expand the boundaries of this myth—which inevitably narrows our understanding of the past—we must remain open to learning more. The scattered bits of information that is there can help us reconcile images that may, at first glance, seem impossible to align.

In his memoir _Against the Current_, Michel Fokine tells:

_“Nijinsky was magnificent in the role of the Negro [in Scheherazade]. The lack of overt masculinity that distinguished this extraordinary dancer—and which made him unsuitable for certain roles (for example, the Chief Warrior in Igor)—was perfectly suited to the role of the Gray Negro. Neither in skin color nor in movement did he resemble a real Black man. He was half-animal, half-man—at times like a cat, leaping softly across great distances; at others, like a stallion with flared nostrils, pawing the ground with his legs…”_

The bottom line: the identity of Vaslav Nijinsky in the photo is definite, the ballet and the role are debatable.
