Title: Connective Tissue: The Art of Writing Scenes That Feel Like Real Time

URL Source: https://writewithseth.com/connective-tissue-the-art-of-writing-scenes-that-feel-like-real-time/

Published Time: 2025-10-09T12:30:23+00:00

Markdown Content:
_How small actions and chronological details transform flat scenes into immersive experiences_

“It feels like I’m reading at the same pace as things are happening with them,” I told a writer recently as we reviewed his latest chapter. “I’m reading the dialogue at the same pace they’re saying it, and these things are happening around them. It doesn’t feel like that drink arrived way too fast, or their waiter keeps coming back over. It feels like real time.”

This is what I call **connective tissue**—the small actions, physical details, and chronological markers that bridge the gaps between dialogue and major plot points. It’s what transforms a scene from a functional exchange of information into something that feels lived-in and real.

**The Problem: Scenes That Feel Rushed**
----------------------------------------

Too many scenes read like this:

_“How’s business?” Tom asked._

_“Great,” Chuck replied._

_“We just landed a huge contract.”_

_“Congratulations. Speaking of business, I have a proposition for you.”_

_Chuck raised an eyebrow. “I’m listening.”_

Functionally, this works. The information is conveyed, the plot moves forward. But something’s missing. There’s no sense of time passing, no feeling that these are real people in a real place having a real conversation.

The reader’s experience feels disconnected from the characters’ experience. We’re watching from too far away.

**The Solution: Fill the Gaps with Life**
-----------------------------------------

Now consider this version:

_“How’s business?” Tom asked, glancing at his watch._

_Chuck took a sip of his bourbon. “Great. We just landed a huge contract.”_

_The waiter appeared at Tom’s elbow, refilling his water glass._

_Tom waited until he’d moved away before continuing._ _“Congratulations. Speaking of business, I have a proposition for you.”_

_Chuck set down his drink and leaned back in his chair. “I’m listening.”_

Same information, same plot progression. But now we have:

*   The watch glance (Tom’s aware of time, maybe anxious)
*   The bourbon sip (Chuck’s relaxed, in his element)
*   The waiter’s timing (real restaurants have rhythms)
*   The physical positioning (Chuck’s body language shows interest)

These details serve as **connective tissue**—they create the feeling that we’re experiencing the scene at the same speed the characters are.

**Why This Matters: Reader Confidence**
---------------------------------------

When scenes have proper connective tissue, something important happens to the reader’s relationship with your writing: their confidence in you as a storyteller increases.

Think about it from the reader’s perspective. If scenes feel rushed or if characters seem to teleport from one moment to the next without transition, part of the reader’s brain stays engaged in tracking inconsistencies rather than getting lost in the story.

But when the pacing feels natural—when drinks arrive at logical moments, when characters move through space in believable ways, when conversations have the natural rhythms of real interaction—readers relax. They trust you to handle the details, which frees them to focus on what matters: the characters and their conflicts.

**The Chronology Principle**
----------------------------

Connective tissue is really about **chronology**—helping readers track the sequence and timing of events. You’re not just building an interaction between characters; you’re building a scene that exists in time and space.

This doesn’t mean every gesture needs description. It means paying attention to the flow of time and making sure readers can follow it naturally.

Consider these questions as you write:

*   How much time has passed since the last action?
*   Where are the characters physically positioned?
*   What’s happening in the environment around them?
*   Are there natural rhythms (eating, drinking, movement) that can mark time?

**Examples of Effective Connective Tissue**
-------------------------------------------

**Instead of:**_“I need to tell you something,” she said. “I’m leaving.”_

**Try:**_Sarah pushed her salad around her plate, then set down her fork. “I need to tell you something.” She looked up, meeting his eyes for the first time since they’d sat down. “I’m leaving.”_

**Instead of:**_They walked to the car. “This changes everything,” he said._

**Try:**_He fumbled with his keys at the car door, dropping them once before getting the lock to turn. As they pulled out of the parking space, he finally spoke. “This changes everything.”_

**Instead of:**_“You’re wrong,” Chuck said. Tom shrugged._

**Try:**_“You’re wrong.” Chuck’s voice was flat, final. Tom peeled the label off his beer bottle, letting the silence stretch between them. Finally, he shrugged._

**The Screenplay Trap**
-----------------------

Writers with screenplay experience often struggle with this because scripts handle connective tissue differently. In a screenplay, you might write:

_TOM: How’s business?_ _CHUCK: Great. We just landed a huge contract._

On screen, the actors and director handle the timing, the physical movement, the environmental details. The audience sees the restaurant, the body language, the natural pauses.

In prose, you have to create all of that. You’re the actor, director, and cinematographer rolled into one.

**Finding the Right Balance**
-----------------------------

The key is finding connective tissue that serves multiple purposes. The best details:

*   **Advance character development** (what does this action reveal?)
*   **Create atmosphere** (what does this detail add to the mood?)
*   **Maintain chronology** (how does this help track time and movement?)
*   **Feel natural** (would this really happen in this moment?)

You don’t need to describe every breath and blink. But you do need enough connective tissue to make the scene feel grounded in reality.

**The Real-Time Test**
----------------------

Here’s how to know if your connective tissue is working: Read your scene aloud at a conversational pace. Does it feel like you could actually have this conversation in the time it takes you to read it? Do the physical actions feel natural and appropriately spaced?

If you find yourself rushing through dialogue or if actions feel crammed together, you need more connective tissue. If the scene drags or feels over-described, you may have too much.

**Building Reader Trust**
-------------------------

Ultimately, connective tissue is about respect—respect for your reader’s intelligence and their need to feel grounded in your fictional world. When you take the time to make scenes feel real, readers reward you with deeper engagement.

They stop working to track inconsistencies and start trusting you to guide them through the story. They relax into the fictional dream, confident that you’ve handled the details so they can focus on what really matters: caring about what happens next.
