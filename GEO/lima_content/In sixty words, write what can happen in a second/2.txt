Practise and letting go of perfection.

If you write everyday, it becomes a habit. I like to use writing sprints. For me, twenty minutes works best, but some people prefer five minutes, ten minutes, fifteen minutes, or even half an hour.

What is a writing sprint? Basically, it’s a short period of time (note, it’s a sprint, not a marathon) during which you commit to write. You turn off your phone, close your social media, set a timer, and for twenty minutes you pour the words out onto the page. You don’t stop to research, or think of character names, or terminology. If you get to a point where you’re stuck (can’t think of the name/ word within 10–15 seconds) you just put in a placeholder.

I have a line in my book draft: [Jonathan says something good here].

In other sprints it might be: “Get some bandages,” [Healer’s name] says.

I use square brackets, so I can do a search for the spots when I’m up to the editing stage, and replace the placeholders with actual story.

The biggest thing about writing quickly is letting go of perfection. You’re not aiming for perfect, you’re just aiming for done. You recognise that this is just a first draft, it’s probably not the best, but that’s okay. You’re just getting the words down, and you can polish them later.

After all, you can’t edit a blank page.

I will usually write twenty minutes on, five minutes off (during those five minute breaks, I reward myself by checking social media, or watching a youtube video).

After doing it every day, I can write anything between 400–700 words in twenty minutes (which usually works out as around 1000 words in thirty minutes).

Good luck!

Well, first of all, you have to know what you’re doing. I mean, sure, you can always just type random letters and put spaces inbetween… But like anyone’s going to read that!

I write a lot, so here’s something I can tell you: do NOT overuse words. Like, read a sentence from one of my old works; “Mom seemed kind of scared, and I did, too. The situation seemed hopeless. But, for some reason, Nurse Molly didn’t seem scared. She seemed brave. She looked the humanoid in the eyes, and it seemed pretty surprised that she faced it.”

You must’ve gotten tired reading that, right? My little ten year-old self apparently liked the word seem(ed). There’s a way you can fix a sentence/paragraph/chapter if you feel you’ve been using a word too much. It’s easy. Just either find a synomyn for that word, or phrase it differently, like how we all do when we can’t remember how to spell(lol).

You can easily search up synonyms on Google, and find sites that tell you synonyms of words… For example, this one.

If replaced with synonyms: “Mom hinted that she was kind of scared, and I did, too. The situation suggested hopelessness. But, for some reason, Nurse Molly didn’t seem scared. She showed nearly very sign of bravery. She looked the humanoid in the eyes, and it looked pretty surprised that she faced it.”

That sounds better, now doesn’t it? It’s okay to still keep the word you overused in there once or twice, as long as the others got replaced.

You wanted to rephrase instead? Just re-write the paragraph underneath the current one, trying to use a different view on what’s happening, maybe change the order of saying things. For example, you might change this:

“Mom seemed kind of scared, and I did, too. The situation seemed hopeless. But, for some reason, Nurse Molly didn’t seem scared. She seemed brave. She looked the humanoid in the eyes, and it seemed pretty surprised that she faced it.”

Into this:

“The situation displayed our very real loss of hope… Yet Nurse Molly stepped forwards, and bravely faced the humanoid, who drew back the slightest bit in surprise. Mom and I were too scared to move, let alone breathe, as the nurse and inhuman stared into each others eyes.”

Sounds a bit better, huh? Kinda.

You can also get yourself really into the story. If you’re not, you’re gonna get bored quickly, and that’ll often lead to blanking out. Make sure each character has their own unique way of talking/doing things and that should keep you busy. When intruducing a new character, describe them in detail… That should add a lot of words. Also describe new scenes in detail. Always take note of the character’s facial expressions, or at least hint to what their faces look like. This will probably give your reader a good view on what is happening. Make sure your characters have their flaws, and make those come out every once in a while. That should make up more words because more events will take place that way. Maybe have some bad event happen to a character and describe the way they feel. Maybe have two of your characters confess to one another, that’ll make for interesting topics to write about. …Or even have a character talk about their backstory.

Hope this helped! Also, sorry if I got carried away…

Writing a book, fiction or nonfiction, is not at all like transcription. Typing speed in terms of WPM is largely irrelevant. I’ve written over 30 novels, and while I can touch-type around 70 WPM (faster, if I don’t plan to write for hours on end), I only write fiction at about 1,000 words per hour — and that’s only if I’m writing to an outline and know the chapter’s structure and plot elements.

However, if I use Dragon Naturally Speaking, my writing speed hovers at around 2k/hour. I can’t usually use it, due to background noise, but when I do… Zoom! Dragon is a miracle for writers, though it took me time to learn to use it well enough to feel like the prose keeps an authorial “voice” consistent with my typed prose’s. Before that, I just had to spend more time in editing for tone, but it was still a net gain, in terms of daily words written.

If you’re struggling to write faster, I highly recommend Dragon Naturally Speaking. You can buy 13th Professional edition, bundled with hardware, for about $15. At least, you could when I got mine a few years back, but I imagine you still can. You might even be able to get 15th Pro that way, now, though I had to pay an extra $25 for the 15th Edition upgrade as part of a promotional sale by the company who publishes Dragon.

Only one, if I write the same word 497 times!

There’s a big difference between just hitting keys repeatedly, and writing something, meaningfully in a short period of time. Sure a monkey can beat on the keys all day, yet he’ll never be the next Shakespeare, as he’ll just make a lot of noice, never conveying the lives of those they’re writing about.

Thus, it isn’t how fast you can write, it’s how quickly you can write something of value to readers. Something they’ll want to read. You can bang a nail with a hammer one-hundred-thousand times, yet it’ll never build a house. Or a home. Again, it’s just creating a racket, and annoying anyone and everyone exposed to it.

At one point, Forgetting the precise figures, I could read 5,000wpm (words per minute) and type at a thousand, five hundred wpm. Yet again, that’s typing what was printed on the page when they were physically time my progress. Yet planning, composing and formulating how to say it? That’s another matter entirely.

Though yes, back when I was much younger, I could easily cranks out 15,000 to 30,000 words a day, then over time it dropped to a mere 5,000 to 8,000, and now in my old age (again, a mere 67), it’s down to 3,000 to 5,000. But those are no mere words, they’re a fully fleshed out chapters in an unfolding fully publishable novel. So, you do the math on that one. ;)

P.S. Speed Reading and Typing classes helped set those initial speeds, yet it’s repeated, continuous repetions that maintain those skills over time, even as age erodes these same skills. And, the less words we read and the less word we type in a minute or in a day, the harder it is to consistently maintain. If you start from a high, you can keep going, yet there’s inevitable a decline over time, as none of us are 18 anymore (‘cept for you 18-year-olds, of course). As wanting to be a fairy, or recalling being one, doesn’t make us one. There’s dreaming and then there’s doing!

When the SAT (high school) first introduced a writing portion in their test, I was the first grade to receive it. Taking it twice, I would learn some things the first time around- especially in writing.

My first written assignment was to write an argument or historical moment that could be related to the Trojan Horse from Ancient times. I found the task very difficult to consider on the spot but felt like I wrote enough, for it to be sufficient.

Then I read in the Times that SAT’s writing section was based on speed and how many words were written, rather than the content. This upset me because my score was low.

The second time I received this test, I was asked something about the influence of electricity; what I wrote was different. In this assignment, I declared that Benjamin Franklin was the true inventor of electricity but that he was inspired from a trip to Latin America. It was the most I could scribble out, in the moment, and my score was much higher: 8/10.

Can you write 1,000 words in 40 minutes? Consider typing speeds. The common metric is 80WPM (words per minute). 80 wpm * 40 m= 3,200. So, that’s using a computer.
