Depicting The Donald: How Framing of Donald Trump in Media Changed Throughout the Course 

of His 2015-2016 United States Presidential Campaign 

Jakob Janoski 

This study examined how framing of Donald Trump in online news images may have changed 

throughout the course of his 2015-2016 United States presidential campaign. Previous research 

suggests that Republican presidential candidates may get more coverage but not necessarily be 

framed as more favorable (Moriarty and Popovich, 1988). The content analysis comprised of 18 

constructed weeks in which images from various outlets were analyzed in nine categories (e.g., 

“facial expression” and “posture”) and coded as either “more favorable,” “N/A” or “less 

favorable.” It was found that image favorability of Trump dipped from the Pre-Primary period to 

the Primary period then rose again during the General Election to similar levels of the Primary 

period. Newsweek had the least favorable imagery of Trump while The Wall Street Journal had 

the most favorable imagery.

Introduction 

Donald Trump’s mugshot from his August 2023 arraignment at the Fulton County Jail in 

Georgia made headlines. It was the first time a former president of the United States had a 

police photograph released to the public. This caused much buzz in the media, from news to 

social media. Although the mugshot is new, imagery of The Donald looking stern is nothing out 

of the ordinary as his image has always seemed to be constructed that way. 

Donald Trump is a long-time public figure, starting out in real-estate in the early seventies, 

later becoming a reality television star and eventually the President of The United States of 

America. The imagery of Trump has changed over time, including the time of his second run at 

the presidency as he dominated the media landscape throughout the course of his candidacy 

(June 15, 2015-November 8, 2016), controlling at least 50% of the news coverage over the 110 

days of the primary campaign period (Bitecofer, 2017). With Trump being so widely covered, the 

way he is portrayed in coverage can vary by medium or political leaning of an outlet, whether 

digital, physical or print. Because of events that occurred during Trump’s 2015-2016 presidential 

campaign, this period provides a useful platform to show how he may have been portrayed 

differently in different media outlets.  The concept of media framing can help to explain this 

phenomenon. 

Due to his large public presence, there are many opportunities to frame the former U.S. 

president to different audiences and in different ways. Framing involves “the persistent patterns 

of cognition, interpretation, and presentation, of selection, emphasis, and exclusion, by which 

symbol-handlers routinely organize discourse” (Gitlin, 1980, p. 7). In the media, framing can be 

used to call attention to certain aspects of an event while making other aspects less relevant, 

> 1

potentially leading to audiences of different media having different perspectives on certain 

events (Entman, 1993). Visuals can play a role in framing, serving to help confirm what has 

happened in a story and create a fuller picture of the way in which it occurred (Graber, 1990; 

Boomgaarden et al, 2015). 

How a publication (digital, television or print) decides to frame someone, particularly a 

presidential candidate, is important due how voters will decide whom to vote for. This study 

examined how images of Donald Trump changed throughout his first presidential campaign. The 

results provide a basis for how to analyze changes in imagery of presidential candidates over the 

course of a campaign, from the first to the last day. 

Research has demonstrated that imagery can influence perception, suggesting why 

producers use it. Visuals can sway someone’s view on a subject to think in one way or another 

regarding a certain topic (McDonald, 2008). Images “are more than decoration; they perform 

important roles in communication such as conveying realism, credibility, and attitudes” 

(Moriarty & Popovich, 1991, p. 372). Close shots of notable people are often viewed as more 

important than those that clarify information or pertain to unfamiliar people (Moriarty & 

Popovich, 1991). Angles and position of candidates can affect how viewers see them (Moriarty 

& Popovich, 1991). Such a prominent figure like Trump has much imagery circulating about him, 

leading to many ways he could be framed in the media.

Literature Review 

This review of literature begins with definitions of framing and establishes a working 

definition for this research. Its main focus was news framing: how major events are depicted, 

how the depiction of presidential candidates in news photos might differ between candidates, 

and how coverage may differ between them. The review concludes with a short illustration of 

how frames can change over time. 

Framing 

Framing is a complex of cognitive processes for selecting and presenting information to 

generate a particular meaning and attitude towards a phenomenon. All individuals engage in 

framing to make sense of their world; it is “the persistent patterns of cognition, interpretation, 

and presentation, of selection, emphasis, and exclusion, by which symbol-handlers routinely 

organize discourse” (Gitlin, 1980, p. 7). The working definition deals with how we present 

information to others, which can also entail a selection of certain facets from events in order to 

promote a certain narrative or interpretation of an event (Entman, 2003). 

Additionally, framing is the “definitions of the situation [that] are built up in accordance with 

the principles of organization which govern events—at least social ones—and our subjective 

involvement in them” (Goffman, 1974, p. 10-11). Goffman is saying that framing can coincide 

with organizations’ belief systems and how people can influence them. For example, Harmon 

and Muenchen (2009) found that Fox News used more “pro-war” framing terms in their news 

broadcasts than other networks like ABC, CBS, NBC and CNN. Likewise, framing influences how 

people may perceive events. It can help people understand how other’s experiences can 

> 3

influence the way they report (Alieva, 2021), possibly through how certain aspects of events can 

be chosen to portray the event in a certain way (Entman, 2003). 

Studying News Framing 

To investigate news content, researchers have examined combinations of manifest and 

latent content.  Manifest content can be interpreted at face value, such as where a picture may 

be placed on a page, while latent content involves qualities beyond what may appear on the 

surface and can be defined by more subjectively such as facial expressions and non-verbal cues 

(Kim & Stepchenkova, 2015). Both are necessary for analysis as some features of photos are 

more concrete while others are left up to interpretation. 

Manifest content “is explicit, refers to observable features of the images, and can be 

recorded with a high degree of reliability” (Kim & Stepchenkova, 2015, p. 30).  In one example, 

researchers studied how photos in print and online media were taken, coding for details such as 

camera shot position (Hellmueller & Zhang, 2017) and the distance from which the photo was 

taken. Such content is manifest because there is no debating whether or not the shot was taken 

from a certain position. If the shot comes from a low angle, one cannot argue the image was 

taken from above. Other manifest content examples involving news photos are placement of 

the photo on the page (Dahmen, 2009; Borah, 2009), size of photos (Dahmen, 2009) and the 

page number the photo is on (Borah, 2009; Parry, 2010). If a photo takes up the whole of page 

two, there is no argument to how much space the photo takes up and where the photo is in the 

paper. In television, researchers also have taken into consideration the length of a shot 

(Tiemens, 1978) and the placement of subjects in the shot (Tiemens, 1978; Stewart et al, 2019). 

Camera movements such as zoom-ins and zoom-outs have also been considered (Grabe et al, 

> 4

2001).  Television frames have also been measured in manifest terms such as how individuals 

look toward or away from the camera (Banning & Coleman, 2009). 

The state of the individuals depicted can also be coded, although this area of content can be 

more latent. Researchers examined disfigurement (Parry, 2010) and role and age of people 

depicted (Greenwood & Jenkins, 2015). These characteristics may be more latent in that these 

characteristics were subjective qualities judged by (trained) researchers. In terms of image 

content, researchers have coded expressions and other non-verbal communication such as 

posture and activity (Banning and Coleman, 2009). When analyzing qualitative data, specifically 

latent content, people can analyze it and extract meaning from what they are viewing (Lindgren 

et al, 2020). Judgment calls from those untrained to view content can be problematic as they 

have a higher chance of misinterpreting the image. 

Researchers have used news photos to identify frames in a variety of ways. Parry (2010) 

used news imagery to compare how two different newspapers framed politicians in the news. 

Borah (2009) did something similar by comparing how two American newspapers covered two 

separate events, the Indonesian Tsunami and Hurricane Katrina, finding that tsunami coverage 

featured more of the tragedy while hurricanes featured more of the relief work. Banning and 

Coleman (2009) used non-verbal cues to identify both body language expressions of 

presidential candidates on television. Tiemens (1978) and Stewart (2019) both used shots on 

television to determine how U.S. presidential debates were framed and found that the framing 

slightly favored former president Jimmy Carter (Tiemens, 1978).

Role of Visuals in Media Framing 

Prior research illustrates how media frames are used to call attention to certain aspects of 

events, groups, and individuals (Entman, 1993). News images illustrate what has occurred in a 

story and capture its happenings (Hagaman, 1993). With regard to images, news framers 

typically use them "in such a way as to promote a particular problem definition, causal 

interpretation, moral evaluation, and/or treatment recommendation'' (Entman, 1993, p. 52). 

Visuals in media help to clarify what may be happening in a story. The adult participants 

(college students, blue and white collar professionals) in Graber’s (1990) experiment believed 

that imagery helped them to create a fuller picture of a topic at hand. Additionally, they 

confirmed that visuals confirm the story and make it easier to understand the scope of a 

situation. Images that were partial to one side of an issue “nonarousing descriptive images that 

were partial to one set of circumstances reported in the news did distort the perception of the 

issue in the direction of the partial image … [and] balanced text must have been present 

enough in the contemplation of the issue to prevent the biasing image effect” (Zillmann et al, 

1999, p. 223). As imagery and framing help create a fuller picture, it creates a better framing 

effect than text alone (Boomgaarden et al, 2015). 

Visually Framing Individuals 

Newsrooms often use an inductive approach to framing, having predetermined frames to 

present to an audience and selecting images to use based on existing newsframes they want to 

present to the audience. Entman (1993) says, “Frames call attention to some aspects of reality 

while obscuring other elements, which might lead audiences to have different reactions'' (p. 

55).  This may be conscious, such as with a strongly liberal newsroom choosing photos of a 

> 6

Republican candidate that portrays them in an unfavorable manner, but it’s more often an 

unconscious product of the news routine, presenting a subject in a way that aligns with how 

things have been done before. 

Identifying Favorability in Images of Presidential Candidates . An American presidential 

candidate is bound to have many eyes on them. A common subject of visual analysis is how 

favorable an image is of a subject. Deephouse (2000) states that media favorability is the 

assessment of an entity in the media.  A common focus researchers have studied is expressions 

of presidential candidates from news outlets to determine whether or not a presidential 

candidate was framed as favorable or not (Lee et al, 2004; Moriarty & Garramone, 1986; 

Moriarty & Popovich, 1991). 

Favorability researchers have coded images to determine whether coverage of a presidential 

candidate was favorable, neutral or not favorable. One commonly used metric was a number 

scale of 1 (less favorable) to 3 (more favorable) (Moriarty & Garramone, 1986; Moriarty & 

Popovich, 1991). In these examples, researchers coded facial expressions as more favorable if 

the subjects were smiling or determined and less favorable if they were looking downtrodden 

(Moriarty & Popovich, 1991). Moriarty and Popovich (1991) used characteristics like arm 

placement, camera angle and facial expressions to determine whether or not an image of a 

subject is favorable or not. Interaction, expression and activity were used by Waldman and 

Devitt (1998) to determine favorability of subjects in imagery as well. 

Another latent element of photos that researchers have examined was posture, with upright 

and slumped being respectively labeled as more favorable and less favorable (Moriarty & 

Popovich, 1991). These investigations also involved more manifest content, such as the setting 

> 7

in which a photo was taken or how the candidate may have been dressed on a particular day 

(Moriarty & Garramone, 1986). 

These approaches to favorability have led to findings that suggest certain candidates may 

receive more favorable treatment than others.  Along with the fact that Bush (in 1988) and 

Reagan (in 1984) received more coverage than their counterparts, they also tended to be 

depicted more favorably (Moriarty & Garramone, 1986; Moriarty & Popovich, 1991). One of the 

most common types of photographs of presidential candidates is that of them interacting 

cheerfully with a crowd (Lee et al, 2004). Bill Clinton was framed more favorably than Bob Dole 

in all publications examined in the Waldman and Dewitt study (1998). If a presidential candidate 

has a lack of facial expressions, the photographers tried to get a picture with more action, such 

as arms being raised as it may portray the candidate as a strong leader (Glassman & Kenney, 

1994). 

Quantifying Coverage for Presidential Candidates. Another area of emphasis in presidential 

candidate imagery research is the amount of coverage candidates received compared to their 

competitors. Researchers have conducted content analyses on how much imagery of each 

candidate was published during a specific period of time during a campaign (Moriarty & 

Garramone, 1986; Moriarty & Popovich, 1991). Quantity of coverage is usually measured as the 

number of photos presented in selected publications. For example, Republicans in the 1984 and 

1988 US presidential elections received more coverage than those of other political parties 

(Moriarty & Garramone, 1986; Moriarty & Popovich, 1991), and both George Bush and Ronald 

Reagan received more coverage than their opponents (Moriarty & Garramone, 1986; Moriarty 

> 8

& Popovich, 1991).  While the amount coverage may differ among candidates, the type of 

coverage they might receive may change as well due to events that may occur. 

Frame Change 

A final area of significance for this research is that of frame change. Media frames can 

change regarding a public figure in the wake of a major event. Two examples illustrate how 

coverage changed regarding attitudes toward Yasser Arafat and the Palestinian Liberation 

Organization (PLO) after 9/11 and framing during the Iraq war. 

After 9/11, framing of Yasser Arafat, the former Chairman of the PLO, became more negative 

in regard to framing compared to that of Ariel Sharon, the former Israeli Prime Minister, the 

coverage of whom became more positive (Tsekouras, 2009, p. 6). To make these 

determinations, researchers sifted through New York Times articles referring to Arafat and 

Sharon and looked for affective and cognitive frames. Tone of coverage was determined by 

affective frames (positive, negative, neutral), “friend or foe” (friend, foe, neutral) and reliability 

of an article (yes or no). 

Frames also changed drastically during the Iraq war. Prominent frames used in this conflict 

were the insurgency frame, which “defined the war as a struggle of a violent, domestic-based 

‘insurgency’ against the Iraqi military, U.S. military, and other coalition forces” (Speer, 2017, p. 

287) and the Danger of Civil War Frame, which “presented ongoing violence in Iraq in terms of 

the possibility of a civil war between different ethnic and religious groups in Iraqi society” 

(Speer, 2017, p. 287). Before the Samarra Bombing, the insurgency frame was prominent. After 

the bombing, journalists associated the bombing to the potential of civil war (Speer, 2017), 

> 9

promoting the Danger of Civil War frame.  This shows that framing of people and events can 

change due to instances that occur. 

Consideration of frame change augments this research’s focus on presidential candidate 

images. It has already been researched how candidates may be favored over other candidates in 

the media over the course of an election period and how their favorability may change 

(Moriarty & Garramone, 1986; Moriarty & Popovich, 1991; Waldman & Dewitt, 1998). However, 

a study about imagery from digital media of a presidential candidate during different periods of 

their campaign seems to be lacking. These findings may serve the research they are based on by 

illustrating how the use of imagery to frame a presidential candidate may or may not change 

throughout the course of their campaign. The results may guide research into candidate framing 

in other campaign periods.

Research Questions 

Research regarding how candidates may be favored over other candidates in the media over 

the course of an election period and how their favorability may change has been examined 

(Moriarty & Garramone, 1986; Moriarty & Popovich, 1991; Waldman & Dewitt, 1998). However, 

this study examined news imagery in digital media of a presidential candidate during different 

periods of his campaign, which seems to be lacking. These findings may serve the existing 

research by illustrating how the tone of news imagery of a presidential candidate may change 

throughout the course of a presidential campaign. 

RQ1: How did media framing of Donald Trump in the 2015-2015 presidential campaign differ 

between stages of the campaign? 

Favorability may change over the course of the periods of photos being examined. The 

pre-primary period being studied may have President Trump appearing more favorable in the 

outlets than the general election period but less favorable than he may look in the primary

Method 

Sample 

This content analysis looked at how the framing of news images from news organizations of 

then-candidate Donald Trump changed throughout the course of his 2015-2016 US presidential 

campaign from June 16, 2015 to election day on November 8, 2016. This date range was used to 

cover the course of Trump’s entire campaign from his announcement of running to before the 

first primary (June 15, 2015 to January 30, 2016), from the first primary to the Republican 

National Convention (February 1, 2016 to July 21, 2016) and from after the convention to end of 

the general election (July 22, 2016 to November 8, 2016). 

Three different legacy media outlets from the United States were used for sampling: The 

New York Times (NYT), Newsweek and The Wall Street Journal (WSJ). Samples of photos were 

taken from these outlets as they cover a range on the political spectrum with WSJ being more 

right-leaning, NYT being more left-leaning and Newseek being more moderate (AllSides, 2023). 

The sample consisted of 18 constructed weeks (126 total photos) with a picture of Donald 

Trump from a story regarding Trump from each outlet from each day of the constructed week. 

Constructed week sampling “involve[s] identifying all Mondays, and randomly selecting one 

Monday, then identifying all Tuesdays, and randomly selecting one Tuesday, etc., to ‘construct’ a 

week that ensures that each source of cyclic variation - each day of the week - is represented 

equally” (Lacy et al, 2001, p. 837). There was two constructed weeks for each outlet for each 

outlet for each period: period one being from the first primary to the Republican National 

Convention (February 1, 2016 to July 21, 2016), period two being from the first primary to the 

> 12

Republican National Convention (February 1, 2016 to July 21, 2016) and period three being after 

the convention to end of the general election (July 22, 2016 to November 8, 2016). 

Coding 

Manifest content that was analyzed in this content analysis is camera angle and posture, 

among other things (Moriarty & Popovich, 1991).  Latent content in the photos refers to 

whether or not Trump is framed as more favorable or more unfavorable (Waldman & Devitt, 

1998), which was explained in the next section. Favorability was be determined on a scale of 1 

to 3 with 1 being less favorable, 2 being N/A and 3 being more favorable, no decimals, i.e., “0.5,” 

were not used (Moriarty & Garramone, 1986; Moriarty & Popovich, 1991). N/A was used if the 

evaluation confirms that the criteria is not met for an element to be more favorable or less 

favorable. The value was “2.” 

Coding Guidelines 

Moriarty and Popovich’s (1991) coding procedure was used for this study. Their reliability 

was similar to that of Graber’s (1988) in the study in which this is based off of. Each photo was 

coded using the criteria in Figure 1. Moriarty and Popovich used newspaper outlets for their 

study, however, for this study, online outlets were used. Their model was still valid for this 

content analysis as news images are still being analyzed just in a different medium. 

Moriarty and Popovich (1991) used three indices in their analysis: were “behavior,” 

“context” and “perspective.”  “Behavior” includes activity, posture, arms, hands, eyes, and 

expression. “Context” includes props, setting, dress, and interaction with colleagues and family. 

“Perspective” includes the position, size, camera angle, and portrayal. The elements of each of 

these indices are explained below. 

> 13

Behavior. Activity was coded for what action the subject is performing. Favorable activities 

include dynamic behavior while less favorable activities include lethargic behavior. 

Posture was coded for how the subject may be seated or standing. Favorable posture 

includes standing upright while unfavorable posture includes being bowed, slumped or leaning. 

Arms were coded in regard to their position. Favorable arm placement includes arms held 

above the head or high while unfavorable arm placement includes them below the head, at rest 

or crossed. 

Hands were coded in regard to how the subject uses them. Favorable hands are gesturing or 

doing something while unfavorable hands are placed at the side, resting or in pockets. 

Eyes were coded in regard to where the subject is looking. Favorable eyes are looking 

directly at the camera or at someone while unfavorable eyes are shut, rolled or looking up. 

Expression was coded in regard to how the subject is acting. If the subject is cheerful or 

confident, it is more favorable. If the subject is unhappy, worried, or tired, they are framed as 

less favorable. 

Context. Interaction was coded regarding the subject's people skills. Cheering crowds and 

attentive colleagues are favorable while the subject being alone or with inattentive crowds or 

colleagues is unfavorable. Props, setting and dress was not be addressed as they were not 

coded for favorability in the Moriarty and Popovich (1991). 

Perspective. Camera Angle refers to the viewpoint at which the subject was shot. A camera 

angle looking up at the subject is favorable while a camera angle looking down at the candidate 

is unfavorable. 

> 14

Portrayal of the subject refers to the tone of the subject. A dignified, serious or presidential 

tone of the subject is more favorable while a comedic or goofy subject is less favorable. 

Position and size from Moriarty and Popovich’s original coding document was excluded as 

the images are all in relatively the same position on the web story and are essentially the same 

size each time on the web story. This was not to be used as images in web stories across the 

same outlets are generally a similar size.

Data Analysis 

To analyze the results, a chi-square test was used. The chi-square is used “to deal with 

significant differences in contingency tables” (Barceló, p. 1, 2018). Overall favorability values 

from the pre-primary, primary and general election periods were compared using this method.

Results 

This study asked how image favorability of Donald Trump changed over the course of his 

2015-2016 campaign across three news outlets: The Wall Street Journal, New York Times and 

Newsweek . This produced statistically significant results in the overall interaction of image 

favorability and publication, as well as in four of six more specific interactions of image 

favorability and criteria type within each publication and era. The overall interaction of image 

favorability and era was not statistically significant. Two interactions did not meet the criteria 

required for chi-square analysis; their results are submitted for descriptive purposes but should 

not be considered statistically significant.

different stages of his 2015-2016 presidential campaign. The relationship between all the eras 

and image favorability and the relationship between image favorability for the Primary era 

yielded data that was statistically significant to the relationship between era and image 

favorability of Donald Trump.

Discussion 

The aim of this study was to find out how the favorability of imagery of Donald Trump 

changed over the course of his 2015-2016 campaign for President of the United States of 

America, with comparisons being made between eras of the campaign period and between 

different news publications. The first section pertains to the favorability of Trump imagery 

between outlets. Data pertaining to each individual outlet ( Wall Street Journal, New York Times, 

Newsweek) and the overall favorability of the outlets is discussed. The second section pertains 

to the favorability of this imagery over time. Data pertaining to each era (Primary, Pre-Primary 

and General Election) and overall favorability of the eras is discussed. The third section regards 

the method and possible shortcomings in using it for analyzing digital publications. Finally, 

limitations and implications are discussed. 

Favorability 

Era Favorability 

Image favorability fluctuated throughout the campaign. In general, coverage started off 

more favorable during the Pre-Primary period, dipped during the Primary period then rose 

again during the General Election period to similar levels as those of the Pre-Primary. This aligns 

with previous research that says candidates wait to attack other candidates until later in their 

campaign to establish a positive image of themselves (Damore, 2002). However, not all indices 

shared the same peaks. Index 1 (Behavior) favorability was highest during the Pre-Primary 

period and stayed relatively the same between the Primary and General Elections periods. Index 

2 (Context) and Index 3 (Perspective) favorability, on the other hand, were both highest during 

> 23

the General Election period. All three indices’ favorabilities were lowest during the Primary 

period (Table 3). 

Building personal credibility in the earlier stages of a campaign can help candidates bolster 

the credibility of their attack advertisements (Damore, 2002). According to previous research, in 

a race of three or more candidates, those that are behind the front runner in regards to polling 

numbers will tend to attack the front runner, who themselves may only attack the second- or 

third-place candidate (Ridout & Holland, 2010). This may help to explain the dip in Trump’s 

favorability during the Primary era. During the primary, then-candidate Trump led competing 

Republicans in a majority of the polls, making him the target of attack ads marring his image, 

speaking to how Tsekouras (2009) found how events or happenings can affect framing. 

Publication Favorability 

Favorability differed between outlets. The Wall Street Journal was the most favorable of all 

the outlets while The New York Times and Newsweek followed in terms of favorability. All the 

publications in the study chose different ways to frame Trump, which aligns with Goffman’s 

(1974) argument that different entities use different frames to project different ideas. The New 

York Times results (Table 7) were closest to the overall results (Table 5), which contradicted the 

expectation, based upon a typology from AllSides (2023), that Newsweek would be the most 

moderate of the three outlets and The New York Times the most left-leaning, showing that 

journalistic principals may overtake personal principals. The Wall Street Journal projected the 

highest number of favorable frames (Table 6) while Newsweek projected the highest number of 

N/A frames (Table 8) and The New York Times (Table 7) the highest number of less-favorable 

> 24

frames, pointing to how Harmon and Muenchen (2009) found that news organizations frame 

the same topic differently. 

Waldman and Dewitt (1998) examined how newspapers framed politicians differently than 

other news outlets, finding newspapers presented different levels of favorability through visual 

choices such as camera angle. With regard to Index 1 (Behavior), Wall Street Journal had the 

highest frequency of more favorable entries (just over half). The New York Times had the 

second-highest frequency (just under half) while Newsweek had the lowest (just under 40%). In 

Index 2 (Context), all publications had less than five entries in the N/A section, meaning that the 

publications would almost always choose a less favorable or more favorable image when 

portraying Trump’s interaction with others. Although The Wall Street Journal had the highest 

number of favorable entries overall (Table 6), it was the least favorable with regard to Index 2. 

This aligns with Entman’s (1993) study where he points to how outlets may choose to portray 

specific aspects of a subject. 

Waldman and Dewitt (1998) showed how different outlets may present the same person in 

different ways. In Index 3 (Perspective), The Wall Street Journal (Table 6) had almost 60% of 

their entries coded as more favorable, while The New York Times (Table 7) and Newsweek (Table 

8) both had fewer than half of their entries as more favorable, meaning a majority of their 

entries were either less favorable or neutral. The Wall Street Journal tended to portray Trump as 

a powerful leader with how they took pictures of the then-candidate while The New York Times 

and Newsweek did not, thus showing how outlets portray Trump in different ways. 

Appropriateness of Method 

> 25

The method used (Moriarty & Popovich, 1991) in this research is well-established, but it was 

also developed for examination of imagery in traditional physical media such as newspapers. 

For this research, that method was adapted to examine digital media. In doing so, criteria had to 

be excluded, such as the position and size of the picture on the page of the newspaper 

(Moriarty & Popovich, 1991), when it was found that each publication had similar picture 

formatting for each article. Finding a way to mold the method in a way that favors 

digitally-based media may produce a more useful set of data. 

Another shortcoming of the method is the low-range spectrum of favorability because the 

study only has three options: more favorable, less favorable, or it does not fit the criteria. A way 

to expand on this would be to have criteria that match criteria like “very favorable,” “somewhat 

favorable” and so on, which would provide a more diverse data set. This does, however, 

introduce complications for coding this latent content, creating greater potential for 

disagreement on the instrument’s more subjective variables. Another shortcoming is that there 

are no neutral criteria for the study. If there was a criteria that met neither the more nor less 

favorable criteria, it was marked as neutral in this study. Criteria such as eyes, activity and 

camera angle were often marked as N/A as they did not either qualify as more or less favorable. 

Limitations 

One limitation is the low number of outlets examined in the study. The outlets used were 

three legacy media outlets that were once all physical, a choice made to strengthen the 

research’s connection to Moriarty and Popovich’s (1991) original instrument. Incorporating a 

greater range of outlets, such as those that are strictly online and those that are on the more 

extreme ends of the political spectrum, could potentially show greater polarity. Every outlet has 

> 26

different political ideologies, so different publications may frame candidates differently even if 

they align politically. 

As noted, the instrument had some shortcomings. Elements of the method (Moriarty & 

Popovich, 1991), such as the size and positioning of images, had to be omitted due to the 

instrument being originally used for physical publications. There are also more aspects to 

consider regarding what may be examined in the picture. With new technology in photography, 

there may be more aspects of the photo itself to examine, such as focus or what signs may be in 

the crowd. Therefore, better adapting the method to be used to analyze digital publications and 

advances in technology could produce higher-quality results.. 

A final consideration for future study would be the time in which the article was published 

in respect to events surrounding the politician being studied. Events such as Trump mocking a 

disabled reporter could have an impact on what imagery was used in the article around the 

time the event occurred. Headlines could also have an impact on what imagery was used in a 

story. 

Implications 

This study gives future research a basis for how to conduct an analysis regarding the 

favorability of imagery of political candidates over time and publications with digital platforms. 

Previous work has examined physical media such as magazines (Moriarty & Garramone, 1986; 

Moriarty & Popovich, 1991) and newspapers (Waldman & Dewitt, 1998), but none appeared to 

analyze digital publications’ publishing of photos in this particular subject area. 

This study shows that even though The New York Times may be the most left-leaning outlet 

of the publications studied, the portrayal of a presidential candidate may go against the grain of 

> 27

what one may expect the outlet to portray a candidate. It was actually Newsweek, a moderate 

outlet (AllSides, 2023), that had the least favorable coverage of the three. In light of this, it may 

not be appropriate to consider a publication politically leaning one way or another as an outlet 

may have coverage that diverges from the political leanings it tends to display. 

Finally, this study gives readers a better understanding about how favorability of a political 

candidate may change over time. The findings suggest that favorability fluctuates throughout 

the campaign periods, starting off high during the Pre-Primary period, dipping during the 

Primary period and rising again to similar levels during the General Election period, agreeing 

with studies that coverage of a political candidate may fluctuate over time (Moriarty & 

Garramone, 1986; Waldman & Dewitt, 1998). Whether this phenomenon is distinct to Trump 

cannot be definitively proven, although previous research suggests it may be common for other 

candidates as well. Therefore, Trump does not break the mold of how favorability fluctuates 

among presidential candidates.
