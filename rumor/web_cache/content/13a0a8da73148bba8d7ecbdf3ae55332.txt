3Executive summary 

1. Introduction 

This study is part of the project Study Sustainable Mobility in Peru, developed by Lima Cómo 

Vamos, with the support and financing of the Embassy of the Kingdom of the Netherlands for 

Peru in Lima (EKN) and the Netherlands Enterprise Agency (RVO). The project aims to 

exploit the potential of Peru's private sector in terms of solutions and services related to 

micromobility, electric transport, and last-mile logistics while identifying opportunities for 

collaboration in the industry between Peru and the Netherlands. This study explores the 

private sector related to urban mobility and serves as a starting point for future more 

exhaustive studies. Its content will also help in drafting a road map. 

2. Methodology 

As a theoretical framework, this study combines the green growth, circular economy, and 

degrowth approaches according to local needs to achieve sustainable mobility in Peru. The 

private sector that provides goods and services related to micromobility, electric transport, 

and last-mile logistics must adapt to these approaches. Opportunities to achieve sustainable 

mobility in Peru must prioritize the following: 

● Human-powered and partly motorized means of transport 

● Transport public, cooperative or collective ownership 

● Vehicle-sharing, vehicle-pooling and public transit systems 

● Local production of transport goods, services, and technologies 

● Transport technologies that require less use of raw materials and fossil fuels 

● The redesign of cities in line with sustainable urban transport 

The study focuses on three fields of sustainable urban mobility defined in line with the 

theoretical approach and according to local needs in Peruvian cities: 

● Micromobility . Human-powered or partly motorized low-speed (up to 25 km/h) small 

devices such as conventional bicycles, cargo bikes, electric bicycles, electric kick 

scooters, electric unicycles, electric two-wheeled self-balancing vehicles, and electric 

roller skates 

● Electric transport . Transport vehicles that make use of one or more electric motors 

and receive electricity from a sole source or in combination with another energy 

source (hybrids), whether the electrical energy comes from a rechargeable energy 

storage system (such as a battery) or directly from the grid (for instance through an 

overhead wire) 

● Last-mile logistics . The distribution of goods to micro-retailers and home delivery by 

light, relatively small, and environmentally and human-friendly vehicles 

This study combined the following strategies to collect data: a review of various sources; 

twenty-three interviews with experts and representatives of private and public, national and 

foreign, and multilateral organizations; an online workshop with seven private organizations 

4related to micromobility; and a mission to the Netherlands which allowed meeting Dutch 

sustainable mobility actors. 

3. Context 

During the COVID-19 pandemic, cycle and PMD sales rose initially but then fell. The 

overdemand turned into an oversupply. The pandemic also negatively affected the 

micromobility vehicle-sharing systems. The rise of micromobility has also benefited the 

consulting and construction sectors since local governments demand services to develop 

policies, regulations, and infrastructure. However, working with the public sector has become 

so unattractive that some practitioners and companies prefer to offer their services to private 

or foreign organizations. Other sectors with the potential to provide services to grow 

micromobility are insurance, manufacturing, and maintenance. The micromobility market is 

sensitive to the regulatory framework and its enforcement. It is necessary to implement 

mechanisms that ensure the quality of products and reduce the illegal and informal markets. 

The Peruvian State is developing the regulatory framework for the massive introduction of 

EVs and charging infrastructure. Although electric transport is a broad field, the visions and 

actions of some public and private organizations tend toward light vehicles and individual 

use and property. Studies' recommendations do not consider vehicle-sharing schemes. 

During the last years, sales on BEV have not substantially increased. It is necessary to set 

clear goals related to the minimum number of BEVs required to enter the country so the local 

market could benefit from an economy of scale. But it is also essential to estimate the 

maximum desired vehicle fleet in cities and prioritize investments in shared use of cars and 

public transit systems to avoid the adverse effects of car dependency. The Peruvian market 

is also composed of locally manufactured vehicles. The use of electric buses for public 

transit is in its infancy. In 2020, the first battery-electric bus partially manufactured in the 

country was launched. 

Some recent studies focus on last-mile logistics within Lima and Callao, but very few on the 

distribution to micro-retail stores and home delivery. There is a lack of proper local 

regulation, which contrasts with the fast development of private initiatives to address 

consumers' demands. The COVID-19 pandemic has significantly increased home deliveries, 

and several companies have benefited from the increase in online shopping. Micro-retailing 

generates problems and challenges in managing the last leg of freight distribution in 

Peruvian cities. These problems result in low competitiveness, and the increased costs 

associated with failures in the logistics system impact the final costs of goods and services. 

Developing proper infrastructures such as micro-warehouses, dark stores, collection points, 

and loading and unloading zones is necessary. 

4. Regulatory framework 

Since 2019, the Peruvian National Government has produced laws and regulations, while 

municipalities have established ordinances in cities related to micromobility. Both have 

addressed cycles and PMDs as two different groups. Municipalities began to regulate the 

use of PMDs in line with three simultaneous processes: the arrival of electric kick scooter 

5rental companies to Lima, the emergence of micromobility stores, and the increase of 

micromobility users. Three municipalities within the province of Lima led the development of 

ordinances. Several companies have faced regulatory gaps, unclear regulation, and different 

local regulatory frameworks within the same territory. 

The Peruvian National Government is preparing the regulatory framework for developing 

electric transport. Studies carried out by the Peruvian State focus mainly on public transport. 

Nonetheless, recently approved national regulations on charging infrastructure focus 

primarily on light vehicles and, to a lesser extent, on public transit, while vehicle-sharing 

systems are absent. In Lima and Callao, some technical standards to introduce hybrid 

electric buses in public transit corridors in future tender processes are under development. 

Local government regulations are vital to securing the distribution of goods to micro-retailers 

and homes. The juxtaposition of local regulations harms the private sector's development. 

Logistics operators that carry out their activities in more than one district of the same 

province must deal with different rules. Last-mile logistics rules are dispersed in multiple 

sub-metropolitan municipal regulatory frameworks that employ different typologies and 

criteria. As a result, some experts and studies recommend unifying and simplifying the 

regulatory framework. In addition to local juxtaposed regulations, there are regulatory gaps 

and a lack of a national policy on last-mile logistics. 

5. Actor analysis 

The study classifies the most relevant actors in groups. About micromobility, the main actors 

are the National Government, local governments, manufacturing and selling businesses, 

repair and maintenance services, vehicle-sharing and vehicle rentals companies, consulting 

firms, and cooperation agencies and multilateral organizations. Concerning electric 

transport, critical actors are the National Government, vehicle importers, sales stores and 

manufacturers, vehicle rental companies, associations, consulting firms, charging 

infrastructure technology providers and institutional customers, and cooperation agencies 

and multilateral organizations. Finally, the most relevant actors in last-mile logistics are the 

National Government, local governments, logistics operators, vehicle rental companies, 

vehicle sale businesses, consulting firms, higher education institutions, and cooperation 

agencies and multilateral organizations. 

6. Initiatives 

The study identified key initiatives that have taken place over the last five years (2017-2022) 

in the three fields of study. These initiatives involve local private organizations, multilateral 

organizations, foreign cooperation agencies for development, and the Peruvian Government. 

Although the study found several initiatives in urban mobility, twenty of them were 

considered most relevant for the three fields of study according to the theoretical approach. 

67. Economic findings 

The findings show the need to allocate more budget to cover the micromobility infrastructure 

gap in Peruvian urban settlements. There is a considerable difference between the local 

governments' budgets and the costs of developing cycling infrastructure in Peruvian cities. 

One way to deal with this shortage of financial resources for the development of 

micromobility is to receive financial contributions from foreign organizations. Although the 

development of cycle tracks and parking is essential when fostering traveling on cycles and 

PMDs, the success of micromobility depends on redesigning the entire urban road networks 

of each city and the investment in the transport system. The main risk of not investing in the 

required micromobility infrastructure is the reduction of micromobility travels. Cities would 

lose micromobility benefits, such as improvements in health, air quality, road safety, and 

household expenses. 

Developing electric transport requires significant investments in charging infrastructure and 

electric vehicles. Although not explicitly focused on cities, some studies provide relevant 

data about the investments necessary to develop the electric charging infrastructure 

network. These studies also identify several benefits of investing in electrifying the transport 

sector. Benefits include savings in operating and maintenance costs, improved health, 

increased productivity, and reduced accidents. Not investing in the transition towards electric 

transport could bring several negative consequences to Peruvian society, such as a greater 

vulnerability to fossil fuel scarcity and increasing GHG emissions. 

The most comprehensive study in last-mile logistics with infrastructure cost estimations for 

Lima and Callao comes from 2016. It seems that the COVID-19 pandemic changed the 

dynamics of urban logistics due to physical distancing, electronic commerce, and home 

delivery growth, which could have affected the findings and proposals of the 2016 study. 

Although financial information related to building intermodal logistics platforms exists, no 

investment estimations regarding infrastructure for distribution to micro-retailers and home 

delivery were found, such as costs of micro-hubs, micro-warehouses, dark stores, pickup 

points, and on-street loading and unloading zones. The Peruvian State must rapidly invest in 

enhancing last-mile logistics infrastructure. Otherwise, cities will be more affected by 

congestion, pollution, and road accidents in the coming years. 

8. Sustainable mobility in the Netherlands 

Today, Dutch cities lead urban cycling in European countries and are a model for other 

countries. In line with the growth of cycle journeys, the cycling industry also flourished in 

Dutch cities. Several companies produce and sell a variety of cycles and provide services 

and products related to planning, design, and management. Other organizations focus on 

defending and promoting the cycling culture locally and abroad. Unlike bikes, the Dutch 

Government does not foster PMD use, particularly electric kick scooters. 

Although other European countries have a more developed automotive industry, the 

Netherlands stands out due to its research on electric and hybrid vehicles, particularly 

driving systems, batteries, lightweight materials, and automated transport systems. The 

7expansion of electric transport in the Netherlands seems to come from EV rental companies 

rather than individual purchases. Dutch companies provide a variety of goods and services, 

including EV-sharing systems, electric charging infrastructure, data management, and 

certification of electric charging solutions. 

The Netherlands has a recognized experience in urban logistics, for instance, in setting 

requirements such as emission standards and maximum freight vehicle dimensions to 

operate in certain areas. An entire ecosystem of Dutch organizations has emerged, covering 

various key aspects of last-mile logistics, such as software services and data management, 

provision of small electric commercial vehicles, and freight vehicle eco-driving training. In 

recent years, the interest in cargo bikes has come back due to new electric storage 

technologies and global concerns about GHG emissions related to transportation. Some 

companies offer innovative and large loadspace cargo bikes. 

9. Bilateral collaboration opportunities 

Potential areas for private sector development and bilateral collaboration for the 

development of sustainable urban mobility in Peru are classified according to each field of 

study. 

Opportunities in micromobility are cycle design and manufacturing, cycle facility and policy 

planning and design, cycle and PMD flow management and monitoring, micromobility 

infrastructure development, regulatory framework development, vehicle-sharing systems 

development, micromobility vehicle parking systems, micromobility and public transit 

integration, promotion of micromobility, association of service and goods providers, and 

research on micromobility. Opportunities in electric transport are vehicle-sharing systems 

development, public transit systems development, data management for decision making, 

electric charging infrastructure development, electric transport actors coalition, 

manufacturing, regulatory and policy framework development, and subsidies and loans. 

Opportunities in last-mile logistics are logistics infrastructure development, regulatory and 

policy framework development, urban logistics in cargo bikes and PMDs, last-mile logistics 

management, driving training, and teaching and research centers cooperation. The road 

map will explore these opportunities in more detail.

12 1. Introduction 

Repeatedly, the inhabitants of Lima have identified public transit as one of the most critical 

problems that affect their quality of life 1. Likewise, the PNTU 2 identifies various problems 

related to mobility in cities, such as the poor state of the infrastructure, excessive travel 

times, insecurity, and low quality of service. Thus, the problems of mobility and urban 

transport have been discussed and studied by various specialists and public and private 

organizations, national and foreign, for the last ten years 3. The approach that has been 

gaining relevance to problematize and seek solutions in the sector has been sustainable 

mobility, paying particular attention to non-motorized mobility and public transport. 

In the global context, the "Sixth Assessment Report, Climate Change 2022: Mitigation of 

Climate Change" 4 highlights transportation's significant impact on the environment. In line 

with the above, it highlights the importance of cities investing in non-motorized mobility and 

public transport to reduce GHG emissions. The report pays attention to the relevance of 

electrification and the supply chain. In recent years, the development of policies aimed at 

favoring urban trips on non-motorized means of transport and the transition of the transport 

sector towards technologies less dependent on fossil fuels have gained relevance 5. Three 

fields that have attained particular interest are micromobility, electric transport, and last-mile 

logistics. 

Micromobility, meaning the use of cycles (such as bicycles, electric bicycles, and cargo 

bikes) and PMDs (such as electric kick scooters and electric unicycles), has been growing 

interest in Peru 6. Cycle and PMD shops have experienced a rapid increase in sales during 

the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic, while cities have had to respond to fast growth in 

trips on these means of transport 7 by developing a micromobility-oriented infrastructure 8.

Currently, the Peruvian State is developing the legal framework to implement policies in 

favor of EVs. Although it is still a nascent sector, the public and private sectors have 

developed various proposals and projects. Lastly, last-mile logistics have substantially 

changed due to the COVID-19 pandemic, with the significant increase in electronic 

commerce and home deliveries.

# 2. Methodology 

This chapter has three parts: the theoretical framework of the sustainable mobility approach 

adopted for the project, the definition of the three fields of study, and the data collection 

method. 

# 2.1. Theoretical framework 

Sustainable mobility (often referred to as sustainable transport) is a widely used approach to 

mitigate the effects of climate change, reduce road accidents, improve urban accessibility, 

and enhance public health. Various actors adopt this concept, such as governments, 

intergovernmental organizations, multilateral banks, development cooperation agencies, 

NGOs, and companies. For instance, the United Nations Secretary General's High-Level 

14 Advisory Group on Sustainable Transport defines the approach as follows: "the provision of 

services and infrastructure for the mobility of people and goods—advancing economic and 

social development to benefit today’s and future generations—in a manner that is safe, 

affordable, accessible, efficient, and resilient, while minimizing carbon and other emissions 

and environmental impacts" 9. Another example is the WB approach: "Transport is 

fundamental to supporting economic growth, creating jobs and connecting people to 

essential services such as healthcare or education (...) Expanding sustainable transport 

options, especially in low-income or vulnerable communities, is a powerful way for countries 

to bolster human development and social inclusion" 10 . According to these views, sustainable 

mobility is related to development and economic growth. 

Green growth and circular economy are two approaches gaining attention worldwide. 

According to the OECD, "Green growth means fostering economic growth and development 

while ensuring that natural assets continue to provide the resources and environmental 

services on which our well-being relies" 11 . The European Parliament defines circular 

economy as follows: "The circular economy is a model of production and consumption, 

which involves sharing, leasing, reusing, repairing, refurbishing and recycling existing 

materials and products as long as possible. In this way, the life cycle of products is 

extended" 12 . Some authors recommend adopting an "in-spiral economy" approach instead of 

talking about a circular economy 13 . In recent years, a new paradigm that critiques the direct 

relationship between development and economic growth, on the one hand, and wellbeing, 

on the other, has gained ground in the academic world and discussions around the climate 

and energy crises. This paradigm called "degrowth" is a sort of umbrella concept. The IPCC 

mentions the recent degrowth literature since authors address the questionable relationship 

between prosperity and economic growth, the "just transition", and the ecological boundaries 

of the planet 14 . According to Schneider et al., "Sustainable degrowth may be defined as an 

equitable downscaling of production and consumption that increases human wellbeing and 

enhances ecological conditions at the local and global level, in the short and long term. The 

adjective sustainable does not mean that degrowth should be sustained indefinitely (which 

would be absurd) but rather that the process of transition/transformation and the end-state 

should be sustainable in the sense of being environmentally and socially beneficial. The 

paradigmatic proposition of degrowth is therefore that human progress without economic 

growth is possible" 15 .

At first sight, green growth, circular economy, and degrowth seem incompatible. 

Nevertheless, countries such as Peru need to combine the findings and proposals of these 

three approaches to achieve a better quality of life. According to a green growth approach, 

sectors such as health, agriculture, education, and transportation require the development of

Based on a sustainable mobility approach, a frequent recommendation by several 

organizations and studies is to invest in active mobility and public transit. Target 11.2 from 

the United Nations General Assembly's SDG 11 indicates expanding public transport in 

cities 16 . One of the GIZ 10 principles for sustainable urban transport is encouraging walking 

and cycling. The IPCC points out something similar: "Strategies for established cities to 

achieve large GHG emissions savings include (...) supporting non-motorised (e.g., walking, 

bicycling) and public transport" 17 . Following degrowth criteria, Cattaneo et al. evaluate 

different urban mobility options, including non-motorized transportation and public transit 18 .

They explain that private motorized vehicles are undesirable due to their social and 

environmental impacts, a persistent statement in sustainable mobility literature. But the 

authors add the following: although public transport is a desirable option, it "depends on 

long-term investment in costly infrastructure [and] cannot contribute sufficiently to the rapid 

change of mobility patterns that climate change and pandemic emergencies indicate is 

required" 19 . Thus, "attention must be given primarily to active and hybrid options, and those 

that operate on a sharing/pooling basis" 20 . Finally, the authors argue for collective property 

and sharing instead of a corporate and individual property 21 .

Sustainable transport development is only possible when cities change into more 

sustainable urban patterns. For instance, promoting walking and cycling is a lost cause in 

low urban concentration territories. Therefore, city design and urban transport policies 

should work together. But change is required not only in the urban design field since urban 

planning and management changes are also necessary. Savini critiques the current planning 

practices and suggests ways to adapt cities to degrowth goals 22 . The author proposes 

autonomy, meaning the "community's capacity to independently provide biophysical, 

material, and social resources for its survival and prosperity" 23 . Within the transport industry, 

autonomy could mean progressively reducing dependence on foreign transport technologies 

and products while developing a local transport industry able to respond to local needs.

growth, circular economy, and degrowth approaches according to local needs. The private

# 2.2. Fields of study 

Micromobility is a growing sector with no consensus over its definition. The ITDP addresses 

micromobility as follows: "Micromobility, though not yet universally defined, has been used to 

refer to a number of different modes. In this report, we use the term ‘electric micromobility’ to 

refer to electric-powered modes of transport that are low-speed (comparable to a bicycle), 

small, lightweight, and typically used for short distance trips. These include primarily electric 

bicycles and standing e-scooters, but also other small electric devices, and can be shared or 

personally owned" 27 . Although micromobility is commonly associated with electrical-powered 

devices, it is not strictly limited to theme. In a broader sense, micromobility includes all those 

human-powered or partly motorized low-speed small devices, such as conventional bicycles, 

cargo bikes, electric bicycles, electric kick scooters, electric unicycles, electric two-wheeled 

self-balancing vehicles, and electric roller skates. 

Electric transport is commonly associated with BEVs and HEVs, mainly light vehicles, such 

as electric cars. For instance, the German Federal Ministry for Digital and Transport says 

that "The term electric mobility (also known as eMobility) stands for forms of movement 

using electric motors. Cars, buses, commercial vehicles, trains, bicycles and motorcycles, 

ships and smaller aircraft – all can be powered by electric energy. An electrically powered 

vehicle within the meaning of the Electric Mobility Act is an all-battery electric vehicle, an 

externally chargeable hybrid electric vehicle or a fuel cell powered vehicle" 28 . The field of 

electric transport is much broader than BEVs or HEVs and includes all those vehicles that 

operate partially or entirely based on electricity. In other words, electric transport is not only 

about vehicles achieving a certain degree of autonomy through energy storage devices,

Last-mile logistics is the last leg of the supply chain. As part of city freight distribution, 

logistics operators provide goods to micro-retailers and homes. Conventional freight 

transport vehicles, such as trucks, can negatively impact dense parts of urban settlements 

causing road accidents, pollution, and damage to public spaces. Due to the increase in 

electronic commerce and home delivery, and the impact of freight transport GHG emissions 

on Climate Change, last-mile logistics has emerged as a field of interest. In the Netherlands, 

the use of cargo bikes and small EVs for good distribution is an increasing practice with the 

support of the Dutch Government. A survey conducted by CityChangerCargoBike 29 ,

supported by the European Union's Horizon 2020 program, shows that the cargo bike 

industry is a growing sector. From a sustainable mobility point of view, urban logistics 

vehicles for distribution to micro-retail stores and home delivery should be light, relatively 

small, and human and environmentally friendly.

# 3. Context 

This chapter analyzes the local context of urban mobility, emphasizing the business 

environment of the three fields of study. 

# 3.1. Micromobility 

With the arrival of PMDs (mainly electric kick scooters) to Peruvian cities, 2019 was a crucial 

year for the development of micromobility in Peru. The National Government and 

municipalities started to produce a more precise and complete regulatory framework related 

to cycles and PMDs based on laws, regulations, manuals, and ordinances. In the private 

sector, several ventures took place in Lima: three electric kick scooter rental startups with 

foreign private capital (Movo, Grin, and Lime) started to operate in some areas of Lima, and 

a bicycle-sharing system (CityBike Lima), in Miraflores, a high-income district. It is essential 

to note that these systems were preceded by the public bicycle program San Borja en Bici, 

launched in 2012 and managed by the Municipalidad de San Borja (Municipality of San 

Borja), which involved private economic participation based on financing infrastructure by 

advertising 30 .

While the COVID-19 pandemic played a central role in the rise of sales and use of cycles 

and PMDs, it also negatively affected the vehicle-sharing businesses and micromobility 

shops. In the year before the pandemic, shared systems generated a lot of interest from the 

community. Still, the actions adopted by the Peruvian State during the outbreak, which 

included mandatory social isolation, damaged the business schemes of the electric kick 

scooter rentals ventures. Initially, the startups tried to reformulate their business strategies, 

but soon everything ended with the departure of the three companies from the country. Of 

the four systems that began operations in 2019, only CityBike Lima survived. According to 

some workshop participants and interviewees, the second half of 2021 meant a setback for 

micromobility shops' sales. The initial enthusiasm of micromobility shops and new users 

during the first months of the pandemic flooded the local market with micromobility devices. 

The overdemand suddenly turned into an oversupply of imported vehicles. Additional 

external factors hit the local industry: the global energy crisis and the mobility restrictions in 

several countries affected by the pandemic involved increasing the prices of certain raw 

materials and freight rates.

Other sectors with the potential to grow are insurance, manufacturing, and maintenance. 

While other countries' local markets offer insurance products to micromobility users (such as 

accident and vehicle insurance), Peruvian companies would not be keen on providing these 

products due to high road accidents and robbery rates. The local cycle manufacturing 

industry is relatively small. Some small businesses manufacture cycles for the local market, 

such as JANKO - Ecobicicletas, which offers bicycles with bamboo frames, and Cargo Bike 

Perú, which manufactures bicycles and cargo bikes on demand. Other businesses produce 

for foreign markets, such as Marino Bike, which makes custom bicycle frames. Larger 

national private companies, such as Modasa - Motores Diesel Andinos SA, which owns the 

brand ZAFIRO, are part of the market. ATU designed an affordable bicycle prototype to be 

produced with local manufacturers, but the project halted during the COVID-19 pandemic 31 .

A growing local market creates several opportunities: the more significant the growth of the 

cycle market, the greater the potential to flourish cycle maintenance and repair shops. 

Finally, the micromobility market is sensitive to the regulatory framework and its 

enforcement. Consulted businesses pointed out that sellers who offer low-quality products 

harm businesses that comply with regulations, such as cycle and PMD shops that import 

vehicles designed to comply with the maximum speed allowed and safe accessories. In this 

sense, it is necessary to implement mechanisms that ensure the quality of products and 

reduce the illegal and informal markets.

Currently, the Peruvian State is developing the regulatory framework for the massive 

introduction of EVs and charging infrastructure. The private sector is accompanying this 

process through two automotive industry associations that, in recent years, have drawn up

charging infrastructure plan in 2022. Although electric transport is a broad field (which 

includes electric buses and trains), the visions and actions of some public and private 

organizations tend toward light vehicles and individual use and property. While the planning 

proposals from the private sector consider public transit, the proposed incentives and 

investments for EVs and HEVs acquisition and use prioritize personal use. If the Peruvian

problems related to EVs will replace current issues related to ICEVs. Even though EVs 

reduce GHG emissions related to car usage, they do not solve issues such as the high

ecosystems related to car manufacture 32 . It is essential to point out that the study's 

recommendations prepared for AAP do not consider vehicle-sharing schemes, a void also 

found in the priorities identified in a study published by Osinergmin 33 .

they reduce the number of units produced and private purchases by individuals. In addition, 

they also optimize the use of cars and public spaces. Toyota's recent initiative, KINTO

# 3.3. Last-mile logistics 

Several studies have been carried out on logistics in Peru during the last two decades, 

particularly in Lima and Callao. These studies address various issues: road freight transport 

services, freight vehicles flow, freight volume, rail freight transport, freight routes, logistics 

corridors, storage and distribution centers, truck centers, loading and unloading zones, and 

Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS). Some interviewees consider a study published in 

2016 the most complete 40 . It identifies the most important previous studies that address the 

urban distribution of goods to some extent: eight addressing the national territory, and eight 

focused on  Lima and Callao. Some recent studies focus on last-mile logistics within Lima 

and Callao 41 .

Of the three fields addressed by this study, last-mile logistics related to the delivery to 

micro-retail stores and homes is the one with less research and planning studies. An 

exception is the "Observatorio de Lima" 42 (a local observatory from the Universidad del 

Pacífico) which has some studies and projects that address these topics. There is also a 

lack of proper local regulation, which contrasts with the fast development of private initiatives 

to address consumers' demands. 

According to some people consulted and publications, the COVID-19 pandemic would have 

significantly increased home deliveries. A news published in February 2021 indicates that, 

according to Touch Task, the home delivery industry grew by 250% during the pandemic and 

that roughly 50,000 people delivered purchases either on motorcycles or bicycles 43 . The 

number of app-based delivery workers would have reached about 10,000 by October 2020 44 .

Home delivery growth is related to electronic commerce growth. The 2021 annual report of 

the Cámara Peruana de Comercio Electrónico (Peruvian Chamber of Electronic Commerce) 

shows how electronic commerce during 2020, despite an initial drop in the weeks following 

the start of the social isolation due to the pandemic, exceeded pre-pandemic growth 

expectations 45 . Several companies have benefited from the increase in online shopping. For 

example, Rappi, the company that entered the local market in 2018, grew in services and 

users during the pandemic 46 . It is not a Peruvian phenomenon but a global one. And

Last-mile logistics is a reasonably complex field with peculiar characteristics in Peruvian 

cities. Unlike other cities, where large stores (such as supermarkets and shopping malls) are 

the primary consumer attraction centers, micro-retail is the predominant way of consumption 

in Peruvian cities such as Lima. Micro-retailing generates problems and challenges in 

managing the last leg of freight distribution. According to consulted experts and 

organizations, as well as the review of the existing literature, the following are recurrent 

problems: increases in logistical costs related to failed deliveries, heterogeneous regulations 

within a city that make it difficult for logistics operators to comply with rules, road congestion 

due to the use of lorries that make multiple deliveries, occupation of scarce public spaces by 

large freight vehicles, absence of well-designed loading and unloading zones, informality of 

various logistics operators that affects the performance of those that comply with established 

standards, use of informal warehouses in the absence of adequate infrastructure, recurrent 

violations of logistics regulations that impact the economy of businesses, and inappropriate 

behaviors of freight vehicle drivers. The problems mentioned above result in low 

competitiveness, and the increased costs associated with failures in the logistics system 

impact the final costs of goods and services. 

Even though the problems, there are several opportunities to develop the private sector of 

last-mile logistics. Urban logistics operators can acquire new vehicles with cleaner 

technologies and smaller dimensions that are safer for pedestrians and non-motorized 

vehicle users. It is necessary to develop proper infrastructures such as micro-warehouses, 

dark stores, collection points, and loading and unloading zones. Logistics operators can 

introduce new software and technologies to manage their fleet. There is also room for 

collaboration between the academic sector and municipalities to improve the conditions of 

urban logistics in local settings 47 .

Developing electric transport requires significant investments in charging infrastructure and 

electric vehicles. Although not explicitly focused on cities, some studies provide relevant 

data about the investments necessary to develop the electric charging infrastructure 

network 113 . An IADB study that addresses transport decarbonization by 2050 pays attention 

to electrification and infrastructure development. It estimates the additional investments 

related to infrastructure at around USD 47,000 million 114 . As part of an analysis of the impact 

on tax collection, the study published by AAP estimates the cost of setting up a charging 

station (taking a 110 kWh charger as a reference and considering issues such as installation, 

assembly, and engineering) at USD 32,290 115 . A study released by AEDIVE PERU estimates 

the cost of implementing charging stations with two types of energy supply, including the 

connection permits and installation expenses. A charging station with a 120 kW charger and 

a photovoltaic solar plant connected to the grid without batteries would cost USD 232,548 116 .

A charging station with a 120 kW charger and a photovoltaic solar plant connected to the 

grid with 2 hours of batteries would cost USD 486,681 117 . According to AEDIVE PERU, the 

total investment of installing 24 charging stations along the Panamericana Norte and 

Panamericana Sur roads (roads that connect the northern and southern Peruvian borders) 

would require an investment of USD 16.5 million. To develop electric transport, the study 

published by AAP recommends the creation of an Electric Transport Fund to centralize the 

management of funds 118 .

# 7.3. Last-mile logistics 

Whereas some studies in micromobility and electric transport that estimate infrastructure 

costs come from 2020-to 2022, the most comprehensive study in last-mile logistics with 

infrastructure cost estimations for Lima and Callao comes from 2016, a study prepared for 

MTC by Táryet and Teirlog Ingeniería. According to the experts' opinion, the COVID-19 

pandemic changed the dynamics of urban logistics due to physical distancing, electronic 

commerce, and home delivery growth, which could have affected the findings and proposals 

of the 2016 study. Thus, although this study estimates the required investment for building 

three intermodal logistics platforms (roughly USD 82 million each 121 ), it does not estimate 

additional facilities costs related to distribution to micro-retailers and home delivery: 

micro-hubs, micro-warehouses, dark stores, pickup points, and on-street loading and 

unloading zones. 

Unlike electric transport, whose success relays on substantial public investments in 

infrastructure (since BEVs cannot operate without charging points), last-mile logistics grow 

with or without public investment plans (since people, no matter what, demand goods not 

locally produced). Access to goods transported to cities from other territories cannot be 

easily replaced in the short term by a local production of such goods. Thus, the Peruvian 

State must rapidly invest in enhancing last-mile logistics infrastructure. Otherwise, cities will 

be more affected by congestion, pollution, and road accidents in the coming years.

39 8. Sustainable mobility in the Netherlands 

Based on reviewed literature and the mission to the Netherlands, this chapter describes the 

context of sustainable mobility in the Netherlands related to the three fields of study. 

# 8.1. Micromobility 

Dutch cities are well-known for their achievements in urban cycling policies. As in many 

parts of Europe, the motorization of the urban milieu during the 20th century affected bicycle 

usage. A rapid decline came from the 1950s, recovery took place in later decades, and a 

significant shift in infrastructure arrived from the 1990s onwards with the national 

transportation plan 122 . Today, Dutch cities lead urban cycling in European countries and are a 

model for other countries 123 . In line with the growth of cycle journeys, the cycling industry 

also flourished in Dutch cities. Currently, several companies produce and sell a variety of 

cycles, such as conventional urban bicycles, electric bicycles, utility bicycles, and cargo 

bikes for transporting goods and people. Examples of companies and brands are Gazelle, 

Sensa, Veloretti, VanMoof, Volare Bicycles, Azor Bike, Urban Arrow, and Bakfiets.nl The 

interest of foreign companies in acquiring Dutch cycling businesses shows the economic 

success of this industry. In January 2022, Accell Group, a former Dutch company that owns 

several cycle brands, announced its acquisition by the American KKR & Co. 

The development of urban cycling also requires the provision of other services and products 

related to planning, design, and management. Companies like Connection Systems and 

Cycle Data BV provide services for monitoring cycling flow. IP Parking and Monit data 

provide solutions for bicycle parking. Companies such as Toogethr develop app-based 

solutions for urban cycling. And advising on infrastructure development is provided by 

companies like Witteveen+Bos. Other organizations focus on defending and promoting the 

cycling culture locally (such as the Fietsersbond, which represents and protects the interests 

of the Dutch cycling community) and abroad (such as the Dutch Cycling Embassy and 

BYCS). Unlike cycles, the Dutch Government does not foster PMD use, particularly electric 

kick scooters. This context contrasts with other European countries that opened their doors 

to electric kick scooters and other innovative micromobility devices during the last years.

# 8.3. Last-mile logistics

Amsterdam, such as the Amsterdam Logistic Cityhub, acquired by CTP in 2021. Last-mile 

logistics is one of the concerns of the Amsterdam Economic Board that, together with 

various other organizations, fosters the adoption of cleaner vehicles (such as electric vans 

and cargo bikes) and smart driving. An entire ecosystem of Dutch organizations has

This chapter presents the most relevant information for the project's next stage. The 

conclusions are focused on the criteria to identify areas for Peruvian urban mobility 

development and bilateral collaboration between Peru and the Netherlands and the key 

opportunities in this regard according to each field of study. 

It is necessary to combine the green growth, circular economy, and degrowth approaches 

according to local needs to achieve sustainable mobility in Peru. The private sector that 

provides goods and services related to micromobility, electric transport, and last-mile 

logistics must adapt to the approaches. The following criteria based on the reviewed theory 

are crucial to identifying opportunities for bilateral collaboration between Peru and the 

Netherlands on sustainable mobility development: 

● C1. Human-powered and partly motorized means of transport are, in general, more 

desirable for urban journeys than motorized vehicles. 

● C2. Transport public, cooperative or collective ownership is a preferable option to a 

private for-profit or single-person ownership. 

● C3. Vehicle-sharing, vehicle-pooling and public transit systems are more sustainable 

options than personal motorized vehicle use. 

● C4. As far as possible, local production must replace imported transport goods, 

services and technologies in the long term. 

● C5. Urban transport must stop using polluting technologies to start using 

technologies that require less use of raw materials and fossil fuels. 

● C6. The redesign of cities should go hand in hand with the shift toward sustainable 

urban transport.
