Markdown Content:
_by Linda Casey, Nadeanne Haftl, Kevin McNellis, Robin Parkinson, Peter Quist, and Denise Roth Barber | 2012-04-12_

#### Key Findings

In the 2009–2010 elections, more than 16,724 candidates raised $2.5 billion in their campaigns for state offices. The record-setting elections did not come as a surprise, as political parties battled to be at the helm during the 2011 re-drawing of legislative and congressional districts.

In addition to money raised by candidates, state political parties and legislative caucuses raised $627.1 million, while committees supporting and opposing ballot measures raised $628.3 million. And lawmakers who were not even up for election raised $103.3 million. All told, $3.8 billion was raised around state elections during the 2009–2010 elections.

A closer look at the $3.8 billion raised in 2009–2010 state campaigns reveals:

**100 candidates raised nearly half of the money.**Of the $2.5 billion raised by 2009–2010 candidates, $1.2 billion (46 percent) was raised by just 100 candidates, 77 of whom were running for governor or lieutenant governor.

Meg Whitman, who lost her bid for governor of California, led the pack with $176.7 million, thanks to her own infusion of $144.2 million. Florida Gov. Rick Scott was a far distant second, raising $67.4 million during the open gubernatorial contest. Jerry Brown, who won the California governor’s race, came in third, with $40.6 million.

**The candidates themselves****made up the largest group of donors.**Candidates provided $469.1 million of their own money, or 18 percent of the $2.5 billion raised by those who ran for election. Political parties, and finance, insurance, and real estate donors, comprised the two next-largest sectors, giving $210.3 million and $208 million, respectively.

**Republicans and Democrats****were neck and neck in fundraising**. Republican candidates surpassed Democratic candidates, raising $1.35 billion to the Democrats’ $1.14 billion. However, Democratic state party committees and legislative caucuses raised $344 million for 2009–2010 races, which is 21 percent more than the $283 million raised by their Republican counterparts.

**Judicial elections attracted millions in campaign cash.**Candidates who ran for high court in 2009–10 raised $26.6 million, while those who ran for intermediate appellate court raised $18.5 million. Partisan judicial races attracted $33.5 million, which is nearly four times the $8.7 million raised in nonpartisan races.

**Office-holders not up for election raised a lot of cash.**About 2,000 state office-holders who were not up for election in 2009–10 raised $103.3 million. Most of that ($71.8 million) was raised by state senators likely saving up for their reelection bids in 2012. Another $16.9 million was raised by sitting governors.

#### Campaigns for State Legislatures, 2009–2010

State legislatures saw an extreme shift in power after the November 2010 elections. Post-election day, Republicans had control of 25 state legislatures, nearly double the 14 they controlled prior to the election.1 Democrats experienced an opposite shift, from control of 27 legislatures prior to the election to just 16 after the election.

Nearly 15,000 candidates who ran for 6,309 legislative seats up for election in 2009 and 2010 raised almost $1 billion.

State ballots across the country had 1,171 state senate seats sought by 2,907 candidates who raised $323.1 million ($111,156 average per candidate). Predictably, races for house/assembly seats were less expensive: 11,892 candidates who ran for 5,138 house/assembly seats raised $667.6 million in contributions, for an average of $56,142 per candidate.

The median amount raised by senate candidates who ran in the general elections was $43,098, more than double the $21,150 median raised by those who ran for the state house/assembly seats.

The 6,625 Democratic candidates raised $522.1 million and 7,016 Republican candidates raised just over $459.7 million. Democrats raised an average of $78,685, while Republicans raised an average of $65,304.

Nationwide, 73 percent of the seats were contested in the general election. In the uncontested 27 percent, about three-quarters of the candidates who faced no competition in the general election had been uncontested during the primary elections as well.

The 3,171 candidates who were eliminated during the primary elections raised an average of $24,806, a median of $6,325.

The average cost of legislative races varied greatly among the states. California’s races averaged $842,919 for senate winners and $711,984 for assembly winners, while in New Hampshire, general-election winners averaged $55,806 and $773 respectively. Almost 300 general-election winners nationwide did not report any contributions, 232 of whom had run for seats in New Hampshire.

State senate races are typically more expensive than house races. The average senate race cost $111,156, while the average house race cost $56,142. Among the states that held both house and senate elections, only Indiana was an exception to that rule, with an average of $82,032 raised for senate races compared to $89,918 for house races.

Winners in the general elections raised $675.3 million, which is about three-quarters of all the money raised for general election races. Those who lost in the general elections raised $232.5 million.

**Legislators Not Up for Election**

The 1,068 lawmakers who were not up for election in 2009–10 nonetheless raised nearly $80 million. Sitting senators in 34 states, who typically have a four-year term, raised $71.8 million. State representatives in the three states that also have four-year terms—Louisiana, Mississippi, and North Dakota—raised almost $6 million. Incumbents not up for election in two states led the averages: 15 Texas state senators raised an average of $636,774 each; 25 Pennsylvania senators raised an average of $357,675 each.

#### Gubernatorial Contests, 2009–2010

Thirty-nine states held gubernatorial elections in 2009 and 2010: two (New Jersey and Virginia) held their elections in 2009, and 37 held elections in 2010. Candidates in those two elections raised $1.2 billion, which is 20 percent more than the $968 million raised during the comparable 2005–06 elections. Candidates in the 2009 elections raised $101.7 million, while candidates in the 2010 elections raised $1.1 billion, the first time these races ever exceeded $1 billion. Incumbent governors and lieutenant governors not up for election in 2009 and 2010 raised an additional $16.9 million.

Overall, Democratic candidates for governor and lieutenant governor raised $451.7 million, which is 64 percent less than the $741.1 million raised by Republican candidates for governor and lieutenant governor. Third-party governor and lieutenant governor candidates raised $20.8 million. Of interest, the previous comparable election (2005–06) saw very different fundraising totals for these offices: Democratic candidates had raised more, at $481.4 million, while Republicans had raised less, at $458.8 million. Third-party candidates also had raised more in 2005–06, at $27.8 million.

Democrats won both the governor and lieutenant governor seats in nine states in 2009 and 2010, while Republicans won both seats in 21 states. After the 2010 elections, 10 states shifted from Democratic to Republican governors (two additional states, New Jersey and Virginia, switched from Democratic to Republican governors after the 2009 election). Democrats held the governor’s seat in 20 states compared to Republicans in 29 states, and one Independent won the seat in Rhode Island.

Vermont and Arkansas elected a Democratic governor and a Republican lieutenant governor. Rhode Island elected an Independent governor and a Democratic lieutenant governor.

The 2010 California gubernatorial race attracted $251.9 million, which totals 21 percent of the money raised in all of the 2009–10 gubernatorial elections. 70 percent of that California total was raised by Meg Whitman. The remaining 30 percent was spread among the other 22 candidates who vied for the coveted seat. The winner, former governor and then-attorney general Jerry Brown, raised $40.6 million, which is 16 percent of the total raised by all gubernatorial candidates in California.

The California governor’s race was one of ten wherein the candidate who raised the most money did not emerge the victor. Seven of those ten gubernatorial races were open, three of which were won by incumbents of other state offices who were seeking the higher seat. Of the three races where an incumbent sought reelection, two were won by the incumbent and one was won by the challenger.

Overall, incumbent governors who sought reelection in 2010 had a 73 percent success rate, with 11 of the 15 incumbents retaining their jobs. This was lower than both the 93 percent success rate experienced in 2006 and the 76 percent success rate in 2002. Candidates who had previously held other state offices had a success rate of only 14 percent when running for governor, with 9 of the 66 candidates winning their races. This is slightly lower than the 2005–06 success rate, when 20 percent of candidates who had previously held other state offices won their gubernatorial race.

While all 39 gubernatorial races were contested, only 27 (69 percent) were monetarily competitive during the general elections, compared to 71 percent in 2006, and 68 percent in 2002.2

Two races were won by candidates whose opponents vastly outraised them. In California, Jerry Brown defeated Meg Whitman in the open race, despite raising significantly less than Whitman: $40.6 million compared to Whitman’s $176.7 million. New Jersey Republican Chris Christie ousted then-incumbent Democratic Gov. Jon Corzine, despite raising $5.8 million to Corzine’s $30.6 million.

Outside of self-funding, the Republican Governors Association (RGA) and the Democratic Governors Association (DGA) were the top two contributors to gubernatorial races. The RGA contributed $24 million to 24 candidates in 20 states, and the DGA contributed $10.5 million to 12 candidates in 11 states. The amount contributed by the RGA in 2009–10 is the most the organization has given in one election cycle over the last decade. The DGA, on the other hand, fell short of the $14.3 million it had contributed in the 2003–04 election cycle.

Washington, D.C. was the largest source of out-of-state contributions to candidates for governor and lieutenant governor in eight states: Illinois, Iowa, Maryland, New Mexico, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Texas, and Virginia. A majority of the D.C. contributions came from the RGA and the DGA, followed by the Democratic National Committee ($3.5 million), the American Federation of State County and Municipal Employees ($3.2 million), and the Republican National Committee ($2.3 million).

Also of note were two couples who gave generously to several candidates in multiple states. Bob Perry, of Perry Homes in Houston, Texas, and his wife, Doylene, gave $2.6 million to candidates in seven states. More than half of their contributions ($1.5 million) went to Rick Perry in Texas. Wyoming businessman Foster Friess and his wife, Lynnette, contributed $802,460 to gubernatorial candidates in 34 states. Friess contributed the most to Republicans Bill Brady in Illinois and Susana Martinez in New Mexico, at $200,000 each.

#### Judicial Races, 2009–2010

In 2009 and 2010, 72 high court races were on the ballot in 35 states and 253 intermediate appellate court races were decided in 31 states. These races attracted more than $45 million: $26.6 million by those running for high court and $18.5 million by appellate court candidates.

Partisan judicial races attracted $33.5 million—nearly four times the $8.7 million raised in nonpartisan races.

As is typical, donors from the law/lobby sector contributed the most. Their $14.7 million accounted for almost one-third of the money raised by judicial candidates. The candidates themselves accounted for 11 percent of the money raised.

Male candidates accounted for 63 percent of those who ran for judicial offices. As a group, women were generally more successful overall: 73 percent of female candidates were winners compared to 66 percent of male candidates. Of interest, female candidates raised more money on average.

Candidates from an ethnic or racial minority made up just 9 percent of all candidates who ran for a judicial seat. As a group, however, they enjoyed a higher success rate than non-minority candidates. And, when they raised money, they raised sums comparable to non-minority candidates.

See the Institute’s report,Money in Judicial Elections, 2009–2010, for a more detailed analysis.

#### Contributions to Political Party Committees, 2009–2010

The 100 state Republican and Democratic political party committees raised more than $449.2 million in contributions during the 2009–10 elections; 162 legislative caucus committees raised an additional $177.9 million.4

Overall, the 100 state parties raised 2 percent less than they did in the comparable 2005–06 elections. Republican state parties, even though they gained the most seats in the 2009–10 elections, raised 19 percent less than they did in the comparable 2005–06 elections, while Democratic state parties raised 13 percent more. Five states saw a more than 50 percent decrease in contributions to political parties since 2005–06; Delaware had the largest decrease with 79 percent less money contributed to their state political parties in 2010.

#### Who Gave to State Candidates, 2009–2010

Candidates and current office-holders, along with their affiliated committees, made up the largest group of donors, providing $547 million, or 21 percent of the $2.6 billion total raised by all candidates and office-holders not up for election in the 2009–10 election cycle. This represents an increase of 49 percent from the $366.3 million given in the 2005–06 election, the previous comparable election cycle.

This significant increase in candidates giving to themselves can be attributed in large part to Meg Whitman, who put $144.2 million of her own money into her unsuccessful 2010 campaign for governor of California—the most any state or federal candidate is on record for giving.6 B. Thomas Golisano was a distant second, contributing $74.1 million to his own (also unsuccessful) 2002 campaign for governor of New York.The following chart compares the contributions by economic sector 7 to all candidates who ran for state-level offices in 2009–10 elections and in 2005–06 elections.

Donors from the finance, insurance and real estate sector were the next-largest group of contributors, giving $218.7 million in 2009–10. This amounted to 8 percent of the total contributions to candidates nationwide, and was down slightly from the $227.2 million contributed in 2005–06. Political parties followed close behind, with $211.8 million, which is nearly 8 percent of the total contributions and 15 percent more than the $184.5 million given in 2005–06.

It should be noted that public funding of campaigns 8 increased by more than one-third, from $43.7 million in 2005–06 to $60.5 million in 2009–10. The following chart shows the public financing totals provided during the two election cycles in states that have such programs.

The largest increase in funding occurred in Connecticut, which launched a public campaign financing program in 2008 9 that contributed $25.3 million to candidates in 2009–10.New Jersey and Florida saw major reductions from 2006 through 2010. New Jersey, which provides public funding for gubernatorial candidates, saw a drop of $5.7 million in funding, with only one gubernatorial candidate using public funds. Florida’s public financing, available to statewide candidates, dropped by nearly $5 million. In 2010, Florida’s Bill McCollum was the only gubernatorial candidate to use public funds,10 whereas 2006 saw four gubernatorial candidates accept public funding.

#### Ideological Contributors, 2009–2010

Another interesting increase was seen in the ideology/single issue sector, where contributions more than doubled from $37.7 million in 2005–06 to $76.6 million in 2009–10.

The increase is due primarily to the Republican Governors Association (RGA), which gave $46.3 million to gubernatorial candidates and political parties in 27 states in 2009–10. That was more than four times the $9.9 million the RGA gave in 2005–06. Its counterpart, the Democratic Governors Association (DGA), also increased its giving, though not as dramatically. The DGA gave $14.9 million 11 in 19 states, which is a 67 percent increase from the $8.9 million it gave to 27 states in 2005–06.The top 15 recipients of those funds in 2009–10 were all gubernatorial candidates; together they amassed $44 million from ideological or single-issue groups. Illinois Republican gubernatorial candidate Bill Brady was the top recipient in this sector. He received 40 percent of his contributions from these groups, with nearly all of those funds coming from the Republican Governors Association, an organization dedicated to electing Republican governors throughout the country.

#### Ballot Measures, 2009–2010

Voters across the country considered 216 measures on their election ballots in 2009 and 2010. Committees supporting and opposing these measures raised $526.5 million. Because some states allow ballot measures only in regularly scheduled election years, comparing 2009 with other odd years and 2010 with other even years provides the most direct analysis.

A total of $131.4 million was raised around all ballot measures in 2009, thanks in large part to a gambling expansion measure in Ohio and a package of budget-related measures in California. This dwarfed the 2007 total of $55.4 million but fell far short of the 2005 total of $466.2 million raised around 2005 measures.

The $395.1 million raised around 2010 measures around the country fell far short of the $813.7 million raised around measures in 2008 and the $649.4 million raised around measures in 2006. California measures drew the most attention in 2010, garnering 56 percent of the money raised around all statewide measures.

For more information on the money raised around statewide ballot measures in 2009 and 2010, see our 2009–2010 Ballot Measure Overview.

#### Putting the 2009–2010 Elections in Context

Candidates for state offices across the country raised $9.5 billion during the 10-year period 2001 through 2010. The 2009–10 elections created a new high-water mark with $2.5 billion, surpassing the previous record of $2.2 billion set during the comparable 2005–06 elections.

**A small number of candidates raised most of the money.**The top 100 candidates raised $2.6 billion, reaping 28 percent of the $9.5 billion raised in the 10-year period by all candidates. And the top 1,000 candidates, who made up just 2 percent of the 50,010 candidates, raised half of the money ($5.4 billion).

**Candidates and political parties were the main source of funds.**Candidates provided $1.3 billion to fund their own campaigns, while political parties came in second with $854.0 million. Together, they accounted for nearly one-fourth of all the money raised by the candidates from 2001 through 2010.

**Political parties raised billions to get their candidates elected.**State Republican and Democratic political parties raised $2.1 billion from 2001 through 2010, and they raised comparable amounts: Democrats $1.1 billion; Republicans $1.0 billion.

Republican and Democratic legislative caucuses brought in an additional $768.6 million during the 10 years studied. However, given the changing nature of these committees from year to year, it is difficult to make comparisons from one year or election to the next.

**Ballot measures attract billions****.** A staggering $3.1 billion was raised to support or oppose statewide ballot measures from 2004 12 through 2010. Controversial measures on the ballots in 2006 and 2008 addressing same-sex marriage, abortion, gambling, and other hot-button issues attracted the most money. While 2010 set a record for candidates’ fundraising, 2010 ballot measures attracted the fewest contributions of any even-year election and the second-least in the past 7 years.
