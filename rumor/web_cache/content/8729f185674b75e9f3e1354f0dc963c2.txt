Title: Sorry, Trump: Chicago Is Not The ‘Murder Capital Of The World’ — Not Even Close, Data Shows

President Donald Trump has repeatedly called Chicago the "murder capital of the world," but his comments are false, data shows. Credit: Colin Boyle/Block Club Chicago

CHICAGO — While flirting with deploying National Guard troops to Chicago, President Donald Trump has in recent weeks invoked images of murderers running amok and carnage in the city.

“Chicago is a hellhole right now,” Trump said at Tuesday press conference in the Oval Office, saying that the city is the “murder capital of the world.”

The trouble with that statement? It’s not true, according to data from the Chicago Police Department and FBI. The data shows ongoing decreases in violent crime — and far worse homicide rates in other cities.

Trump this week seized on reports that at least eight people were killed and more than 50 were wounded over the Labor Day weekend. He pointed to that bloodshed as evidence that he should send federal troops to Chicago to fight crime — which local officials (and a judge in California) have said would be illegal.

Instead, experts who study crime data say more funding for social safety net and violence prevention programs are what’s needed to continue the downward trend in violence.

Federal data also reveals that Chicago isn’t even among the United States’ most dangerous cities, much less the “murder capital.”

Chicago, which is home to about 2.64 million people, saw about 17 homicides per 100,000 people in 2024. Birmingham, Alabama, with a population of 195,418, recorded about 58 homicides per 100,000 residents in 2024 — more than three times the homicide rate of Chicago, according to FBI crime data.

Birmingham’s homicide rate was the United States’ highest last year, followed by St. Louis and Memphis, Tennessee. In fact, there were 22 major cities with higher homicide rates than Chicago’s, according to FBI data.

“There are just a lot of cities that have higher homicide rates, and do most years,” said John Roman, a senior fellow who studies crime policies with research group NORC at the University of Chicago.

Chicago’s Violent Crime Is Falling
----------------------------------

From the start of this year through Aug. 31, Chicago saw 278 murders — a 31 percent drop from the same period last year, and the lowest at this point since 2014, according to the Police Department.

And this summer, Chicago recorded the fewest homicides since 1965, WBEZ reported this week.

“It’s been such an encouraging year, not just in Chicago, but around the country,” Roman said. “Homicides are down a little more than 30 percent … this year compared to this point last year in Chicago. And nationally, that number is more like 20 percent. So Chicago is doing 50 percent better than the national average.”

Despite these downward trends, Chicago’s homicide rate is used by Trump and other critics to paint the city as a dangerous place.

Recent data analyzed by the nonprofit Council on Criminal Justice found that other violent crime in Chicago has dropped, too, with all categories lower in the first six months of 2025 than during the same period in 2024.

What Could Help More Than The National Guard
--------------------------------------------

Despite the overall downward trend in violent crime, some Chicago neighborhoods are still experiencing disproportionate levels of violence and need resources to address the root causes of that violence, said Lance Williams, a professor at Northeastern Illinois University who works with violence intervention groups across the city.

“For those who understand how violence plays out. We know that most violence incidences, particularly gun-related violence, is something that is spontaneous,” Williams said. “Although individuals may be gang affiliated, violence doesn’t play out along gang lines or gang territory like it did 30 years ago. It’s more interpersonal conflict.”

A person at risk of gun violence is more likely to be shot by a member of their own gang than a rival organization, he said.

“That violence is more interpersonal, and there’s nothing federal troops or law enforcement can really do to get in front of that, right?” Williams said. “That’s something that has to be done with people who are from the community. [People] who understand the lay of the land and who speak the language. And even they find it difficult to get in front of it.”

And data shows that increasing armed police patrols and surveillance alone won’t lead to long-term decreases in crime, Roman said.

“That’s likely to cut crime in the short term. But it’s not a long-term solution,” Roman said. “It’s not sustainable. It’s very expensive. And there are other things that have a much stronger evidence base.

“The big wins are all things that happen outside the criminal justice system.”

A CARE van. Credit: City of Chicago

One evidence-based pilot is Chicago’s Crisis Assistance Response and Engagement initiative, known as CARE, which works to improve how the city responds to 911 calls for mental health crises. But the organization only operates 10:30 a.m.-4 p.m. Monday-Friday across seven police districts.

And money for CARE and other city programs came from federal COVID-19 relief funds that will run out at the end of the year. Roman and Williams said that — plus the Trump Administration’s penchant for freezing, clawing back and cutting funding for safety-net programs — could harm efforts to continue Chicago’s downward trend in violence.

“We saw the largest one-year decline in homicides we’d ever seen in 2023, and then we saw an even larger national decline in 2024. And the year-to-date numbers suggest that there’s an even larger decline in 2025,” Roman said. “A lot of this is simply federal funding that was put in place in response to the COVID pandemic, and huge amounts of this money went to state and local governments.”

Money used to deploy the National Guard troops to Chicago would be better spent investing in long-term social-safety-net programs, Williams said.

“My big concern is this, if those federal troops are deployed to the city’s neighborhoods, then we have another problem on our hands. And I don’t think people are really thinking about this, and they really need to think about how this may escalate things,” Williams said. “I don’t think it’s a good idea at all, I think it’s a total waste of money to deploy federal troops here in the name of addressing violence, right? I think it’s totally misguided.”
