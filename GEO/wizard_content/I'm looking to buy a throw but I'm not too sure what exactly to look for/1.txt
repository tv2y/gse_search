A duvet is only as good as its filling. The filling is what makes a duvet heavenly or utterly appalling.

*   Natural filling such as down and feather are always a great choice. They are highly breathable and allow moisture to escape, creating a soft and warm duvet. Goose and duck are the most available of natural filling duvets.
*   TOG The density of the filling is most commonly measured in togs. Fillings up to 7 tog are lighter and airier, making them ideal for the summer months. Higher togs are more suitable for the colder months.
*   Fill power measures the volume taken up by the filling in the duvet. The volume of the duvet is also an indicator or how fluffy the duvet is going to be.A high fill power means the duvet will keep you warm. The higher, the warmer but also the lighter, airier and more breathable a duvet can be.

Choosing a throw blanket requires balancing purpose, comfort, durability, and style. Evaluate these factors and use the checklist and quick recommendations below to pick the right one.

Primary considerations

*   Purpose

    *   Warmth: choose dense, insulating fabrics (wool, heavy fleece, faux shearling, chunky knit) for cold climates or cozying on a couch.
    *   Lightweight/seasonal use: choose cotton, linen, or lightweight waffle weave for summer or layered use.
    *   Decorative accent: prioritize color, pattern, texture, and edge finish rather than maximum warmth.
    *   Travel/portable: prioritize packable, wrinkle-resistant fabrics (microfiber, travel blankets with nylon backing).

*   Fabric and feel

    *   Wool (merino, lambswool): excellent warmth, breathability, natural odor resistance; can be scratchy unless high grade.
    *   Cashmere: luxurious, very warm and soft; expensive and needs gentle care.
    *   Cotton: breathable, washable, good for year-round and homes with allergies.
    *   Fleece/microfiber: very warm, soft, affordable, quick-drying; can pill over time.
    *   Acrylic/faux fibers: budget-friendly, soft, colorfast but can feel synthetic and trap heat.
    *   Faux fur/sherpa: plush and very cozy; heavier and mostly decorative.
    *   Linen: cool, textured, best for summer or a casual look.
    *   Blends: combine benefits (e.g., wool–acrylic for warmth + durability).

*   Weight, weave and construction

    *   GSM/weight: higher grams-per-square-meter → warmer/heavier. Choose based on desired warmth.
    *   Knit vs woven: chunky knits are cozy and decorative but can stretch/pill; tight weaves are durable and drape well.
    *   Pile height: longer pile (faux fur, sherpa) = more plush but harder to maintain.
    *   Edges: fringed, hemmed, or piped edges affect style and durability.

*   Size and coverage

    *   Typical sizes: 50×60 in (throw), 60×80 in (larger throw/blanket). Match to sofa size and intended use (lap vs full drape).
    *   Consider extra length if you like tucking under legs or sharing with another person.

*   Care and durability

    *   Machine-washable vs dry-clean-only: choose washable for daily use or homes with pets/kids.
    *   Pilling resistance: tighter weaves and higher-quality fibers pill less.
    *   Colorfastness: look for pre-washed/dyed labels to avoid bleeding.
    *   Sun resistance: pigments and fibers that resist fading if used outdoors or in sunlit rooms.

*   Safety and special considerations

    *   Hypoallergenic options: cotton, bamboo, or high-quality synthetic for sensitive skin.
    *   Flame retardancy: avoid heavy flame-retardant chemical treatments for home use; follow sleep-safety guidelines if used with children.
    *   Pet-friendly: durable, washable fabrics and patterns that hide hair and wear.

Style & finish

*   Color and pattern: use throws to inject contrast, repeat a room accent color, or break up a monochrome palette.
*   Texture layering: mix textures (smooth leather chair + chunky knit throw) to add visual depth.
*   Fringe and tassels: decorative but can snag or be chewed by pets.

Budget and value

*   Low budget: microfiber, acrylic, basic cotton — good for frequent replacement.
*   Mid-range: cotton-linen blends, better fleece, wool blends — balance comfort and longevity.
*   High-end: cashmere, premium merino wool, designer pieces — invest if frequently used and cared for.

Quick selection checklist (3 questions)

1.   What’s the primary use? (warmth, decoration, travel)
2.   Do you need machine washability and pet/kid durability?
3.   What size and style will complement your furniture?

Typical recommendations

*   For cozy warmth and long life: premium wool or wool blend, dense weave.
*   For everyday washable comfort: heavyweight cotton or high-quality fleece.
*   For luxury and softness: cashmere or high-end faux fur.
*   For summer or a light layer: linen or lightweight cotton waffle.

Final tip

Buy a throw you’ll touch frequently: prioritize tactile comfort and practical care over trend-driven aesthetics. If unsure, choose a medium-weight cotton blend in a neutral color—versatile, durable, and easy to launder.

A rug goes on the floor.

A throw is like a small decorative blanket that can go on a couch or bed. (generally 42″x78″)

Blankets and quilts are meant to cover a bed. Ideally they drape over the sides of the mattress but, do not go to the floor.

A quilt is a coverlet for a bed, made of two layers of fabric with some soft substance, such as wool, synthetic batting or down, between them and stitched in patterns or tufted through all thicknesses in order to prevent the filling from shifting.

Blanket usually are made of felted fabrics and finished around the edge with stitching or some type of trim. Some blankets are made of woven yarns. Blankets can be composed of wool, cotton, silk, camel, cashmere and fiber blends.

The thing that distinguishes a bedspread is its size. A bedspread covers the bed and drapes to the floor at the sides and bottom. A bedspread can be styled “closed” so that the pillows are covered by the bedspread; or “open”, where the pillows are displayed on top of the spread. An abbreviated version of a breadspread is called a coverlet and with the edges falling slightly below the profile of the mattress or even covering the boxspring.

If a bedspreads is quilted, it technically can be refered to as a quilt. Bedspreads can be made of chenille and other lightweight fabrics.

Quilts can also refer to down comforters which usually have a white or ivory colored shell or fabric. This type of quilt can be dispayed on a bed, as is, but frequently is covered by a decorative duvet cover.

Choosing the right blanket becomes trickier the more you search and start to uncover the huge selection available.

In general, the choices come down to:

1.   Which material? Blankets can be made from pure new wool, recycled wool, merino wool, other wool, alpaca wool, cotton, blends of natural fibre and synthetic fibres like acrylic…you get the picture! Whether you go for one fibre or another is will be influenced by use.
2.   Why do you want a blanket? If you want a blanket for your home that is high quality, good at keeping you warm less likely to irritate allergies, then wool is a good choice. If you are looking for a blanket that will need to be machine washed regularly, then acrylic is a good option.
3.   What’s your budget? There is a huge difference in price between a cheap 100% acrylic blanket and an alpaca wool blanket: hundreds or even thousands of pounds in some instances. In between, there’s something to suit every budget and taste.
4.   Environmental impact? Nearly every production process on the planet has an associated environmental cost and increasingly the responsibility is placed on the consumer to make the right choice. Consider for example that synthetic fibres like acrylic persist in the natural environment for many years and have been found to be a major contributor to the build up of harmful microfibres on land and in our oceans. While the rearing of sheep and transport of wool to market will have its own carbon footprint, in the right conditions, the fibres biodegrade easily.
5.   How safe is my blanket? One of the features of wool that has enderared it to firemen, soldiers and police alike is that it is naturally fire retardant, which could be useful depending on how you want to use a blanket: in the living room next to an open fireplace, camping…it is well known that some commonly used synthetic fibres are not fire retardant and require treatment to reduce their inflammatory properties.

These are but a few ideas which I hope people find useful. I will try to add to this post as I go, but I would love to hear other people’s opinions on the matter. Choosing a blanket is certainly not simple.
