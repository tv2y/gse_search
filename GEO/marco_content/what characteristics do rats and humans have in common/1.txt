Q: Are rats related to humans? If not, why do we share similar characteristics with them (like intelligence)?

A: All life is related, it is just a matter of how closely. Rats are relatively close, they are mammals after all. I’m not going to look up the last common ancestor, but in casual language that common ancestor is right before the primates. Primates evolved from rodent like creatures. Rats, mice, and squirrels can all use their front paws as hands a trait present and emphasized in primates.

As far as intelligence, they have a foreshadowing of the primate brain structure, including a rudimentary neocortex. They are social, apparently have emotions, good memory, are curious and can learn. And, to answer you question, those similarities exist because they directly share some of our brain structure inherited from a common ancestor that had the beginnings of structures present in the primate brain.

There are a lot of statistics on the internet about rodent - human genetic similarity but most of those numbers are either wrong or misleading. But there is an important lesson about evolution here.

Fake News

For some reason, mouse data has gotten more publicity than rat data. This beautiful chart reports that mice are less similar to humans than chimpanzees are:

[1]

 It would be nice to see where those numbers came from but the link in the source article is broken and I cannot find any site citing the same numbers.
The New Scientist magazine gives a much higher value:

[2]

That value seems unreasonable given that chimpanzees supposedly have 98% the same genes as humans. Remarkably, CNN published a number that makes mice seem more similar to humans than chimpanzees are:

[3]

What’s going on?

Those statistics come from a misunderstanding of the genetic concept of ortholog and paralog. When a geneticist says a human and a rat have the same gene, that doesn’t mean that the genes are identical.

A given gene could be very different in rat and human but will be described as the same because it serves the same function. Globin genes in various species are very different from each other but they all serve to carry oxygen in the blood. They use technical terms to describe how similar these genes are: homolog, ortholog, and paralog. This chart shows the evolutionary history of globulin genes.

[4]

 Mouse, chick, and frog have the same alpha chain gene (orthologs) even though the DNA for these genes is different.
Percent similarity doesn’t have a set meaning

There’s other ways to define genetic similarity. You could look at percentage of individual nucleotides the DNA that are the same, genes having at least one change, genetic changes that actually affect function, order of genes on chromosome, and lots more. I discuss some of those elsewhere and point out that you can get anywhere between 29% to 99% similarity between chimpanzee and human, depending on how you compute the numbers.

[5]

Many of the genetic differences between rodents and humans are unimportant.

The rate of genetic change has been higher in rodents than in primates, including a three-fold higher rate of change in “neutral DNA.” That’s changes in DNA that don’t have any effect on the organism. Interestingly, the rate of this type of genetic change has been a bit higher in rats than mice.

Rat, mouse, and rat genomes contain multiple copies of “immobilized transposable elements.” That’s a kind of parasitic genetic material found in most animals. You could call them truly selfish genes. They constitutes about 40% of the mouse and rat genomes, and almost 50% of the human.

A sizeable number of the genes that differ between rodents and humans are involved in smell (olfaction). Rodents have 1,200–1,400 genes for smell but people have a lot fewer.

Rats, mice, and humans also have a form of DNA called “large segmental repeats.” This is probably nonfunctional but makes up about 3% of the rat genome, 1–2% of mouse, and 5–6% of the human genome.

Important genes are similar in rodents and humans.

About 90% of rat genes are “strict orthologues” in both mouse and human genomes. Again, orthologs are genes that may have changed but still do mostly the same thing in rats and humans.

There are more than a thousand human disorders showing Mendelian inheritance. A very high fraction of them, possibly nearly all, have orthologs in rats.

There are very few genes that are present in one species but not another. One group of researchers looked at a sample of 731 mouse genes. They found that only 14 of them, less than 2%, didn’t seem to exist in people.

Mice and rats look similar but are genetically different.

The common ancestor of mice and rats lived about 16 million years ago. The common ancestor of humans, rats, and mice lived about 87 million years ago. (Picture taken from

The rate of genetic evolution has been higher among rodents than primates but much of this is neutral evolution involving changes that don’t affect function. Rodents have also had a higher rate of chromosomal changes than primates.

Lessons about evolution

Anybody who has studied anatomy or physiology should have noticed that mammals are made up of mostly the same parts.

To get a sense of that look at a rat skeleton.

And then look at a human skeleton.

[11]

You will see that the two species have mostly the same bones although they are shaped differently. That’s also true for internal organs; both species have the same organs. Again, rats and people have mostly the same biochemicals inside them.

So it isn’t surprising that rats and humans have mostly the same genes, although the DNA sequences differ.

Creationists have often pointed out that it is difficult for mutations to produce new functions.

[12]

 That’s a fair point and that’s why evolution works mostly by modifying existing genes and only rarely creates entirely new ones.

“How intelligent are rats”?

For their size, very. They can be quickly trained to do tricks and perform tasks, and are social and interact well with people.

“do they make good pets”?

Depends on the person. In general yes, they make good pets, but that does not mean they will make good pets for you.

I, for example, do not like them due to their short lifespan. I take a while to really bond with an animal, and right about the time I really get used to them they are ready to check out. People who like pet rats need to get comfortable with the idea of getting a new pet every two to four years.

Other may not like that they are messy animals who communicate with, uh, scent. Sure, rats keep themselves pretty clean on the whole (varies by individual), but that does not help the fact that they communicate and mark territory by spreading feces and urine. You can sort of train this behavior out of them, but it never goes away entirely. You will be cleaning constantly, both for their health and your sanity.

Rats also have strong and unique personalities. Just because your last rat was super smart, clean and cuddly will not mean the next one will be. Even raising them by hand does not ensure you get the behavior you want.

“can they bond with humans?”

Oh yeah, to the point where they can become inseparable. They are highly social animals, and this easily translates to humans if you put in the time and effort.

Well, there are several issues with rats. First, they are intelligent and very social. So, it’s not uncommon to encounter packs of rats. They can also be friendly and trainable in the right circumstances. But, that depends on the species of rat — and there are a lot of them. However, since your posting in “pest control”, I’m assuming that you’re asking about the detrimental aspects of rats. So, here are some of those issues as well:

Rats can eat through wallboard and wood easily. They have sharp claws and teeth. So, a rat bite is quite painful. Depending on what the rat’s been exposed to, the rat can carry multiple diseases such as rabies. They also leave saliva where they’ve been chewing and that contaminates the area as well. Their fleas can also carry disease. So, if the rats have been around, expect other insect pests that will live both on the rats and in the rat’s feces and all the bad things that can come from fleas and ticks.

Rats also like to chew wires. Depending on the insulation, they may chew through wires trying to get to a destination or because the insulation (like the old cloth insulation) is somehow tasty. In chewing through insulation, they can cause electrical fires.

Rats build nests that require soft material. So, they’ll tear up almost anything from cardboard, newspapers or stored clothes to get what they want for their nests. If they’ve chewed through wiring, these nests become tinder.

Rats, especially those is major cities, can get rather large. I’ve seen city brown rats that look more the size of a small dog or cat. And, they can fight. So, they’re not going down easily. Even field rats can get pretty big. However, the typical rat can be 5–8″ long, not including the tail, and can weigh in excess of a pound (500–650 g).

Rats will consume any stored grain. So, corn, wheat, grass seed, bird seed, etc. is fair game to them. So, a large colony of rats will eat a village's food stores in just a few days. Because rats are nocturnal, you will rarely see them unless you have night vision equipment such as infrared cameras. But, depending on the food source, rats reproduce and an alarming rate. Rat’s seem to be born pregnant.

To kill off rats, there are typically just a few techniques. One, you can poison them. This is effective, but potentially leaves dead rats in your walls that you’ll have to deal with. And, the poison doesn’t typically just kill the rats. There is likely collateral damage to other animals including pets like cats or dogs. So, you’ve got to keep the poison isolated.

You can use mechanical traps. These break the rat’s neck or crush its skull. You’ll then have to empty and reset the traps. This works if the colony is small. But, is ineffective if the colony is large because it takes too much time to wipe them out one-by-one.

There are now electrical traps. These electrocute the rat when it steps on two different plates inside the trap. However, like the mechanical trap, you’ll have to empty the traps. But, you might actually get more than one rat caught in a single trap depending on your bait. A good bait, BTW, is common peanut butter. Rats and mice love the stuff. If you smear a small amount of peanut butter on an actual peanut, you’ve got a combination that almost can’t be ignored. And, make sure you put the traps along the wall. Rats run along the baseboards typically.

Both the mechanical and the electrical traps are generally quick deaths. However, there are glue traps that stick the rat’s feet and the rat starves or dehydrates to death. This is generally considered to be quite inhumane. But, has the advantage of potentially dealing with multiple rats at a time.

Animals such as cats and some dogs like terriers are pretty good at dealing with the smaller rat species. They like chasing and catching the rats. However, sometimes they don’t kill the rat, but rather bring you a half-dead rat because you’re such a poor hunter that they want to teach you how to deal with small prey. But, the larger rats are too big for all but the most aggressive and largest hunting “pet”. Of course, snakes are also good with killing rats. But, poisoning the rat will also poison the snake. Still, many people don’t like having snakes around the house.

Shooting is another approach. A .22 LR round will take out just about any rat. Some .22 or .177 cal pellet guns or Co2 BB guns will also do the trick. This works in rural areas, but forget using this approach in a city.

Finally, there are the “humane” traps like the “havahart” traps that catch the rats live so you can move them. But, you’ve got to move them far away and hope they don’t cause problems where ever you moved them to. And, if there’s a whole colony, this could take a while to catch and release all of them.

That’s probably way more than you wanted to hear on this topic. So, I’ll close now. Good luck with your rat issues.

Because a hell of a lot of those genes are for basic ‘housekeeping’ functions - DNA storage and replication, various RNAs involved in protein synthesis, basic metabolism of sugars and lipids etc.

These date back to the very beginnings of eukaryotes and beyond. The last common ancestor of bananas and humans was something single celled floating in the seas a billion and a half years ago (give or take), but both bananas and humans still retain the same fundamentals of cellular metabolism. They diverged in many, many ways since, but the basic processes that keep a cell alive and dividing are fundamentally the same, but the way those cells are structured differ a fair bit, and the way they are organised into tissues and the functions of those tissues are completely different.

We share a lot more DNA with rats. The last common ancestor of rodents and humans lived a mere 100 million years ago (approximately), the basal placental mammal being one of many varieties of mammal living under the noses of the dinosaurs.

Meet our last common mammalian ancestor

We have a lot more in common with rats than we do with bananas. Our cell structures are identical, tissue types very similar, our organs do the same things in almost the same way, we have the same basic skeleton give or take a few tail bones, we reproduce and develop in the same way etc. The differences are at a higher level of organisation - what shape bones and teeth, what adaptations to diet in the alimentary canal and attendant organs, what size and shape each organ is, what size and shape the whole animal is.

The differences are mainly in the many controlling genes that coordinate the functions of the more basic ones. Yep our skin cells are extremely similar to rats when looked at individually, but when you come to the organ level we differ a bit. How thick are the various layers, how much collagen, which colouring proteins like melanin and how much, how closely do hair follicles occur in the tissue and how active are they, ditto for sebaceous glands - all controlled at a higher level of organisation.

So we are not completely different to rats and bananas except on a comparatively superficial level - get down to the organs, the tissues and cells and we are very much like rats, go further to the nucleic acids, proteins, fats and sugars and how these molecules interact and most aspects of cell structure and we are the same as bananas.
