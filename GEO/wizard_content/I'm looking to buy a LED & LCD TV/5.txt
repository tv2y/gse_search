Why you should trust me
-----------------------

I cover TVs and other AV equipment for Wirecutter. I have reviewed TVs since 2012, and I have ISF Level III training and calibration certification. There are more TV stands in my house than there are people.

*   For the latest version of this guide, I tested five 2025 TV series that meet our minimum requirements for inclusion, and I considered others that were tested by trusted sources at CNET, Rtings, and Tom’s Guide.
*   I consulted hardware engineers, scientists, and Hollywood colorists regarding subjects such as mini-LED functionality and the Helmholtz-Kohlrausch effect (video).
*   Like all Wirecutter journalists, I review and test products with complete editorial independence. I’m never made aware of any business implications of my editorial recommendations. Read more about our editorial standards.

Who this is for
---------------

This guide is aimed at anyone who wants better performance than the average budget 4K TV delivers but doesn’t want to spend thousands of dollars for the absolute best.

In our tests, we’ve found that OLED TVs consistently deliver better-looking images than LCD TVs do. But OLED technology is prohibitively expensive for most people, and the best LCD/LED TVs can deliver a picture that looks almost as good for less money. They also get brighter than many OLED models, so generally they’re a better fit for a bright viewing environment.

How we picked and tested
------------------------

 We back up all of our LCD/LED TV recommendations with thorough, objective testing using industry-standard hardware and software.Lee Neikirk/NYT Wirecutter

For us to consider an LCD/LED TV for this guide, it has to include the following elements (see our TV buying guide for explanations of these terms):

*   full-array LED backlight with local dimming
*   the inclusion of quantum dots for improved color reproduction
*   support for as many HDR formats as possible
*   at least a native 120 Hz refresh rate
*   inputs that support HDMI 2.1

We prioritize LCD TVs equipped with mini-LED backlights, though that is not a strict requirement. We aren’t currently testing TVs with 8K resolution, as we don’t think there’s enough 8K content yet to justify spending more on them.

Although we don’t target a specific price range for this guide, we do keep an eye toward picture quality versus value. Sometimes, barely perceptible picture improvements can cost significant amounts of money, an investment that may only be worth it for hardcore videophiles.

We focus on the popular 55-inch and 65-inch screen sizes in our testing because TVs of those sizes can fit into most living rooms, and they offer a large, cinematic experience without overwhelming the room. But many of our picks come in other screen sizes, as well.

To evaluate LCD/LED TVs, we use a combination of objective measurements, subjective viewing, and side-by-side comparisons. Our measurement equipment consists of Portrait Displays’s Calman Ultimate software, a C6 HDR5000 colorimeter, and a VideoForge Pro signal generator. For subjective testing, we source material from streaming apps, Blu-ray discs, gaming consoles, and HDR benchmark discs. Read more about Wirecutter’s TV testing methodology.

Top pick: Hisense U8QG Series
-----------------------------

Lee Neikirk/NYT Wirecutter

#### Top pick

### Key specs

**Screen sizes (inches)**55-inch (55U8QG), 65-inch (65U8QG), 75-inch (75U8QG), 85-inch (85U8QG),

 100-inch (100U8QG)
**Backlight type**mini-LED with local dimming
**Refresh rate**165 Hz
**Color tech**quantum dots
**HDR formats**HDR10, HDR10+, HLG, Dolby Vision
**HDMI connections**three HDMI 2.1 (one eARC)
**Smart-TV platform**Google TV
**TV tuner**ATSC 3.0

The Hisense U8QG is the LCD TV to buy if you want advanced performance and features but don’t want to pay a premium for the very best picture quality.

**This TV has some of the best local dimming I’ve ever seen.** The reason OLED TVs look so good is that they can turn off individual pixels when displaying darkness in a scene, maximizing contrast in a way that’s realistic and immersive. LCD TVs mimic this behavior through a backlight process called local dimming.

To that end, the U8QG offers the best of two worlds: It’s extremely bright — I measured a peak around 3,200 nits, which is brighter than any OLED TV — and its local dimming is very strong, meaning it can pair deep shadows with super-bright highlights. Its contrast is still not as good as that of an OLED TV, but it’s very convincing.

While LCD TVs have used local dimming for years to achieve better image quality, the U8QG’s control of its mini-LEDs is on a different level. I did my best to stress test the TV’s dimming, looking for common backlight issues like blooming (when bright objects shed light into the shadows around them) or flashlighting (when light bleeds into black bars on the top and bottom of the screen). The U8QG’s dimming wasn’t flawless, but it was hard to see any sign of backlight activity unless I was watching from off-center.

**HDR content looks awesome on this TV.** The two things you need for impressive high dynamic range (HDR) performance are high contrast and a wide color range. With high brightness and dark black levels, the U8QG nails the first requirement. And because it’s equipped with quantum dots (nanocrystals that increase a TV’s color saturation), it’s capable of creating the fiery oranges and canopy greens that bring HDR content to life.

In my measurements, the U8QG managed to reproduce about 85% of the current DCI-P3 color space used for HDR video, while our upgrade pick and the best OLED TVs I’ve tested can achieve 90% or more. Still, this TV delivers a rich color presentation that, when coupled with its high contrast, makes for an excellent HDR experience.

This was especially apparent when watching the U8QG side by side with its main competitor, the TCL QM8K. I watched scenes from several 4K HDR Blu-ray discs on both TVs — including _Jurassic Park_ and _Barbie_ — and while they’re almost identical performers, the U8QG’s colors looked punchier in several scenes, if only by a small margin.

It’s also worth noting that, no matter what you’re watching, the U8QG is pretty bright all of the time. Even in the Theater Night picture mode, the U8QG averages around 1,500 nits of brightness, which is too bright for comfortable nighttime viewing. So you might want to turn the backlight control down before movie night.

 The U8QG’s Google TV platform is one of our favorites, as it’s both intuitive and full-featured.Lee Neikirk/NYT Wirecutter

**The U8QG is a great choice for gamers, with a unique feature seldom found on TVs.** Nowadays, the best TVs are kitted out to make video games as smooth and responsive as possible. The U8QG not only fits the bill, it also has a feature that I’ve never seen on a TV before: a USB-C video input.

The U8QG boasts a high native refresh rate (165 Hz), which is about as speedy as gaming TVs get right now. I measured just 10 milliseconds of input lag using our Leo Bodnar tester, which assesses input lag during 4K 60 Hz playback. That’s a very good result, and the lag will be even lower (5 ms or less) with a 120 Hz or higher refresh rate.

The U8QG uses Hisense’s Game Bar, a menu overlay that deploys automatically when you start playing a game. The Game Bar allows you to see stats like HDR/Dolby Vision Gaming status, VRR status, current refresh rate, and frames per second.

Unlike last year’s U8N model, the U8QG has only three HDMI 2.1 inputs instead of four. Hisense has opted to replace one of them with a USB-C video input. This is designed for PC gamers whose HDMI outputs may not support refresh rates up to 165 Hz, but you can also use it to connect a Nintendo Switch without needing the separate HDMI dock. Note that the USB-C input doesn’t support HDR; it’s built for speed, not for picture quality.

I played a good deal of _Clair Obscur: Expedition 33_ (on an Xbox Series X in the _Dolby Vision Game_ picture mode) and was really impressed with its pairing of bright colors and smooth gameplay. I also tried _Final Fantasy XVI_ (on a PlayStation 5 in the HDR Game picture mode) and found that, while it looked good in its 60 fps Frame Rate mode, it had some unsightly blurring in Graphics mode (capped at 30 fps) due to slower pixel transitions, one area where the U8QG still lags behind OLED TVs and our upgrade pick.

 The U8QG comes with a backlit remote control.Lee Neikirk/NYT Wirecutter

**This version of Google TV works just like it should.** Google TV is our favorite smart platform thanks to its superior search features, cross-app integration, and multi-profile personalization. But not every version of Google TV is created equal: The platform, being somewhat robust in its feature set, can run sluggishly on lower-end TVs that lack the processing power to execute it properly. Fortunately, the U8QG has the chops to allow Google TV to run swiftly and efficiently.

**A backlit remote control is always a plus.** As modern LCD/LED TVs go, the U8QG is quite thick and hefty, and it might even look a little silly when mounted on the wall. But its center-oriented, pedestal stand holds it sturdily, and the materials feel generally robust.

My favorite physical feature is the TV’s backlit remote: Whenever you touch it, all of its buttons briefly light up, making it much easier to control the TV in a dim or dark room compared with a remote without a backlight.

### Flaws but not dealbreakers

**Accuracy is not this TV’s strong suit.** During both SDR and HDR playback, my 65-inch U8QG test sample struggled to add brightness at the appropriate increments as it moved from dark to bright signals (technically speaking, its electro-optical transfer function, or EOTF, is inaccurate).

This can make darker scenes in movies and video games look overly bright, and it may be annoying if you’re coming from a well-calibrated TV. The U8QG’s color accuracy is also not as good as it could be (even in Filmmaker Mode), but it isn’t that far off the mark.

**The TV’s viewing angles and reflectivity could be better.** Like most LCD TVs, the U8QG’s viewing angles are fairly poor, not nearly as good as an OLED TV’s. This means you have to be careful about your seating arrangement. Anyone watching from off to the side will see shifting colors and reduced contrast.

Likewise, the glossy screen is not very good at handling reflections (and nowhere near as good as a TV like our upgrade pick, which has a matte screen finish). You’ll want to be careful about where you place the TV with regards to lights and windows.

**This TV’s upscaling and handling of older content could be better.** The U8QG isn’t the best choice if you still watch a lot of older content (like DVDs or last-generation video games). You may notice banding, or uneven steps, during color transitions, excessive grain in film content, and/or edges that don’t look as sharp and well-defined as they could.

### Recommended settings

If you want the most accurate picture settings, we recommend setting the Hisense U8QG to its **_Filmmaker Mode_** picture mode, whether in SDR or HDR. Dolby Vision content looks best in the _**Dolby Vision IQ**_ mode.

Like all smart TVs, the Hisense U8QG will try to spy on your viewing habits. To disable this setting, you’ll want to go into the **_Privacy_** menu and toggle off **_Viewing Information Services_.**

Budget pick: Hisense U75QG Series
---------------------------------

Lee Neikirk/NYT Wirecutter

#### Budget pick

### Key specs

**Screen sizes (inches)**55 (55U75QG), 65 (65U75QG), 75 (75U75QG), 85 (85U75QG), 100 (100U75QG),

 116 (116U75QG)
**Backlight type**mini-LED with local dimming
**Refresh rate**165 Hz
**Color tech**quantum dots
**HDR formats**HDR10, HDR10+, HLG, Dolby Vision
**HDMI connections**four HDMI 2.1 (one eARC)
**Smart-TV platform**Google TV
**TV tuner**ATSC 3.0

If you’re willing to sacrifice a little brightness and contrast to save money, the Hisense U75QG Series is a smart choice. In many ways, its performance is identical to that of our top pick, and it comes in six screen sizes.

**The U75QG compares similarly to our top pick in performance and features.** While this TV is less bright than the U8QG (peaking at around 2,750 nits versus 3,200 nits), it’s still impressively bright and has almost all of the same strengths as its pricier sibling. It uses essentially the same mini-LED backlighting and local-dimming function as our top pick (but with fewer dimming zones), so you get excellent contrast. It’s also equipped with quantum dots, and in my testing it offered roughly the same color reproduction as our top pick.

While the U75QG’s local dimming doesn’t always activate as quickly as it should and occasionally struggles with complex light patterns, it’s an improvement over last year’s U7N, and the TV’s black levels are better, too. Even with its very high peak brightness, shadows remained satisfyingly dark in my tests.

**This is one of the best HDR TVs for the money.** Because the U75QG is very bright and decently colorful, and because it supports all of the major HDR formats, it’s a great choice for viewing HDR video via streaming, Blu-ray discs, or video games.

One thing that really sets the U75QG apart from its direct competition — especially the TCL QM7K — is that it’s a lot brighter in its HDR game modes. During my side-by-side comparisons, the QM7K was too dark for a well-lit room, specifically while playing video games in Dolby Vision HDR. The U75QG maintained better brightness, giving it superior room flexibility.

When I watched HDR content side by side with our top pick, the difference in their peak brightness was noticeable, but not major. Scenes where most of the screen is lit up (like the corn-field scene during _Interstellar_) looked better on our top pick, but only in a bright room.

 The U75QG’s Google TV implementation was just as speedy and stable as it was on the higher-priced U8QG.Lee Neikirk/NYT Wirecutter

**Like our top pick, the U75QG is great for gaming.** You get four HDMI 2.1 inputs (rather than the three and USB-C input you’ll find on our top pick), a native 165 Hz refresh rate, and low input lag. In fact, I measured slightly better input lag using our Leo Bodnar input lag tester (which measures a 4K 60 Hz signal). Our top pick averaged around 10 ms, while the U75QG averaged 9.8 ms, though you aren’t likely to notice such a small difference during gameplay.

One area where the U75QG showed some weakness during gameplay was with its local-dimming function. Video games tend to throw a lot more variables at a TV’s video processor than TV shows and movies do, and games with tons of small, bright spots or moving text occasionally challenged the U75QG’s local dimming more than I saw on the U8QG, resulting in small instances of bloom or shifting brightness levels.

 The U75QG’s remote is identical to that of the U8QG.Lee Neikirk/NYT Wirecutter

**You get the same smooth Google TV experience as our top pick.** In my testing, I really couldn’t tell any difference in processing speed or general efficacy while using the U75QG’s Google TV platform versus using it on the U8QG. The home menu boots quickly, and jumping into apps is about as fast as it is on our favorite media streaming device.

**I still dislike this TV’s tabletop stand, but it’s better than last year’s model.** One of my complaints about last year’s Hisense U7N was its flimsy tabletop stand. The U75QG still uses the same “slab of plastic that snaps over two metal feet” design, but at least it snaps on more sturdily this year.

In general, the U75QG’s design is quite robust. The TV is not as thick as the U8QG, but it feels heavy and sturdy enough to absorb some bumps. The U75QG includes a backlit remote, which is a great addition when you’re trying to use it in a dim or dark room.

### Flaws but not dealbreakers

**The U75QG has the same flaws as our top pick.** With so many similarities between the U75QG and U8QG, it shouldn’t be a surprise that they share almost all of the same flaws.

The U75QG’s out-of-the-box calibration could be better, and it has some of the same issues with over-brightening HDR content. Its color accuracy is also not as good as it could be, though it’s solid.

Additionally, the U75QG’s horizontal viewing angles aren’t very good, and it looks better with 4K HDR video than it does with lower-resolution sources.

### Recommended settings

As with the U8QG, your best bet with the U75QG is to pick the **_Filmmaker Mode_** for both SDR and HDR content. Dolby Vision content looks best in the **_Dolby Vision IQ_** mode.

Like with other smart TVs, the Hisense U75QG will spy on your viewing habits if you let it. To disable this setting, you’ll want to go into the **_Privacy_** menu and toggle off **_Viewing Information Services_**.

Upgrade pick: Samsung QN90F Series

Lee Neikirk/NYT Wirecutter

#### Upgrade pick

### Key specs

**Screen sizes (inches)**43-inch (QN43QN90F), 50-inch (QN50QN90F), 55-inch (QN55QN90F),

 65-inch (QN65QN90F), 75-inch (QN75QN90F), 85-inch (QN85QN90F),

 98-inch (QN98QN90F)
**Backlight type**mini-LED with local dimming
**Refresh rate**165 Hz
**Color tech**quantum dots
**HDR formats**HDR10, HDR10+, HLG
**HDMI connections**four HDMI 2.1 (one eARC)
**Smart-TV platform**Tizen Smart Hub
**TV tuner**ATSC 1.0

If you want an LCD TV that avoids our other picks’ flaws, the Samsung QN90F Series is worth the extra money. This year, Samsung has added a glare-reducing matte screen to this TV line’s bag of tricks, which also includes premium design and build quality, excellent HDR and gaming performance, and better handling of lower-resolution content. It’s also offered in more screen sizes to suit more rooms.

As you’d expect from an upgrade pick, the QN90F isn’t bad for any specific use case, but it isn’t as many miles ahead of our more affordable picks as it might have been a few years ago.

**The matte screen makes a huge difference for daytime viewing.** The QN90F’s standout feature is that it’s equipped with a matte screen finish, similar to what Samsung uses on The Frame lifestyle TV.

Most TVs have a glossy screen finish, historically favored because matte screens sacrifice some brightness and color. In the case of the QN90F, that’s only partially true. This TV is not as bright as our top or budget pick, but it’s still capable of around 2,500 nits of peak brightness, which is better than a lot of LCD and OLED TVs on the market. Plus, it can reproduce more of the current HDR color space (DCI-P3) than our other picks (95% versus 85%).

The end result is a TV that offers the pros of a matte screen — cutting down on light reflections to deliver better performance in a bright room — with almost none of the cons.

Samsung’s screen technology also does a better job than many LCD TVs at preserving image contrast and color at wider viewing angles. All three of our picks are capable of pairing high brightness with black levels that read zero on my light meter, but the QN90F’s superior viewing angles will deliver better black levels for folks sitting off-center.

 Samsung’s Tizen-based Smart Hub is less intuitive than Google TV, but it’s very responsive on the QN90F.Lee Neikirk/NYT Wirecutter

**The QN90F’s out-of-the-box calibration is better than that of our other picks.** In our measurement tests, the QN90F was not perfectly accurate, but it came closer to reference color and luminance standards than our other two picks, especially in Filmmaker Mode. In comparison, the Hisense U8QG and Hisense U75QG were further off the mark in color accuracy, brightness across the grayscale spectrum (EOTF), and handling of more complex color palettes (like flesh tones).

If there’s a movie you love and you want it to look exactly as the director intended, this TV will be more faithful in that regard.

 Although the QN90F’s remote control isn’t backlit, it is rechargeable via solar or USB-C, which we really like.Lee Neikirk/NYT Wirecutter

**I preferred gaming on the QN90F over our other picks.** In terms of gaming specs, this TV doesn’t outshine the Hisense TVs. Comparing it to the U75QG specifically, both TVs give you four HDMI 2.1 inputs, a native 165 Hz refresh rate, a Game Bar overlay that lets you see HDR, VRR, and FPS status at a glance, and the exact same input lag measurement: 9.8 ms with a 4K 60 Hz signal.

But the human element matters, and I simply preferred gaming on the QN90F because of its matte screen. Unlike with most live-action HDR content, video games tend to push brightness and color to hyper-realistic proportions, which can be aggravating on the eyes after many hours in a dark room (which is why many gamers use a bias light). With the QN90F, you can leave lights on or window shades open and still see everything perfectly.

I tested the QN90F hot on the heels of two premium OLED TVs and genuinely felt like _Clair Obscur: Expedition 33_ looked better on this TV due to its higher brightness and rich color saturation.

**It has a sleek yet reliable build.** The QN90F manages to be lighter in weight while also feeling more sturdy than the lion’s share of LCD TVs. The QN90F comes with Samsung’s SolarCell remote, which can charge via ambient light (yay) but doesn’t have the backlighting you get on the Hisense remotes (boo).

This series is available in six screen sizes, including smaller 43- and 50-inch sizes that are good for desktop gaming.

### Flaws but not dealbreakers

**It doesn’t support Dolby Vision HDR.** One of Samsung’s biggest flaws every year is the company’s refusal to pay to license Dolby Vision support for its TVs. It’s a bit absurd to buy a TV that’s so good at HDR and not get support for what is arguably the most premium HDR format. This TV does support the similar HDR10+ format, but it’s not as prevalent as Dolby Vision in streaming or disc content.

**The upscaling is better than that of our other picks but still not perfect.** In years past, Samsung has struggled to implement good sub-1080p upscaling in its TVs. The QN90F fares better than our other picks, but it doesn’t reproduce lower-resolution content as cleanly as the Sony TVs listed in Other LCD/LED TVs worth considering.

**Samsung’s Tizen Smart Hub is fine, but we prefer Google TV.** The Google TV platform continues to be our favorite smart-TV platform and the basis of our favorite media streaming device, whereas Samsung’s Tizen Smart Hub feels overdesigned. It introduces too many submenus and insists on identifying every HDMI device you plug in. That’s great when it works, but when it doesn’t, it makes finding and switching to the source you want annoying.

**Broadcasts are limited to 1080p.** Unlike our other picks, the QN90F doesn’t have an ATSC 3.0 tuner, so it can’t receive 4K over-the-air broadcasts. That isn’t ideal, but you can always buy a separate ATSC 3.0 tuner if you need one.

### Recommended settings

The Samsung QN90F looks most accurate in Filmmaker Mode, whether you’re watching SDR or HDR video. If you want more brightness, turn on the**_AI_**picture mode (the new name for Intelligent Mode).

Like all smart TVs, the Samsung QN90F spies on what you watch. If you turned this function on during setup and want to disable it after the fact, go to **_Settings_**, then **_Privacy Choices_**, and turn off **_Viewing Information Services_**.

Other LCD/LED TVs worth considering
-----------------------------------

**If you want a major performance upgrade in a larger screen size:** The TCL QM9K is the most souped-up LCD TV of the year, peaking at over 4,000 nits of HDR brightness and offering better backlight control (via full-array local dimming) than any of our picks. Its color production is on par with our upgrade pick, and it tested with a very low 9.3 ms of input lag. It also uses our favorite smart platform (Google TV), is ATSC 3.0 compatible, and (unlike our upgrade pick) is compatible with the Dolby Vision HDR format.

Purely from a performance standpoint, the QM9K has more horsepower than our picks, making it an easy choice if you only care about getting the best-performing LCD TV at the best price. However, it’s not available in a screen size smaller than 65 inches, the screen is quite reflective, and its out-of-the-box accuracy is notably worse than that of our upgrade pick and the Sony Bravia 9. We also have general concerns about TCL’s quality control compared to other premium brands.

**If you’ll spare no expense for bright, cinematic accuracy:**Sony’s Bravia 9 flagship TV gets almost as bright as our top pick, but it handles screen reflections, viewing angles, and audio performance as skillfully as our upgrade pick. It supports Dolby Vision HDR, it has outstanding color saturation (hitting 99% of the DCI-P3 color space), and it does a better job upscaling SD and HD content to 4K than our picks. This is the TV to buy if you want older movies and other lower-quality video content to look good without having to tinker with any settings.

But this TV is limited to screen sizes of 65, 75, and 85 inches, it’s worse for video games than our picks, and it commands a premium price, with the 65-inch model going for around $2,700 at this writing. For that much money, we recommend buying an OLED TV unless you need a TV with tons of light output for a really bright room.

**If you want a highly accurate TV that won’t break the bank:** The more affordable Sony Bravia XR X90L is a surprisingly good option. It lacks mini-LED backlighting, but it’s extremely accurate out of the box, so it’s ideal for the videophile on a budget. The only drawbacks are that it doesn’t get as bright as our picks, and it adds a bit more input lag in games.

The competition
---------------

Our requirements for the best LCD/LED TV (full-array local dimming, at least a 120 Hz native refresh rate) keep most LCD TVs from qualifying for inclusion in this guide. Look for lower-priced TVs in our guide to the best 4K TV on a budget. We aren’t currently considering 8K TVs, as we believe there still isn’t enough compatible content to justify their steep prices.

The TCL QM8K is extremely bright, hitting 3,700 nits of brightness during my sustained-peak-brightness test. It’s also equipped with the core technologies that make our picks shine: mini-LED backlighting, local dimming, and quantum dot color. But we still think the Hisense U8QG is the better all-around choice. The QM8K has higher input lag, it’s available in fewer screen sizes, it has only two HDMI 2.1 inputs, and it can’t reproduce as much of the HDR color gamut, which was noticeable in side-by-side comparisons with the Hisense TV.

We tested the TCL QM7K and found it to be a staunch performer that’s very similar to the Hisense U75QG. It has excellent black-level performance, good color, and solid peak brightness around 2,000 nits. But the QM7K has higher input lag and only two HDMI 2.1 inputs, and it’s much dimmer during video-game playback. It’s also limited to an ATSC 1.0 tuner. Overall the Hisense U75QG is the better value.

We didn’t test the Samsung QN85F, QN80F, or QN70F. The QN85F is only available in a 100-inch size, the QN80F has poor brightness and sub-optimal local-dimming performance according to reviews we trust, and the QN70F uses an edge-lit backlight array, so it doesn’t qualify for inclusion.

We also didn’t test LG’s QNED lineup of LCD TVs, most of which use IPS panels in some screen sizes. IPS panels improve the viewing angle, but at the expense of image contrast.

Reviews we trust report that the Sony Bravia 5’s HDR brightness peaks below 1,000 nits, putting it well out of the performance range of our picks.

Vizio did not release any new TVs in 2025.
