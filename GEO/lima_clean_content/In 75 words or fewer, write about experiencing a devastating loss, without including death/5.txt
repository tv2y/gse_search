Bereavement is a common experience in adults age 60 and older, often leading to acute grief characterized by yearning, decreased interest in activities, and frequent thoughts of the deceased. For most individuals, acute grief evolves into integrated grief, allowing them to reengage with life. However, approximately 7% of older adults develop Complicated Grief (CG), a mental health disorder where grief symptoms remain severe and impairing.  

CG is associated with prolonged acute grief symptoms like intense yearning, emotional pain, preoccupation with the deceased, avoidance of reminders, difficulty accepting the loss, and feelings of meaninglessness. Risk factors include female gender, older age, low socioeconomic status, lack of social support, traumatic death circumstances, and loss of a close relationship (such as a spouse or child).  

Compared to major depressive disorder (MDD), CG involves grief-specific symptoms like preoccupation with the deceased and avoidance of loss reminders rather than pervasive sadness or guilt. Treatments such as Complicated Grief Treatment (CGT), a 16-week therapy combining attachment-informed strategies with cognitive-behavioral techniques, have shown efficacy in reducing CG symptoms. Medication may help when combined with psychotherapy, but antidepressants alone are less effective for CG.  

Older adults with CG face heightened risks of hypertension, sleep impairment, suicidality, and overall health decline. Increased awareness, early detection, and specialized interventions like CGT are crucial to mitigate these adverse effects. Research continues to refine diagnostic criteria and explore prevention strategies to reduce the burden of CG in older populations.
