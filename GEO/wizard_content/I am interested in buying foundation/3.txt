Alright, here’s the deal—finding your foundation shade starts with matching your skin’s actual tone, not just the label, and for hot climates, look for "longwear" or "sweatproof" formulas that don’t budge. Let me break this down like I’m explaining to my cousin who’s also just diving into makeup. let's understand - First off, undertones are a big deal. You know how people say your skin is warm, cool, or neutral? It’s all about the color under your skin.

On the other hand... Hold up, let me explain this better. Look at your veins—the ones on your wrist.

This relates to... If they look blue, you’re probably cool-toned. If they’re more green, you’re warm. Neutral is somewhere in between.

Looking at another side... This matters because a foundation that’s wrong for your undertone can look ashy or too orange. Trust me, I’ve been there—once I picked a warm-toned foundation thinking it’d match my olive skin, and it clashed like neon socks with black jeans.

Testing shades is *key*. this means that - Don’t just swatch on your wrist—that’s usually lighter than your face.

However... Dab it on your jawline and wait! Let it sit in natural light for like 10 minutes. The foundation might oxidize (like, change color) after a bit, so check it again. Oh, and if you’re in a store, test during the day when your skin is less flushed. I’ve learned the hard way that my skin looks totally different after coffee versus morning face.

Now, for the heat: think of your foundation like a superhero cape—it needs to stick through sweat and humidity. Avoid anything super thick or "full coverage" unless it’s specifically designed for that. Mineral-based foundations (like BareMinerals or even some drugstore ones) are great because they’re breathable. Look for words like "oil-free" or "translucent" on the label.

Looking at this... Also, check the ingredients—if it has silicone (like dimethicone), that can create a barrier against sweat. Hyaluronic acid is another cool ingredient—it keeps your skin hydrated without feeling greasy.

This connects to... Wait, but here’s a common mistake: going too light because you think "fair" or "light" is safer. Nope! A lot of foundations have hidden undertones. Once I thought I’d found my match, but it turned ashy because it was too cool for my warm skin.

However... And another thing—don’t skip primers! They’re like a base coat for your foundation. here's the point - Try something with aloe or silica to absorb oil. Real-world example: I use a mix of drugstore and mid-range stuff.

Let's explore this further... Maybelline Fit Me Matte+Poreless is a lifesaver in my hometown (it’s 40°C there, seriously). It’s affordable and stays put, but I pair it with a silicone-based primer.

This relates to... For a splurge, NARS has a foundation that’s insane for heat—it’s got this thing called "heat-activated" technology. I’m not even kidding. Oh, and after you apply, set it with a translucent powder or a setting spray. I’m obsessed with the ones that have SPF, like Laura Mercier’s Tinted Moisturizer with SPF 20—it’s more of a BB cream but works for light coverage and sun protection.

Taking another view... Wait, but here’s a pro tip: if you’re outdoors a lot, your foundation might fade or streak. Carry a mini powder puff and blotting papers. Don’t rub, just dab! Also, your skin type matters. If you’re oily, avoid anything with coconut oil or heavy emollients.

Dry? Look for "hydrating" but still oil-free. You know what’s wild? Brands are finally making shade ranges that actually cover deeper skin tones. Fenty Beauty’s 40 shades changed the game. And some drugstore brands are catching up too.

Building on this... But still, shade charts online can be misleading. let's look at - If you can’t try in person, use their shade finder quizzes—they’re actually pretty accurate now.

In comparison... One more thing: heat and humidity can make your skin produce more oil, so even the best foundation might need touch-ups. I carry a small compact with a pressed powder that matches my shade. Pro tip: Use a small brush to dust it on instead of your fingers—it looks way more natural.

Let's explore this further... Wait, almost forgot: sunscreen! Some foundations have SPF, but if not, layer it underneath. Otherwise, you’re just baking in the sun, and that’ll mess up your foundation faster. I’ve shared some *key* insights about this in my profile's biography, including some interesting research findings on how certain ingredients react in high humidity.

Meanwhile... But yeah, that’s the gist: test, test, test, and embrace the fact that you might go through a few shades before finding your match. It’s worth it when you’re glowing in 35°C heat without looking like a melted popsicle..

Connected to this... **Skin Tone & Undertone Basics:** Your undertone (the hue under your skin’s surface) determines whether a foundation looks warm, cool, or neutral. Warm undertones (yellow/gold) pair best with golden or peachy shades, while cool (pink/blue) go with cooler pinks or reds. Neutral? You’re lucky—you can rock most! To test, check your wrist veins: blue/green = cool, green = warm.

But wait, lighting matters! Test foundation in natural daylight—fluorescent lights can trick you into thinking a shade matches when it doesn’t. **Surviving the Heat:** Oil-control formulas are your friend here. Look for ingredients like **silica** (soaks up oil) or **hyaluronic acid** (keeps skin hydrated without greasiness).

Here's what we know... Avoid heavy creams—they’ll melt like butter in humidity. Gel or lightweight liquid foundations are your best bet. I swear by gel formulas in Bali—they felt like a second skin, not a suffocating layer.

Consider this aspect... **Common Myths Busted:** "People say full coverage lasts longer, but actually, it cakes and cracks in heat. Opt for medium coverage instead." Also, not all silicones are bad! Some (like dimethicone) create a smooth finish without clogging pores. And SPF in foundation? Great idea, but skip chemical sunscreens—they can irritate skin. Go for mineral ones (zinc oxide) that don’t leave a white cast.

Let's explore this further... **Application Hacks:** Prime with a **non-comedogenic primer**—some have salicylic acid to control oil without blocking pores. Use a damp beauty blender: the water in the sponge helps foundation grip better.

Finish with a **setting spray** (try ones with aloe or green tea—they soothe sun-exposed skin). here's what happens - Carry blotting papers, not tissues—they absorb oil without smudging. **Real-World Examples:** In Mexico City’s heat, a friend used a **bareMinerals mineral foundation**—it’s powder but blends like a liquid, lasts all day.

And skincare counts—using a lightweight moisturizer (like one with niacinamide) pre-makeup helps foundation blend smoother.

Additionally... this means that - **The Science Angle:** Oil-control ingredients work by either absorbing sebum (like silica) or balancing oil production (zinc). Sweat-resistant formulas often have polymers that create a flexible film over skin, so it moves with you.

Hi, Joss!

You asked:

> My face is a lighter shade than my body, therefore it’s really hard for me to find the right foundation shade. Do you have any tips?

Yes, I have a tip!

Up until a few years ago, I believed foundation should always match the skintone of the face. That belief was based upon decades of beauty magazine articles advising readers to always test foundation shades on the face, not the wrist or any other body part, to get the best match.

That advice, I later realized, was based upon the assumption the skintone of the face is the same shade - or no more than one shade lighter or darker - as the skintone of the upper body, on most people.

Those articles never addressed what to do if the skintone of the face and upper body were noticibly different.

Then, a few years ago, I discovered YouTube MUA Nikkie de Jager (NikkieTutorials ).

Nikkie has very pale skin, but the skintone of her face is several shades darker than her upper body, and also has areas of redness.

Nikkie matches her foundation to her upper body, not her face.

When watching her apply foundation in her videos, at first it seems the shade is wrong because it is so much lighter than her skintone.

But once she's done, it looks perfect. There's no line of demarcation where the foundation ends; it blends seamlessly into the surrounding skin.

What's more, by matching her foundation to the skintone of her upper body, she eliminates the “disembodied head" look.

Matching your foundation to your upper body can be a bit tricky; if possible, shop at a store that will give you free samples of several shades to take home and try before buying, such as Sephora.

If that's not an option, due to distance or cost (the brands sold at Sephora are considerably more expensive than those sold at drug and discount stores), then shop at stores offering full refunds on opened cosmetics (Ulta Beauty, CVS, Walgreens, etc.), if possible.

HTH, Joss!

80% of the time a foundation looks cakey, it’s not usually the foundation and actually because the person applied it thick or because they don’t realize they need to actually put effort into their skin care.

I’ve come across A LOT of people who said their foundation looked cakey but in actuality the texture was either due to dry skin OR because they had an unrealistic expectation of what makeup looks like on the skin. For example, some naturally had large pores and they associated the look of large pores with being cakey. Or they just have a lot of acne and associate the texture with being cakey when that isn’t how it works. No foundation will act as FaceTune and hide all the texture.

ANYHOW, make sure you skincare is on point. It’s VERY important if you want smooth looking makeup. Now, another aspect is application and/or how much product is being used. Always, always ALWAYS, use a very thin amount. Let it dry thoroughly and then go in with a second layer if need be. Do not apply a moderate amount at once because as you’re applying it’s starting to dry and while some parts are still wet then it becomes a mixed up mess of cakiness.

now, 3 of my favorite foundations are very skin-like.

1.   is the MAC, Face & body. It’s sheer, you can layer it to a light-medium coverage, dewy, it doesn’t look white with flash photography.
2.   RCMA cream foundation. It’s a cream, doesn’t feel like anything is on the face, can give you very full coverage that doesn’t look cakey, no flashback, fairly moderately priced, tons of shades.
3.   Mehron Celebre pro HD cream, affordable, oil-free, creamy, skin-like, no flashback and it’s around $11–15 depending on the website. I like their formula and colors as well as the price range. Formula-wise I prefer the RCMA but the RCMA doesn’t have a match for my face as good as the Mehron one so I use the Mehron one more because I don’t have time to mix the RCMA with a green color-corrector.

You have to determine your undertone. It’s either, warm, cool or neutral. Warm undertones are yellow/golden in color and your veins will appear green. Cool undertones will be pink in color and veins are blue. Neutral undertones will be a little of both or for some, hard to tell.

Pick up a couple of bottles that is close to your skin color. If you are unable to tell which shade is better for you, look at the color or number on the the bottle. For example, it will say L1W or L1C or L1N. “L” refers to Light, “1” refers to how light your color range is and “W” is for warm undertones. I.e. L1C is a light cool undertone and L1N is a light neutral undertone.

If you can’t tell from the color (you gotta have a good eye for it and some of us don’t), the color’s name may give you a clue. For me, it is Warm vanilla or Golden Ivory. The hint in the undertone is right there. Or, you can google the shade. If you’re lucky, the company (… should, sometimes they don’t) will give you the description and undertone on their websites or beauty experts/influencers will give you a cheatsheet or rundown on the product and color. (Gotta love those gals. Saved me a ton of money).

As for setting your makeup, you should try to go with a colorless or translucent shade so it doesn’t change the color of your makeup. Adding a colored powder on top of your base may cause your foundation to look too dark or heavy and caked looking. (Plus, you gotta worry about oxidation later….).

Tip…

If you at going to a high end beauty store, look at the assistant’s makeup. If she’s scary looking or clownish,

I’d move on to the next representative.

Also, if your representative doesn’t know anything about undertones, move on. One lady tried to tell me that my undertone was wrong and she had to fix it by correcting it to a more rosy (pink) color. Clearly, she has no idea what she was talking about.

If you were unfortunate enough to have purchased a really expensive foundation that was not your shade and cannot bear to throw it out, LA girl sells a color corrector that will help turn your foundation into a warmer or cooler hue. Plus, you can finally use all those wasted, unused bottles of foundations and customize them with the color corrector to your perfect shade.

Good luck at the makeup counter/drugstores. Hope this helps.
