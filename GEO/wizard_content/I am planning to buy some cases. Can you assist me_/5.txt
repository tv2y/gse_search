Cases come in basically three varieties, either they’re “no-name” Chinese cases (they actually have brand names but you should ignore that), and these come in a huge amount of designs, but generally they are all similar and lightweight.

The second are design cases made by companies like NZXT, which specifically cater to gamers and people who care about their computer case. This is the high end and the price reflects it.

Third are sort of in between, they are Western or Taiwanese or Korean companies that design a whole bunch of cases of all types, this would be like Antec or Thermaltake or Cooler Master or Zalman. They’re neither low end, nor very specialized like NZXT. This is sort of like “mid-range - high end”.

I’ve had all of these cases, and they all work.

Here’s what you should pay attention to:

If you’re a gamer or enthusiast you might really care about the NZXT or Antec/Thermaltake, etc. offering and spend a lot of time browsing through them to find you dream case.

Be aware some of these cases are very heavy, so if you move them around often that’s a serious drawback, but on the other hand they are more stable and may last longer (meaning they’ll last you maybe 60 years, not 20–30).

Decide on a form factor like desktop, tower or mid-tower, or even various micro-cases. The form factor will be the most important in the long run. Full tower is very big, not many people get that, and the desktop cases are mainly used for like media centers nowadays. Mid-tower seems to be popular and it’s a reasonable size.

Some cases (all across the spectrum) have a really annoying blue or red led light. I often ended up taping those over and then not seeing the led feedback.

Some of the first category Chinese cases mimic the features of higher end gaming cases but generally their execution of led lights, etc is poorly done and gaudy at best. I don’t recommend buying design lighting cases in the low end.

However, the cheap, first-category cases can also be super light, which is great if you carry your computer from place to place. If you do move your system getting a heavy case will be a major nuisance.

Handles on cases are awesome if you carry them from place to place, that’s really useful.

Some of the easy-insert and things like that in high end cases I found to be pretty much not-so-important gimmicks. Easy insert for hard drives is good if you like switching them around.

Be sure the case will fit your video card if you happen to get a really large (tall) video card.

So it’s really up to you, they all work. Some of the cheap ones may be a nuisance (minor things) but they still work, and most of them aren’t even a nuisance. When I buy low end cases I always get ones without any LED designs.

If you have any part that stands out, like a really big (tall) air cooler or radiator, a large triple fan GPU or a dual CPU motherboard you will need to check for specific clearance information that not always is available, although lately is becoming easier to find.

Drive bays are becoming something outdated, so if you have large storage need be sure to keep an eye on that.

Dust is a huge maintenance problem, so I always make sure that all intake fans will have good dust filters and they’re easy to clean.

Personally I don’t like acrylic windows as they look dirty and scratch easily, so I look for tempered glass instead, but if you plan moving the system around I wouldn’t recommend it.

Last but not least, favor a classic and sober design that won’t make you feel tired of it fast.

What should I look for when buying a PC case?

You want to make sure it will fit your components, namely, your motherboard and your video card.

The motherboard part is fairly straightforward because the case sizes match the name of the motherboard.

While a smaller motherboard in most cases will fit in a large case the inverse is not true.

A few questions to ask:

*   Where is my PC going to sit?
*   What am I going to used my PC for?
*   How big are the components I have?
*   How often are you going to upgrade components?

Location is a factor. If this is a desktop PC and you have plenty of space then case size is less of a factor. Most “Mid-Tower” cases will meet anyone’s needs, functionally, and asthetics becomes a factor. Are you going to be showcasing a water cooling loop, or RGB setup? Then maybe a larger case would be more suitable so these features aren’t obscured or squashed together. Less desk space, go for a thinner, taller case.

Have a bit more desk space and maybe a shorter wider case suits. If however, you’re really cramped for space, there’s a growing market for much smaller cases. These frequently get marketed to those setting up home cinema PCs, so they don’t swamp all the other boxes and consoles located by your TV.

So what’s the PC’s use case. A home cinema PC is going to be looking for high storage capacity, but isn’t going to be doing much in the way of heavy computing, so airflow, whilst a necessary factor to consider won’t be as important as, say, a 3D graphic designer who needs to keep his CPU cool, or a regular gamer playing the latest graphics intensive AAA games on ultra settings on their 144 Hz 4k monitor.

In those cases You need to be sure that your case can move heat away from the components, so having a larger space inside the case means more air to dissipate heat into, and more space to mount fans to move that air away.

Next factor is what are you actually installing in your case. This can swing both ways. Do I need to fit a 330mm graphics card? Then I need a case that has the space. Do I have a standard ATX motherboard, or am I trying to fit in an E-ATX that needs the height and depth. What about if I have an ITX or micro-ATX board. Do I really need a massive case that’s going to dwarf the motherboard.

Do I need space for a reservoir and pump for my cooling solution? How about extra drive bays for my collection of, ehm, educational videos. These also need to be considered when looking at the size of the case.

Then comes getting things in and out of the case. A small case, even if it technically can fit your chosen motherboard, cooler, and graphics card, will be harder for you to get those pieces in and out off. Less of a factor if you’re not planning on any changes to your setup any time soon. If you’re regularly upgrading your system, however, you’ll appreciate the access a few extra centimetres here and there will give you.

For example, my own rig:

Now as a point of reference, I’m an average computer gamer who uses the same PC for my amateur 3D rendered art (a CPU and GPU intensive hobby that also requires storage space)

I’m using a Lian-Li PC-O11 Dynamic case

Mine is the black version, but the white model is easier to see as a photo

The 2 chamber design, putting the power supply and drive bays behind the motherboard tray, means the case has a wider footprint than many cases, but is also shorter than similar capacity cases, so doesn’t dominate my workspace.

It also means there’s more space to move cables away from the chamber with the major heat sources, allowing cleaner air flow

The multiple locations for SSDs and HDDs means I have space for my files, pictures, 3D assets and games. The side facing fan space in front of the motherboard means I’ve got depth to fit pretty much any graphics card on the market. it also means I can fit 3 fans in the top as well as the three on the side, allowing me to move that hot air about.

The design also allows me easier access for upgrading graphics cards (should they ever become available without the exorbitant markup we’ve been seeing the last couple of years) and the like. without having to contort my fingers to fit into the gaps

At the end of the day, it’s a balancing act between form and function.

Is worth also considering, do you like what it looks like?
