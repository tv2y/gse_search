You should select brand name devices and avoid the very lowest cost devices. Make sure the router has gigabit Ethernet ports (not “Fast Ethernet”, which is 10 times slower.) After that, WiFi is WiFi. There are three versions available today.

WiFi 5 works as well as any. It supports 50 Mbps on the 5 GHz band when you have a strong signal (roughly speaking). Advanced features such as MIMO (multiple spatial streams) do not measurably improve your network performance. You can get a perfectly fine router for $50 to $75.

WiFi 6 adds features, but does not perform substantially better than WiFi 5 in most homes.

WiFi 6E is interesting. If you live in an apartment, you and your neighbors compete for access to the WiFi channels. 6E has new channels in the 6 GHz band. Of course, your computers and other devices have to also support 6E. The price is quite high. For most people, the improvements are real, but very expensive.

What is the best 5ghz WiFi channel to use?

The best one for the circumstances. Yeah, that sounds snarky. It was not intended that way, though. There just is no way to answer your question absolutely without more information. And, the truth is, whatever information you give me right this instant will likely change at another point in time.

I am assuming that your question is about utilization of WiFi. If not, much of this still applies, but not all.

So, the means that the right answer is my favorite answer: it depends.

Interference. This is the most important. There are basically two different kinds of interference: other WiFi systems that are operating in the same channel and anything that can be heard but not understood.

The first category is not all that bad if it is not very active. WiFi was written so that multiple networks could coexist on the same channel and they will cooperate with each other.

The second category can be more detrimental. If you cannot understand a signal, you cannot cooperate with it. Perhaps this is at the crux of problems between democrats and republicans in the US? Hmm. . . But I digress. This category includes systems that are not using WiFi or, if we were talking about both 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz, it could also include WiFi systems on overlapping channels. But luckily, we are not. In 5 GHz, channels do not overlap. They can be bonded, but that does not have the same affect.

There are also two ways of handling interference. Before a WiFi device transmits, it will listen to hear if the air is clear. If it hears interference that it cannot recognize as WiFi, it will backoff and wait a while, if that interference is above a certain level. If it hears another WiFi device, it will backoff and wait a while, if that interference is above another specific level. The level used as a threshold is lower for recognized WiFi devices than for others. So, it is possible for non-WiFi interference to be ignored more readily than WiFi interference. As you can see, this can be a bit complicated.

So, what does this mean for choosing the best channel? The one with the least of amount of interference present is generally the best, as long as you weigh the other factors listed below.

A fair question to ask next is, which channels tend to be the most clear of interference? Good question; I’m glad you asked. The UNII 2 and UNII 2e channels tend to be the most clear, channels 52 through 140. But read on before you decide to automatically choose them.

The UNII 1 channels come next in the list because the power limitations for that portion of the band are the most restrictive. This means that sources of interference are not able to propagate a signal that can be heard as far using these channels. The UNII 3 channels tend to have the most interference because of the regulations regarding power limitations. But read the next section before deciding on either of these portions of the spectrum.

Duty cycle. This is a fancy way of saying, how busy the spectrum is. It could also be considered an aspect of interference. Basically, if interference is present on channel A and it is very strong but not very busy and on channel B there is weak interference but it is very busy, channel A is likely to be a better choice.

Country where you live. There are different regulations for different countries. Actually, there are fewer regulations than there are countries as many countries share the same rules, such as most of Europe does. Some countries use very similar rules, such as the US and Canada. But the gist of this explanation is that those varying rules will affect the answer.

For example, in the US and Canada, the UNII 3 band (5.725 - 5.850 GHz) allows for higher output power than the other 3 bands that make up what we refer to as 5 GHz. Client devices are typically low power, so this does not actually affect what they can do to transmit. But WiFi APs, generally have the ability to transmit at higher power when it is allowed by law. For this reason, channels in the UNII 3 band (148, 153, 157, 161, and 165) offer the possibility of being heard more easily by client devices. In Europe, this is not the case, though.

The UNII 1 band tends to have the lowest power allowed in all regions.

Not all of these bands are present in all countries. And, in some regions, operation above 5.850 GHz is allowed, such as in India.

Client device. Client devices (like your cell phone, laptop, or TV) vary in their capabilities. Some do not support channel 165, or they might not support the UNII 2 or UNII2e bands. This is not so common today, but I still see it happen from time to time. As such, those bands end up not being the best choice, for those devices.

DFS. Dynamic Frequency Selection. In much of the world (back to regulations again) the UNII 2 and UNII2e bands require that any unlicensed radios have the ability to recognize when radar systems are being used in the same band. If radar is present, such as around many airports or any naval base, the UNII 2e band can become unusable. I’m not aware of anyone using radar in the UNII 2 band, though. I really don’t understand why that one also requires DFS. In any case, the process of DFS involves changing channels, but first listening to check if radar is present. That listening time is 30 seconds, which is an eternity for WiFi communications. More advanced WiFi AP designs involve a second 5 GHz radio whose job it is to listen for things like this to speed up the process and so you can hope to avoid the situation from ever happening. Strictly speaking, DFS could also go under the Country Where You Live section as the rules are dependent on a region’s regulations, although they tend to be the same in most.

Light at the end of the tunnel. If you have slogged through all of this information, let me now say that there is good news. Most APs have the ability to make an intelligent choice about which channel to use based on the environment in which they are operating. That is, they can alter their channel when needed. Not all of them do this well. Not all of them do this. Most do, however. Some of them allow you to tweak their thresholds for making a decision. Pretty much all of them allow you to block out some channels or force them to a specific channel. In order to do this well, an AP must either have a second 5 GHz radio (read as more $$) or, on occasion, go offline to listen over other channels. It can then start to build a database of what channels are most likely to be the next best channel should the AP reach a point where it has determined it should change from the channel it is currently operating on. The amount of time that they go offline is minimal and generally not noticed by client devices unless latency sensitive applications, like voice, are being used. In those cases, it is nice to have an AP with enough adjustable settings to allow you to extend the time between offline scans and the amount of time spent scanning offline.

The really good news about this ability to change channels, specifically in the 5 GHz band, is that almost all client devices understand a protocol called 802.11h. The AP will send out an 802.11h message to all clients that are connected before it changes channels, telling those clients what channel it will move to and when. They can then move with it.

Note. With APs changing channels to adjust for interference, there is no way to know if a channel that is clear today will be clear tomorrow, or 1 second from now. Being able to change channels is good, but don’t expect to be able to predict what the spectrum will look like with any real accuracy.

The bad news, and this is pet peeve of mine, is that some client devices have drivers that were written by people that just don’t understand WiFi. The client devices don’t know how to roam, they choose the wrong the AP when more than one is present, and they don’t follow 802.11h messages as they should. When you run into one of these, destroy it and buy a different brand. Luckily, there are not many of them still around. Chromebooks often used these with NICs from Azurewave. Hopefully, Azurewave has corrected this problem in newer NICs, but I have not seen evidence of that yet.

None Wi-Fi is a weak radio signal and designed not to go very far because there is only a limited number of frequencies available. Wi-Fi does not penetrate walls very easily, especially if they have metal HVAC ductwork and electrical wires in them which further limits the range.

Wi-Fi “Extenders” are available that may solve your problem, but my experience with Wi-Fi “Extenders” or “Repeaters” has not been impressive. But you may want to try one.

First, you go from Ethernet which is full duplex to Wireless Access Points (AP’s or Wi-Fi) which are half duplex. Then to half duplex again through extenders, reducing your connection speed by at least 75 percent. Half duplex means it is either sending or receiving, but not both at the same time. Add to that switching overhead. Secondly, stray signals from other nearby Wi-Fi users, cordless phones and ambient noise can also interfere and decrease connection reliability and speed.

Use Wi-Fi extenders only if absolutely necessary and as a last resort, especially if you have nearby Wi-Fi users. You are better off installing an AP. There are many available on Amazon. But you need a wired Ethernet connection.

If the issue began after a recent OS update (within the last 5 years or so), look into your smartphone OS's security features. For example, iOS has a "Private Wi-Fi Address" feature (built-in MAC address spoofing), which might cause problems if your workplace uses MAC address-based filtering.

In some workplaces I manage, both a login and authorized devices with whitelisted MAC addresses are required. If users enable the Private Wi-Fi feature, their devices will be unable to access the Wi-Fi access points. If you're bringing in a new device, you'll need to submit the MAC address to the admin for whitelisting.

Another common issue is an incompatible WiFi SSID. While not as prevalent in the USA, it's known that certain Android and IoT devices elsewhere can have bugs with non-ASCII characters. For this reason, it's recommended to use only ASCII characters and avoid symbols or spaces in the WiFi SSID.

These are the first things I check when troubleshooting these problems.

Some networks also drop support for 'b' or 'g' and only support 'n' for 2.4GHz, or even drop support for 2.4GHz entirely. Some 5GHz WiFi networks might drop support for 'a' or 'n' and only support 'ac'. While I haven't done this with the access points I manage, it's an option for some admin to drop support for older wireless mode.
