Writers can use mythology in their novels and modify its stories, but the appropriateness depends on execution. Changes should aim to avoid confusing readers unless intentional (e.g., mystery resolution). Examples like John Updike's "The Centaur" and Neil Gaiman's "American Gods" show successful adaptations where mythology is remapped or altered with justification.  

Key considerations:  
1. **Reader Clarity**: Changes should be explained implicitly or explicitly (e.g., Persephone willingly joining Hades to escape Demeter).  
2. **Purpose**: Modifications should serve the narrative (e.g., modernizing gods in "American Gods").  
3. **Originality**: Blending myths or creating new twists (e.g., Hades as a stoner) works if it fits the story’s tone.  

Writers have creative freedom, but respecting source material’s core themes helps maintain coherence. Examples include Tolkien’s inspirations and Rowling’s use of mythical creatures.
