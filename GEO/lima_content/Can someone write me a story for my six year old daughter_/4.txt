Markdown Content:
**Creating a life story for a child who's been removed from their birth family is a really important task in social work. It’s one I’ve been involved with many times before, and I’m sure many of you reading this will have been there too. For some of you however, this might be a brand new concept or you’re here looking for advice on doing your first life story.**

This work is really important because it helps children understand their past, make sense of their experiences, and form a sense of identity as they move forward in life. In this week's _Social Work Skills_ we’ll take a brief look at life story work and go over the five key steps for writing a life story for a child.

**Understanding the importance of life story work**

Life story work is really important for children in foster care or those who've been adopted because it helps provide a narrative for them understand why they were removed from their birth family. This, in turn, helps offer a framework to integrate their past into their present and future- tying together their whole life story. By doing so, it helps children process feelings of loss and trauma, and aids in developing a strong sense of self as they grow into adults- no matter how their care revolves around them at any one time.

**A step-by-step guide to writing a life story**

**_Step One: Preparing and planning_**

Preparation is vital for successful life story work, so be if you're not prepared then be prepared to fail!

Start off your life story work by gathering all information about the child’s background, including their birth family, reasons for becoming looked after, and key life events. You can do this by:

**Gathering information**: Collect details from various sources, such as digital case records (LCS, Liquid Logic, Mosaic etc) , court documents, medical records, school reports, and interviews with family members and carers.

**Involving key people**: As part of your work you must make sure you speak with people such as foster carers, fellow professionals, and adoptive parents. These people can all help provide insights into the child’s life.

**Setting clear objectives**: Define what you aim to achieve with the life story work and what you feel needs to be set out within it- ask yourselfwhat story are you trying to tell here? The primary goal here is to help the child make sense of their past and feel secure in their current situation, but set out your objectives for that work (which will be determined by factors such as age, circumstances, and current organisational policy).

**_Step Two: Building trust and engaging the child_**

Creating a good life story requires a trusting relationship between you and the child it’s all about. With this in mind, it’s essential to approach this work with empathy and an aim of build trust. You can do this by:

**Establishing trust**: Spend time building trust with the child you’re working for. Listen actively and validate their feelings to create a safe space for them when sharing their views, wishes, and feelings (this will be age and stage dependent).

**Explaining the process**: Clearly explain the purpose and process of life story work to the child in an age-appropriate manner, and make sure you tell them what your job is too (sounds silly, but a lot of social workers don't do it). Assure them that their story will be handled with the care and respect it warrants and you'll do them justice.

**Involving the child**: Encourage the child you’re working for to participate in creating their own life story. This can include them choosing photos, offering drawings, and suggesting personal items to include in the book.

**_Step Three: Writing the life story_**

When writing the child’s life story, make sure that the narrative is clear, compassionate, and appropriate for the child’s age and stage. These following four points can help you with the planning and writing involved at this point:

**Start with the present**: Begin the story with the child’s current life. Include details about where they live right now, their current school, their best friends, holidays, what they did at Christmas (or other festivals), their likes, hobbies, and favourite activities. This will help the child feel grounded and secure, offering a solid place of identity to build from.

**Move to the past**: Gradually introduce past events into the life story. Be honest but gentle in how you describe things, providing a straightforward but caring explanation of why they were removed from their birth family. Use language that's easy for the child to understand here.

**Make sure you add positive memories**: Include happy memories and positive aspects of their birth family wherever this is possible (trust me, even in the most abject of circumstances you’ll be able to find something). This focus on some positive aspects of their birth family helps balance the story out and acknowledges the complexity of their parents’ own lived experiences.

**Address difficult topics sensitively**: When discussing traumatic events or going into the reasons for separation, do so with care and empathy. Focus on facts without casting blame on birth parents. Make sure that you’re also offering reassurance about the child’s worth and innocence in the situation too, setting out a blame-free narrative that places no burden on them.

**_Step Four: Using visual aids and interactive elements_**

Visual aids and interactive elements can make a life story book more engaging and meaningful for the child you’re writing it for. Here’s three ways to do just that:

**Include photos and drawings**: As mentioned earlier, this is really important so make sure you’re doing things like including photos of the child and their birth family, mentioning their foster family, and including images of significant places that were part of their earlier years. Encourage the child to add their own drawings and artwork too, if they’re able to.

**Add memory items**: Incorporate items such as letters, school certificates, or small mementos that can fit into a book (a personal favourite of mine is to find the ankle and wrist tags from birth, then add these to the book).

**Use digital tools:** Consider using digital formats for interactive elements within the child’s life story. Digital life story books can include videos and voice recordings that help bring a life story to life.

**_Step Five: Reviewing and updating_**

A life story is not a static document, and it should instead be seen as a piece of work that evolves as the child grows up and it grows with them. In this sense, it can be seen like an assessment in need of periodic review. You can embed this approach by:

**Providing regular updates**: Review and update the life story book every 6-12 months. Add new experiences, achievements, and major milestones to keep it current and relevant to the child as they grow up.

**Involving the child in updates**: Encourage the child to help when updating their life story, seeing each update as the setting down of a new chapter in their life. This ongoing involvement helps them to continue to process their experiences and nurture their need for identity. It also offers a sense of progress and development, showing how far they’ve come.

**Offering opportunities for feedback and approval**: Before finalising any updates to the life story, review them with the child to ensure they feel comfortable and agree with what you’re adding- make it something you do with them, not for them.
