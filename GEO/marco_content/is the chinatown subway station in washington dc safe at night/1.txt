Washington, D.C. is a large city with neighborhoods that vary block-by-block. “Bad” areas change over time; the guidance below reflects patterns and public safety data available through May 2024 and practical, street‑level experience. Use it to reduce risk—not as a definitive crime map—and always prioritize situational awareness, travel in groups, and well‑lit routes.

General principles

*   Avoid poorly lit streets, parks after dark, and long isolated stretches without foot traffic or businesses. 
*   Transit hubs can be safe during busy hours but riskier late at night—stay on main platforms, near staff, and in well‑lit areas. 
*   Stick to avenues and streets with active businesses, restaurants, or foot traffic; avoid alleyways and vacant lots. 
*   Rely on recent local information (hotel staff, police advisories, neighborhood apps) for same‑day conditions.

Neighborhoods and corridors commonly flagged as higher risk at night

*   Southeast/Anacostia area (certain blocks east of the Anacostia River): parts of Congress Heights, Anacostia, and neighborhoods farther southeast can have higher violent‑crime rates after dark. Use major thoroughfares and avoid walking alone on side streets. 
*   Northwest pockets at night: portions of lower‑income blocks around Columbia Heights and the western edge of Petworth have had incidents after midnight—prefer 14th Street, U Street, and Georgia Avenue corridors instead of vacant side streets. 
*   7th Street SE and nearby blocks: some segments near Navy Yard/Capitol Riverfront after late hours can be quiet and attract risk once venues close. 
*   Certain blocks around the Southwest Waterfront and the area between I‑395 and the river late at night can be poorly lit and less populated. 
*   Parts of near Northwest rowhouses with vacant storefronts west of Shaw late at night—avoid empty blocks with boarded windows. 
*   Transit corridors after hours: specific Metro stations with lower late‑night ridership (some east‑of‑river stations and isolated surface routes) can feel unsafe; use stations with staff and police presence or get a ride from a vetted ride‑hail company.

Specific situational cautions (high‑risk scenarios regardless of neighborhood)

*   Parks after dark: Rock Creek Park, Fort Dupont Park, Lincoln Park, and other large green spaces are best avoided after sunset. 
*   Isolated commercial blocks with closed businesses: empty retail strips and dark side streets have reduced natural surveillance. 
*   Long detours or missing sidewalks: streets without sidewalks or with poor lighting increase vulnerability. 
*   Showing valuables or counting cash while stationary: reduces safety everywhere. 
*   Walking alone while intoxicated: significantly increases risk and impairs judgment.

Practical safety measures

*   Travel in groups or use ride‑hail/taxi late at night. 
*   Plan routes along major, well‑lit streets with businesses open late. 
*   Keep phone accessible, share ETA with someone, and carry minimal valuables. 
*   Use neighborhood apps (Nextdoor, Citizen), local news, and MPD crime map for up‑to‑date incidents. 
*   If a route feels wrong, cross the street, move into a populated place, or call for a ride.

If you need a short, practical rule of thumb: after dark, stay on main thoroughfares and near active businesses, avoid parks and empty side streets, and prefer group or vehicle travel through areas with lower pedestrian activity.

So, you think you’ve drafted a tweet, an email, a short story, or even a novel. These are different forms of communication, but the process of bringing them to fruition has a necessary, sometimes overlooked step: editing! Unless you’re a professional writer, it’s unlikely that you have an editor who can review your writing regularly. Here are some tips to help you review your own work.

1.   Give your writing some space. Have you ever felt a mix of pure relief and joy when you’ve finished a draft of something? Don’t downplay that feeling and the ability to walk away from your work before you start editing it. You may need minutes, hours, or days, but once you sit back down with what you originally had on the page, you’ll have the thrill of looking at it with fresh eyes. You’ll notice errors you may not have seen the first time. You’ll come to new realizations about its overall tone and structure. If it’s a text or email, maybe you only need a few minutes away from it. If it’s a story or essay, perhaps you’ll need longer. Regardless of what type of work it is, it will help your writing tremendously.
2.   Don’t use overachieving synonyms. Looking at your work for the second, third, or fourth time around may inspire you to spice up your language with longer, more uncommon words. There’s nothing wrong with having a thesaurus nearby, but try to limit the repetition of long, pretentious-feeling words so your work flows well and doesn’t feel too bogged down. At the end of the day, you want it to feel true to you and the message you’re conveying.
3.   Remember who the reader is. Don’t forget your own voice as the writer—but don’t forget who your reader is. Many writers get too close to their work; editing is a chance to try to get out of your own head. Who is your ideal reader? What do you want them to take away from the writing? It’s a unique time to step in their shoes, to make sure your communication is as effective as you’d like it to be.
4.   Kill your darlings. Don’t be scared to remove chunks of your work, even if it feels precious to you. If it’s a passage that’s really tough to part with, try saving it somewhere else, so you can return to it later in your piece or for another work.
5.   Use Grammarly. Last but not least, Grammarly has countless resources for editing your work. Our writing assistant helps you find areas of your writing that are unclear or too wordy, as well as help you find mistakes you might not have caught.

Editing may feel tedious, but it’s just as important as writing itself. For an extra pair of editing eyes on everything you write, download the free Grammarly for Windows and Mac today.
