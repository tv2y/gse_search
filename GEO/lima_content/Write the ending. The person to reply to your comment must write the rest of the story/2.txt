Is it okay to write “the end” at the end of the story?

Yes, it’s perfectly fine, but most people will know the story is over because there are no more words.

The only real reason to put ‘the end’ is if you finish your story unexpectedly as a style choice, or the story happens to finish at the bottom of a page in a PDF.

Most of the time ‘the end’ isn’t necessary. It’s like putting ‘the start’ at the beginning of your story, most readers don’t need to be told the obvious :)

I believe the protocol used to be that you put “mf” (for more follows) in the footer of every page except the last, and you wrote “ends” after the last line. That way if they drop your manuscript on the floor they can tell if they’ve found every page.

However it is literally impossible to do this with a modern word processor* and it isn’t necessary. Generally these days every page has a page number and a page count. The last page will be Page n of n.

It’s also highly unlikely that they will ever print your manuscript before it’s sent to the printer. You can’t fumble the pages of a file… or at least not without being stupidly clumsy with a computer.

So there isn’t a requirement now other than the publishers style guide for submitted manuscripts, which is the first filter to getting read. If you don’t follow it to the letter, nobody there will read a word of what you have written. It’s worse than writing in green ink. If that style guide says to put “The End” at the end then you do so. If it doesn’t say what to put at the end then put whatever you feel like.

* * *

* At least it’s impossible with Word and LibreOffice and that is the file format publishers accept.

Short answer: Yes—it's acceptable, but use it deliberately and sparingly. How and whether to use "The End" depends on genre, tone, audience, and publication context.

When "The End" works

*   Traditional or oral-feel stories: In fairy tales, children’s books, folktales, and some short stories, "The End" signals closure and suits reader expectations.
*   Emphasis and closure: It can give a formal, theatrical finish when you want to underline that the narrative is conclusively over.
*   Self-contained pieces: Very short pieces, vignettes, or humoristic/novella-style texts sometimes benefit from the blunt punctuation of "The End."

When to avoid it

*   Modern literary fiction: Contemporary readers expect closure through narrative technique (final lines, imagery, resolution). "The End" can feel dated or intrusive.
*   Serialized or digital formats: In blogs, magazines, and platforms where posts are part of a flow, it can be unnecessary or jarring.
*   Subtle/ambiguous endings: If you want ambiguity, resonance, or an open ending, adding "The End" undermines that effect.

Practical alternatives

*   Let the last line do the work: A strong, conclusive closing sentence is usually more elegant.
*   Decorative or themed closers: A symbol (—), a short epigraph, or a scene-break device can signal ending without literal text.
*   Epilogues or author notes: For explicit closure or commentary, use an epilogue or brief afterword rather than "The End."

Formatting considerations

*   Children’s picture books: Common and acceptable; ensure page layout gives the phrase room (larger type, centered).
*   Manuscripts for submission: Omit it. Industry standard is to end with the final scene; editors and agents expect no "The End."
*   eBooks and print: Either is fine stylistically; consistency across your work matters more than the choice itself.

Stylistic tips

*   Match tone: If you use "The End," make typography match the story’s voice (plain for classic, playful for whimsical).
*   Avoid redundancy: Don’t add it if the final paragraph already provides unmistakable closure.
*   Consider reader expectations: Younger audiences and certain genres welcome it; literary readers generally do not.

Examples (typical stories)

*   Children’s tale: “...and the frog slept. The End.” (Fits expectations, quick closure.)
*   Noir short story: Final line: “He walked away into the rain.” (No tag—mood sustained.)
*   Flash fiction with twist: End with the twist; adding “The End” can spoil subtlety.

Rule of thumb: Use "The End" when it enhances the reader’s experience or fulfills genre conventions; otherwise rely on craft — a definitive last line, scene, or formal device — to close the story.

It is often done when submitting a story to a publisher.

Before electronic submissions, the story would be sent in physically. In physical form, it is possible that pages get out of order or lost. By typing “The End” on the last page, it is clear that this is the last page. “The end” is rarely published, however, just like your email, phone and address won’t be published. it’s just there for informational purposes during the submission process.

The practice is not as important now, but still acceptable. If your story ends somewhat abruptly and this is your intent, you may wish to include “The End” so that your editor or publisher knows you intended to end the story there and that you did not send a WIP version of the story by accident.

Yes.

See, you must understand that this is based on individual preference so other people will disagree with me. In fact, even you might disagree with me.

Now, let me just assume that the novel you are writing fulfills one of two conditions;

1.   It is the last novel in an entire series, where it will detail the end of the protagonist’s journey.
2.   It is a stand-alone novel that is not going to have a sequel. It will contain details of the entire journey of the main character.

But what I hate most about a lot of the series that I do read is that the protagonist gets a happy ending by the end of the story.

You might not agree with this but I find that to be rather boring, unless you’re planning on doing a sequel. See, when I read a novel, I want that novel to be a description of the protagonist’s life. When you leave the protagonist alive at the end of the novel, it brings up many questions about what they did afterwards, you know?

I want to know their story, the mistakes they made, the decisions they made, their good times, their bad times, their changes in personality throughout their journey. I want to know what happens to them at the very end of their lives. As they die, how does their mindset change? How do they look at the life they have lived as they take their dying breath?

The thing is, when I finish the novel, I want to remember the protagonist as an actual individual I knew about. In real life, we often find it difficult to connect to people and know them to that extent. Books make this a rather easy thing to do if it is done right.

This is why I insist on the main character dying at the end. Yes, it is a sad ending in a way. It is painful. It hurts. But it is also a privilege to see how they lived and what they left behind.

Usually, if an author can make the attachment between the reader and the protagonist so strong that the death of the protagonist becomes very emotional for the reader, it’s a sign that the author is brilliant.

Though, there are series that I have liked that don’t have the main character dying at the very end. The Percy Jackson series and the Harry Potter series do come to mind. I still do have problems with them but I liked them, as a whole.

It depends on the author.

Every author has her/his special way of coming up with a story whether it’s a flash, a short or extended, a novella, a novel, or a novel of several parts/chapters published as a series of chap books.

I am an aspiring writer and I have several WIP novels that I “visit and revisit” from time to time depending on my mood, inspiration, or urgent desire to continue where I stopped. Hence, as it is my personal process (and not because I’ve read about it or it’s a “general rule of writing”), I later found out that there are stories that reveal their ending at some point in writing them.

Some examples:

*   A flash fiction that started with an interesting title (for me) revealed an ending that puts a twist to the whole story—while I’m still writing the first paragraph.
*   A WIP novel gave me an ending that surprised me after I’ve finished writing chapter 11. The ending not only added an interesting and unexpected twist but changed the direction of the story that I had to write it down. Still, the ending provided a need for an Epilogue (not one but two!—in which the Prologue became the first Epilogue). The new Prologue made the story clearer and more interesting as it combined two genres I had not planned at all from the start.
*   An extended short story rolled on its last few paragraphs before it revealed an ending that exposed additional stories that makes it a novel of five parts—complete with subtitles, epigraphs, and synopsis! (I couldn’t ask for more.)
*   A story concept that started with a curious title gave me its ending while I’ve only written 375 words of its draft. (Now, I have to write it down as it requires more immediate attention than the story itself. And guess what, it’s a f*****g novel!)

So, is it necessary to know the ending of a story while it’s being written?

NO. The story will provide the ending. If it does not, the story is not worth writing.
