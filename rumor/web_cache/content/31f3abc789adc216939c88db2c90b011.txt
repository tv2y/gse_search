Title: What’s in the 2025 House Republican Tax Bill | Bipartisan Policy Center

URL Source: https://bipartisanpolicy.org/explainer/whats-in-the-2025-house-republican-tax-bill/

Markdown Content:
**President Trump signed H.R. 1, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, into law on July 4, 2025. This explainer covers the House-proposed version only. For an explainer on the tax provisions of the final law, see here.**

The following is a section-by-section for the tax title of H.R. 1, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act that passed the House of Representatives on May 22, 2025. Cost estimates reflect the Joint Committee on Taxation’s June 2 score.

On net, the tax title is projected to add approximately $3.8 trillion to federal deficits over the next decade (fiscal years 2025-2034). The tax cuts are partially offset by spending cuts and fee revenue in other parts of the bill. See more from BPC on the reconciliation package here.

_For a summary of when recent changes go into effect, please read more here._

**Subtitle A: Individual and Family Tax Policy**

_Part I: Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) Extensions_

**Sec. 110001: Lower individual tax rates**

*   Permanently extends the lower individual income tax rates and thresholds from TCJA, currently scheduled to expire after 2025.
*   Provides an extra inflation adjustment to the bottom six brackets (not the top bracket), which results in lower tax bills for those taxpayers.
*   **Costs $2.2 trillion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on TCJA’s changes to individual tax rates, see here.

**Sec. 110002: Standard deduction**

*   Permanently extends the doubled standard deduction from TCJA, currently scheduled to revert to approximately half its current amount after 2025.
*   Provides an extra inflation adjustment to the standard deduction, which results in lower tax bills for taxpayers taking the standard deduction.
*   Adds $1,000 to the standard deduction for single taxpayers and $2,000 for married couples from 2025-2028, which results in lower tax bills for taxpayers taking the standard deduction.
*   **Costs $1.3 trillion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on TCJA’s changes to the standard deduction, see here.

**Sec. 110003: Personal exemptions**

*   Permanently repeals personal exemptions, currently scheduled to return in 2026.
*   Personal exemptions acted like an additional deduction based on family size. Under current law (TCJA expiration) they are expected to be valued at $5,300 in 2026.
*   **Raises $1.9 trillion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on TCJA’s changes to personal exemptions, see here.

**Sec. 110004: Child Tax Credit (CTC)**

*   Permanently extends the $2,000 per child credit amount from TCJA, currently scheduled to revert to $1,000 per child after 2025.
*   Provides a temporary $500 per child boost (so that the maximum credit is $2,500 per child) from 2025-2028.
*   Sets the per-child amount to grow with inflation from 2029 on, after the temporary $500 per child boost expires. The inflation adjustment will be from the $2,000 per child amount. For example, if inflation increases 2.5% per year over four years, the CTC would be $2,200 in 2029.
*   Permanently extends the refundable amount of the CTC (currently $1,700 per child) and adjusted for inflation each year.
*   Permanently extends the CTC phase-out, which starts at $200,000 for single taxpayers and $400,000 for married taxpayers.
*   Requires Social Security numbers (SSNs) for the parent, spouse (if taxpayers are married filing jointly), and child(ren) to claim. Current law requires an SSN only for the child.
*   **Costs $797 billion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on the House Republican tax bill’s changes to family tax policy, see here.

**Sec. 110005: Pass-through deduction**

*   Permanently extends the 20% deduction—and expands it to 23%—for pass-through business income from TCJA, currently scheduled to expire after 2025. This deduction is also referred to as 199A or the qualified business income (QBI) deduction.
*   Eases the rules that limit the deduction for high-income taxpayers.
*   Adds a category of eligible income for the deduction, qualified business development company interest income.
*   **Costs $820 billion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on TCJA’s pass-through deduction, see here.

**Sec. 110006: Estate taxes**

*   Permanently extends a higher estate tax exemption from TCJA, currently scheduled to expire after 2025.
*   Sets the exemption at $15 million in 2026, adjusted for inflation thereafter.
*   **Costs $212 billion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on TCJA’s changes to estate taxes, see here.

**Sec. 110007: Alternative minimum tax (AMT)**

*   Permanently extends the higher AMT exemption amounts and thresholds from TCJA, currently scheduled to revert to much lower amounts after 2025 (i.e., subjecting more income to the AMT).
*   Resets the base year upon which inflation adjustments are made, from 2017 to 2025, reducing AMT relief relative to TCJA extension.
*   **Costs $1.3 trillion** from FY2025-2034 (**previous estimate was $1.4 trillion**).
*   For more on TCJA’s changes to the AMT, see here.

**Sec. 110008: Mortgage interest deduction**

*   Permanently extends the $750,000 limitation on mortgage interest indebtedness allowed for the mortgage interest deduction, currently scheduled to increase to a higher $1,000,000 limit (i.e., allowing higher mortgage interest deductions) after 2025.
*   For a home purchase with a 20% down payment, this effectively begins limiting the mortgage interest deduction for purchases above $937,500 ($750,000 x (1-0.20)).
*   **Raises $6 billion** from FY2025-2034, combined with Secs. 110009 and 110010.
*   For more on TCJA’s changes to the mortgage interest deduction, see here.

**Sec. 110009: Casualty loss deduction**

*   Permanently extends the limit on personal casualty losses from TCJA to only losses in a presidentially-declared disaster area and only to the extent net disaster losses exceed 10% of income.
*   **Raises $6 billion** from FY2025-2034, combined with Secs. 110008 and 110010.
*   For more on TCJA’s changes to deductions, see here.

**Sec. 110010: Miscellaneous itemized deductions**

*   Permanently extends the repeal of miscellaneous itemized deductions from TCJA.
*   **Raises $6 billion** from FY2025-2034, combined with Secs. 110008 and 110009.
*   For more on TCJA’s changes to deductions, see here.

**Sec. 110011: Limit on itemized deductions**

*   Permanently extends the repeal of the pre-TCJA overall limitation on itemized deductions, called the “Pease” limitation.
*   Adds a new limitation on itemized deductions that effectively reduces the value of SALT deductions for taxpayers in the top (37%) bracket by five percentage points and all other itemized deductions for taxpayers in the top bracket by two percentage points. In other words, a $100 SALT deduction for a taxpayer in the top bracket would equal a $32 reduction in tax liability instead of $37, and a $100 other itemized deduction would equal a $35 reduction in tax liability instead of $37.
*   **Raises $41 billion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on TCJA’s changes to deductions, see here.

**Secs. 110012-110019: Additional tax extensions**

*   Extends a number of smaller tax provisions from TCJA, including its repeal of the tax exclusion for bicycle commuting reimbursements, the exclusion and deduction for moving expenses (other than for members of the Armed Forces), and tax provisions concerning Achieving a Better Life Experience (ABLE) accounts for people with disabilities.
*   **Raises a combined $14 billion** from FY2025-2034, though the only provision with a budget impact of more than (plus or minus) $400 million over 10 years is the moving expenses provision (raises $14 billion).

_Part 2: New Tax Cuts_

**Sec. 110101: No tax on tips**

*   Adds a deduction for qualified tips from 2025 through 2028.
*   There is no limit on the amount of the deduction, though the deduction is generally limited to occupations that the Treasury Secretary certifies “traditionally and customarily” received tips before 2025. The deduction is also not available for taxpayers making above $160,000 in 2025, adjusted for inflation in future years.
*   The tips deduction is available whether a taxpayer takes the standard deduction or itemizes their deductions.
*   **Costs $40 billion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on no tax on tips, see here.

**Sec. 110102: No tax on overtime**

*   Adds a deduction for qualified overtime compensation from 2025 through 2028.
*   The deduction applies to the _extra compensation_ a taxpayer earns in overtime, not their entire compensation _during_ For example, if a taxpayer makes $20 per hour regularly and $30 per hour in overtime, her deduction would apply to the $10 per hour in extra compensation (_not_ the full $30 per hour earned in overtime).
*   The deduction is also not available for taxpayers making above $160,000 in 2025, adjusted for inflation in future years.
*   The overtime deduction is available whether a taxpayer takes the standard deduction or itemizes their deductions.
*   **Costs $124 billion** from FY2025-2034.

**Sec. 110103: Larger standard deduction for seniors**

*   Creates an additional standard deduction for taxpayers over 65 from 2025 through 2028.
*   The deduction is equal to $4,000 per individual, and phases out at a 4% rate for single taxpayers making above $75,000 and married taxpayers making above $150,000.
*   The senior deduction is available whether a taxpayer takes the standard deduction or itemizes their deductions.
*   **Costs $66 billion** from FY2025-2034 **(previous estimate was $72 billion**).

**Sec. 110104: No tax on auto loan interest**

*   Creates a new deduction for certain auto loan interest from 2025 through 2028.
*   The deduction is limited to $10,000 overall and phases out at a 20% rate for single taxpayers making above $100,000 and married taxpayers making above $200,000.
*   The deduction only applies to vehicles that are completed (i.e., assembled) in the U.S.
*   The auto loan deduction is available whether a taxpayer takes the standard deduction or itemizes their deductions.
*   **Costs $58 billion** from FY2025-2034.

**Sec. 110105: 45F Credit for Employer-Provided Child Care**

*   Expands the 45F credit, starting in 2026, in a number of ways: 1) increases the credit rate for a business’s child care expenditures from 25% to 40% (and 50% for small businesses with receipts less than $31 million); 2) increases the maximum credit from $150,000 to $500,000 ($600,000 for small businesses); 3) allows businesses to receive the credit for contracting with a third party to provide child care services; and 4) allows businesses to jointly own or operate child care facilities and claim the credit.
*   **Costs $731 million** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on the House Republican tax bill’s changes to family tax policy, see here.

**Sec. 110106: 45S Credit for Employer-Provided Paid Family and Medical Leave**

*   Permanently extends the 45S credit from TCJA, currently scheduled to expire after 2025.
*   Allows employers to use the credit to offset their premiums paid for insurance that provides their employees with paid leave.
*   **Costs $5 billion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on the House Republican tax bill’s changes to family tax policy, see here.

**Secs. 110107-110108: Adoption credit**

*   Makes up to $5,000 of the adoption credit refundable (i.e., available even if a taxpayer has less than $5,000 in federal income tax liability) in 2025, and adjusts that refundable amount for inflation from 2026 onwards.
*   The current credit is up to $17,280 for taxpayers making up to $259,190, but is non-refundable.
*   **Costs $2 billion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on the House Republican tax bill’s changes to family tax policy, see here.

**Secs. 110109-110111, 110113: Education provisions**

*   Sec. 110109 creates a non-refundable income tax credit, from 2026 through 2029, for charitable contributions made to an organization that grants scholarships to elementary or secondary students making less than 300% of the area’s median gross income. The maximum credit is the higher of $5,000 or up to 10% of a taxpayer’s income. Total credits nationwide are subject to a volume cap of $5 billion per year.
*   Sec. 110110 and Sec. 110111 expand eligible expenses for 529 accounts to certain elementary, secondary, home school, and postsecondary career or apprenticeship credentialing expenses.
*   Sec. 110113 makes the $5,250 tax exclusion for employer-provided educational assistance permanent (it is currently scheduled to expire after 2025) and adjusts it for inflation after 2026.
*   **Costs $32 billion** from FY2025-2034.

**Sec. 110112: Charitable deduction for non-itemizers**

*   Creates a deduction for charitable contributions from 2025 through 2028 for those who do not itemize deductions, equal to $150 for single taxpayers and $300 for married couples.
*   **Costs $7 billion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on the charitable deduction, see here.

**Sec. 110114: Personal casualty losses**

*   Expands the list of types of disasters for which a taxpayer may claim a deduction.
*   **Costs $60 million** from FY2025-2034.

**Secs. 110115-110116: Trump accounts**

*   Creates a new tax-advantaged savings account called a Trump account.
*   Accounts are available only for children under 8 in a given year, and the contribution limits are $5,000 per year adjusted for inflation.
*   Amounts from Trump accounts may not be distributed to beneficiaries until after they have reached age 18, and amounts used for higher education, post-secondary career or apprenticeship credentialing, starting a small business, or the purchase of a principal residence are subject to capital gains tax rates that are generally lower than individual income tax rates.
*   A pilot program provides a $1,000 credit to Trump accounts for U.S. citizens born from 2025 through 2028.
*   **Costs $17 billion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on the House Republican tax bill’s changes to family tax policy, see here.

_Part 3: Health Tax Policy_

**Secs. 110201-110203: Health reimbursement arrangements**

*   Codifies into law individual choice health reimbursement arrangements (ICHRAs) established during the first Trump administration as Custom Health Option and Individual Care Expense (CHOICE) arrangements, effective 2026.
*   Allows individuals enrolled in CHOICE arrangements to purchase health insurance on individual exchanges.
*   Provides a $100 per month employer credit for the first year an employee is enrolled in a CHOICE arrangement, and a $50 per month employer credit for the second year; credit amounts adjusted for inflation from 2027 onwards.
*   **Costs $492 million** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on the House Republican tax bill’s changes to health tax policy, see here.

**Secs. 110204-110213: Health savings accounts**

These sections make a number of expansions to health savings accounts (HSAs), including:

*   Allowing Medicare Part A participants to contribute to HSAs (though not if they are enrolled in other parts of Medicare);
*   Allowing individuals with direct primary care arrangements to contribute to HSAs (if the fees do not exceed $150 per month, adjusted for inflation);
*   Allowing individuals with bronze and catastrophic plans on individual health exchanges to contribute to HSAs;
*   Allowing individuals with access to worksite health services to contribute to HSAs;
*   Expanding eligible HSA expenses to gym and fitness memberships;
*   Allowing both spouses in a married couple to make catch-up contributions to one HSA account;
*   Allowing more individuals to make FSA or HRA rollovers to HSAs;
*   Allowing individuals to reimburse for HSA expenses in the 60-day period between being covered by a high-deductible health plan (HDHP) and establishing an HSA;
*   Allowing HSA contributions even if one’s spouse is covered under a health FSA; and
*   Doubling HSA contribution limits for single taxpayers making under $75,000 (from $4,300 to $8,600) and married taxpayers making under $150,000 (from $8,550 to $17,100).
*   **Costs $43 billion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on the House Republican tax bill’s changes to health tax policy, see here.

**Subtitle B: Business Tax Policy**

_Part 1: TCJA Extensions_

**Sec. 111001: Bonus depreciation/full expensing**

*   Extends TCJA’s 100% bonus depreciation for business investments in machinery, equipment, and other short-lived assets from Jan. 19, 2025, through 2029. See the table below for a comparison of bonus depreciation under current law vs. the 2025 House GOP tax bill.
*   **Costs $37 billion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on TCJA’s bonus depreciation, see here.

##### **Table 5. Bonus Depreciation, TCJA Expiration vs. House Republican Tax Bill**

| Year | Current Law (TCJA Expires) | House Republican Tax Bill |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2025 | 40% | 100% (from Jan. 19, 2025) |
| 2026 | 20% | 100% |
| 2027 | 0% | 100% |
| 2028 | 0% | 100% |
| 2029 | 0% | 100% |
| 2030 onwards | 0% | 0% |

**Sec. 111002: R&D expensing**

*   Restores full expensing for businesses’ domestic research and development (R&D) investments from 2025 through 2029. Since 2022, domestic R&D investments have had to be amortized (i.e., deductions spread out) over five years.
*   Foreign R&D investments must still be amortized over 15 years.
*   **Costs $23 billion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on TCJA’s changes to R&D expensing, see here.

**Sec. 111003: Business interest deductions**

*   Restores a more generous interest deduction limit that was in place under TCJA (from 2018 through 2021), for deductions from 2025 through 2029.
*   The more generous interest deduction limit is based on earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization (EBITDA) rather than earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT). The EBIT standard went into effect in 2022.
*   **Costs $40 billion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on TCJA’s changes to interest deductions, see here.

**Secs. 111004-111005: International tax rates**

*   Extends lower international tax rates on certain income earned by U.S. companies abroad (under the Global Intangible Low-Taxed Income or GILTI tax) and on income earned by U.S. companies that is derived from exports (under the Foreign-Derived Intangible Income or FDII deduction), with minor modifications.
*   Under TCJA, the GILTI rate is scheduled to rise from 10.5% to 13.125% in 2026. The effective tax rate on FDII is scheduled to rise from 13.125% to 16.406%. The House Republican tax bill extends lower rates of 10.668% and 13.335% for GILTI and FDII.
*   Also extends the lower tax rate on base erosion payments to foreign affiliates of U.S. companies (the Base Erosion and Anti-Abuse Tax or BEAT) and the special treatment of certain U.S. tax credits under BEAT.
*   Under TCJA, the BEAT rate is scheduled to rise from 10% to 12.5% in 2026. The law also scheduled a change in the calculation of BEAT so that U.S. tax credits no longer reduce BEAT liability. The House Republican tax bill cancels both of these scheduled changes and sets the BEAT rate at 10.1%.
*   **Costs $165 billion** from FY2025-2034 **(previous estimate was $174 billion**).
*   For more on TCJA’s changes to international rates, see here.

**Sec. 111006: Business meals**

*   Modifies TCJA’s limit on the deductibility of business meals, by only allowing deductions for meals which are provided in the course of providing entertainment sold to customers.
*   **Costs $909 million** from FY2025-2034.

_Part 2: New Tax Cuts_

**Sec. 111101: Partial expensing for structures**

*   Creates temporary, 100% depreciation for non-residential structures (i.e., real estate) that is placed in service in the U.S. before Jan. 1, 2033, for which construction begins between Jan. 19, 2025, and Jan. 1, 2029, and that is used for “qualified production activity.”
*   “Qualified production activity” includes manufacturing, refining, agricultural production, or chemical production. Offices, lodging, and parking are among the types of properties not eligible for the deduction.
*   **Costs $148 billion** from FY2025-2034.

**Sec. 111102: Opportunity zones**

*   Ends TCJA’s OZ period early, on Dec. 31, 2026, instead of Dec. 31, 2028, in order to create a new OZ period with new rules under the House Republican tax bill.
*   Establishes a new OZ period from Jan. 1, 2027 through Dec. 31, 2033.
*   Allows states to establish new OZs, with requirements for minimum poverty rates and maximum median family income. Also adds new requirements that a minimum proportion of OZs be in rural areas.
*   Provides a bonus “basis boost” for OZs in rural areas, at 30% after five years of investment instead of 10%.
*   **Costs $5 billion** from FY2025-2034.

**Sec. 111103: Expensing for small businesses**

*   Increases the sec. 179 expensing allowance targeted at small businesses, from a maximum of $1.25 million in 2025 to a maximum of $2.5 million in 2025.
*   Raises the threshold at which the allowance begins to phase out, from $3.13 million in sec. 179 property in 2025 to $4 million in sec. 179 property in 2025.
*   Both amounts above are indexed for inflation after 2025.
*   **Costs $25 billion** from FY2025-2034.

**Secs. 111104-111105: Information reporting**

*   Restores the pre-2022 thresholds for IRS information reporting from third-party settlement organizations (e.g., Venmo, StubHub).
*   The American Rescue Plan Act (ARPA) of 2021 established a $600 threshold for information reporting, with no transaction threshold. Before ARPA, the threshold was $20,000 and 200 transactions. The House Republican tax bill restores the latter thresholds.
*   Increases, from $600 to $2,000, the threshold for IRS information reporting for payments to contractors and other sole proprietors, effective 2026. The $2,000 threshold is indexed for inflation.
*   **Costs $13 billion** from FY2025-2034.

**Secs. 111106-111107, Secs. 111109-111110, Sec. 111112: Additional tax cuts**

*   Sec. 111107 excludes from taxation 25% of bank or insurance interest on real estate loans secured by farms and ranches, effective in years following enactment.
*   Sec. 111108 extends expensing allowances to sound recording production costs incurred in the U.S., up to $150,000 per year, effective in years following enactment.
*   Sec. 111111 increases the gross receipts threshold for manufacturers from $25 million to $80 million, which enables manufacturers under the new limits to access a number of tax benefits. The new threshold is effective 2026.
*   Sec. 111112 restores a higher standard for a real estate investment trust (REIT) to be considered a REIT, from holding not more than 20% of its assets in securities of taxable REIT subsidiaries to holding not more than 25%.
*   **Costs a combined $20 billion** from FY2025-2034 (**previous estimate was $17 billion**).

**Sec. 111108: Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC)**

*   Increases state allocations for LIHTC by 12.5% from 2026 through 2029.
*   Lowers the threshold for LIHTC that applies to buildings financed with tax-exempt bonds, from a minimum of 50% bond financing to 25% bond financing, from 2026 through 2029.
*   **Costs $14 billion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on the House Republican’s tax bills changes to housing tax policy, see here.

**Sec. 111111: Clean fuel production credit**

*   Extends the clean fuel production credit, currently scheduled to expire after 2027, through 2031. Limits the credit to fuel derived from feedstock produced and grown in the U.S., Mexico, or Canada.
*   **Costs $45 billion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on TCJA’s changes to energy tax policy, see here.

**Subtitle C: Pay-Fors**

_Part 1: Revenue Raisers_

**Secs. 112001-112016: Changes to clean energy tax provisions**

*   For BPC’s comprehensive analysis of the House Republican tax bill’s changes to energy tax provisions, see here and here.
*   **Raises $570 billion** from FY2025-2034 (**previous estimate was $559 billion**).

**Sec. 112017: Sports franchise deductions**

*   Limits the proportion of professional sports teams’ intangibles (e.g., contract values, brand value, goodwill) that can be amortized over 15 years to 50% of the value of those intangibles, down from 100% under current law. Effective upon enactment.
*   **Raises $991 million** from FY2025-2034.

**Sec. 112018: State and local tax deduction**

*   Extends TCJA’s $10,000 SALT cap, but adds a new, larger cap for certain taxpayers described below.
*   The new cap is $40,000, effective 2025, and phases down at a 30% rate for individuals making over $500,000.
*   Increases $40,000 and $500,000 amounts by 1% each year from 2026 through 2033.
*   Applies new limits to SALT deductions for pass-through businesses, partially overturning 2020 guidance from the IRS that allowed for SALT pass-through workarounds (though continuing to allow workarounds for certain types of businesses).
*   Counts charitable payments to states—those made in efforts to work around the SALT cap—towards the SALT cap if the credit the taxpayer receives back for payment is greater than or equal to 25%.
*   **Raises $787 billion** from FY2025-2034 (**previous estimate was $916 billion**).
*   For more on business SALT, see here.

**Sec. 112019: Deductions for employee compensation**

*   Makes stricter the limits on deductibility for certain highly compensated employees by accounting for employees who may be compensated by several different affiliates of the same parent company.
*   The $1 million limit on deductibility for the CEO, CFO, and three other most highly-compensated employees of publicly held corporations (eight others from 2027 onwards) remains in place.
*   **Raises $16 billion** from FY2025-2034.

**Secs. 112020-112025: Changes to tax rules for foundations, colleges, universities, and other tax-exempt organizations**

*   Sec. 112020 expands the excise tax on over $1 million in compensation (or “parachute payments”) for highly-paid employees of tax-exempt organizations, applying the rule to all employees, former or current, receiving compensation in excess of $1 million.
*   Sec. 112021 creates a graduated rate structure for the excise tax on college endowments, currently 1.4%. The provision allows for an excise rate as high as 21% for institutions with per-student endowments above $2 million.
*   Sec. 112022 creates a graduated rate structure for the excise tax on private foundations’ net investment income, currently 1.39%. The provision allows for an excise rate as high as 10% for foundations with assets above $5 billion.
*   Sec. 112023 modifies the rules for the tax on excess business holdings of private foundations, disregarding from the “excess” rules certain purchases of employee-owned stock.
*   Sec. 112024 adds to unrelated business income tax (UBIT) liability for tax-exempt organizations the value of transportation fringe and parking benefits; houses of worship are exempt.
*   Sec. 112025 adds to UBIT liability for tax-exempt organizations any income that is derived from research that is not freely available to the public.
*   **Raises $29 billion** from FY2025-2034 (**previous estimate was $33 billion**).

**Sec. 112026: Net operating losses for pass-throughs**

*   Makes permanent TCJA’s limits on net operating losses for pass-through businesses. The limit is $313,000 for single taxpayers in 2025 and $626,000 for married couples, adjusted for inflation.
*   **Raises $27 billion** from FY2025-2034.

**Sec. 112027: Floor on corporate charitable deductions**

*   Sets a 1%-of-taxable income floor for corporations seeking to deduct charitable contributions, adding to an existing 10%-of-taxable income ceiling.
*   **Raises $17 billion** from FY2025-2034.

**Sec. 112028: International tax provisions**

*   Increases withholding taxes on foreign individuals and businesses with income earned in the U.S., if those individuals and businesses are from a country that has levied “discriminatory” taxes against the U.S., including and not limited to digital services taxes (DSTs) or an undertaxed profits rule (UTPR).
*   Increases the Base Erosion and Anti-Abuse Tax (BEAT) rate from 10% to 12.5% for foreign businesses from countries that have levied “discriminatory” taxes against the U.S., and generally increases the tax base on which those taxes are applied for those foreign businesses.
*   **Raises $116 billion** from FY2025-2034.

**Sec. 112029: Excise tax on firearms silencers**

*   Eliminates the $200 excise tax on firearm silencers.
*   **Costs $1 billion** from FY2025-2034.

**Sec. 112030: De minimis trade**

*   Removes the tariff de minimis exemption for commercial shipping imports, and creates new civil penalties for violating other trade laws using the de minimis exemption, effective July 1, 2027.
*   **Raises $39 billion** from FY2025-2034.

**Sec. 112032: Partnership payments**

*   Clarifies Treasury’s regulatory authority applying to payments for property or services made in business partnerships.
*   **Raises $12 billion** from FY2025-2034.

_Part 2: Tax Credit Eligibility_

**Secs. 112101-112103, 112105: Tax credit and Medicare coverage limits for undocumented immigrants**

*   Sec. 112101 eliminates the ability of undocumented immigrants to claim premium tax credits, effective 2027.
*   Sec. 112102 prevents asylum applicants, immigrants granted parole, those with temporary protected status, and additional categories of immigrants from claiming premium tax credits, effective 2027.
*   Sec. 112103 prevents lawfully-present immigrants with household income below 100% of the federal poverty level from claiming premium tax credits, effective 2026.
*   Sec. 112105 requires Social Security numbers for claimants of the American Opportunity Tax Credit and Lifetime Learning Credit, and for their spouses and child(ren) if applicable, effective 2026.
*   **Raises $130 billion** from FY2025-2034 (**previous estimate was $118 billion**).

**Sec. 112104: Remittance tax**

*   Creates a 3.5% excise tax on remittances (i.e., payments) from people within the U.S. to people outside of the U.S., effective 2026.
*   **Raises $22 billion** from FY2025-2034 (**previous estimate was $22 billion**).

_Part 3: Improper Payments and Recapture of Excess Premium Tax Credits_

**Secs. 112201-112203: Modifications to the premium tax credit**

*   Sec. 112201 generally increases requirements for health insurance exchanges to verify individuals’ and families’ eligibility for premium tax credits, effective 2028.
*   Sec. 112202 prevents enrollment in plans using a special enrollment period, created in 2021, for individuals making less than 150% of the federal poverty level. It does not prevent those individuals from otherwise enrolling in coverage if they are eligible, or individuals who already used the special enrollment period, but prevents individuals from using the special enrollment period going forward (effective three months after the date of enactment).
*   Sec. 112203 eliminates the recapture limit on excess payments of premium tax credits.
*   **Raises $102 billion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on the House Republican tax bill’s changes to health tax policy, see here.

**Sec. 112204: Artificial intelligence (AI) tools for Medicare**

*   Allocates $25 million for the Health and Human Services Department (HHS) to contract with AI vendors and data scientists to identify and recoup improper Medicare payments.
*   For more on the House Republican tax bill’s changes to health tax policy, see here.
*   **Costs $25 million** from FY2025-2034.

**Sec. 112205: Employee Retention Tax Credit (ERTC)**

*   Prevents any ERTC refunds for claims made after January 31, 2024.
*   Enhances penalties on “ERTC promoters” who assisted with erroneous or fraudulent claims, and applies those penalties retroactively.
*   Provides the IRS six years to pursue erroneous or fraudulent claims of the ERTC, from the date on which the original return is filed or the claim for the ERTC is made (whichever is later).
*   **Raises $6 billion** from FY2025-2034.

**Sec. 112206: Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC)**

*   Creates a new certification program for the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) to determine child qualifying status and address duplicate claims; creates Treasury task force to address administration and improper payments.
*   **Raises $15 billion** from FY2025-2034.
*   For more on the House Republican tax bill’s changes to family tax policy, see here.

**Secs. 112207-112209: Tax administration reforms**

*   Sec. 112207 creates a task force on the termination of the IRS Direct File program originally established in the Inflation Reduction Act.
*   Sec. 112208 increases penalties for unauthorized disclosures of taxpayer information.
*   Sec. 112209 prevents the Treasury Department and the IRS from creating regulations on the deductibility of contingency fees, used by some attorneys.
*   Has negligible revenue effects from FY2025-2034.
