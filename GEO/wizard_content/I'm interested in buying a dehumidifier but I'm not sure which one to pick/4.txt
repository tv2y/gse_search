Having used a dehumidifier, in various locations for many years now - my advise is to consider seriously how the condensed water is going to be dealt with. If you have a floor drain - you are in luck. Have a short drain hose that directly drips into the drain.

The answer is given in US regulations 10 CFR 430.2 [Federal Register :: Request Access] for consumer product efficiency.

A room dehumidifier is defined as a “portable” dehumidifier. It is designed to be placed in the room it is to dry, typically a basement or crawlspace, without any ductwork. Most units in this category are in the 20–49 pint per day size range. Some have a built in “bucket” that must be emptied. Larger ones will have a hose that runs to a drain or condensate pump to get rid of the water. The common purpose for these units is to capture moisture in a basement or crawlspace before it gets up into the living space.

A “whole-house” dehumidifier is typically installed with ducting to work with your A/C, heat pump or furnace system to dry out all rooms of your house instead of just the basement. They will be larger in capacity, typically 50 - 150 pints per day. They are almost always installed with a hose to run the water to a drain. They will keep the entire house comfortable. Depending on where the humidity is coming from, a portable unit may still be installed in the basement/crawlspace.

It really depends upon the size and use of the room:

For bedrooms, and spaces typically less than 200 SF in size, I would recommend the dehumidifier, be placed relatively close to the vent at an exterior window. When the air kicks on, the dehumidifier is adjacent to collect a portion of the air and filter the moisture from it - but more importantly, it collects any drafts and moisture coming from the exterior window. While fresh air from the exterior can have moisture in it, it typically runs through 1–2 air filters to collect the dust, and the dry dust also collects moisture as the air passes through it. However, any penetration within a wall opening poses the greatest risk of moisture and air leakage. If a room is very humid compared to the rest of the spaces, the problem is usually from one of the wall penetrations to the exterior.

For enclosed spaces such as basements or interior rooms, on a wall opposite of the air ducts is usually a good place to start. In the event air is provide from all around, somewhere in the middle of the room is usually best.

For rooms over 200 SF, more than one humidifier may be needed depending upon the age and amount of air and moisture leakage the building or space has. If the entire building or house needs to be dehumidified, an in room humidifier will be very inefficient. In this case, a dehumidifier connected to your hvac ducts would be best and then just supplement as required with a moveable humidifier.

As a final note, the goal of dehumidifiers are to remove excess moisture from spaces to make them “comfortable” to the users. While many doctors recommend dehumidifiers for treating illnesses and specific medical conditions such as asthma, removing too much moisture from the air can be damaging to the building space. Removing too much moisture from wood-based products will cause the wood to shrink and crack/splinter. Plastics, when exposed to direct sunlight and heat, will expand and contract, and over time, break down. While rust may be reduced from metal based products, the oils from our skin can still damage their function and addition oil or lubrication may be needed for machinery, hinges, and other moving parts. Likewise, constant and quick changes of moisture and humidity can be very damaging to brick, concrete, and stucco. Drywall can become brittle and crack under constant and quick changes of humidity. Make sure to check the manufacturer’s recommend settings to prevent building damaged from little humidity or quick changes of humidity.

The ideal relative humidity for health and comfort is somewhere between 30-50% humidity, according to the Mayo Clinic. This means that the air holds between 30-50% of the maximum amount of moisture it can contain.

Too much humidity can negatively impact your comfort, home, possessions and health. When the air around you has too much humidity, your body can't regulate its internal temperature through the process of evaporation. If you're in a humid room that's the same temperature as a room with less humidity, the humid room will feel warmer to you.

Too much humidity won't just make you uncomfortable, it can also make you sick. High moisture levels encourage the growth of fungus, mold and dust mites, which can create breathing difficulties for people with asthma and allergies.

When it comes to your home, overly humid air creates a breeding ground for mold and mildew, which results in costly damage. Over time, high humidity levels can cause wood to rot and attract unwanted insects. Too much humidity also causes swelling in furniture and other items, causing them to warp.

Your personal comfort, is the best way to know what the proper setting for your dehumidifier is. People very greatly in what is comfortable to them.

A look at a psychometric chart will provide you with a window in the middle of the shoe ( The graph is shaped like a shoe ) This is the comfort range for most human beings, and it is very handy for working out efficient air handling, and temperature control of large buildings, and sometimes special scientific, and manufacturing applications.

This comfort zone is the relation ship between humidity, and temperatures where people feel comfortable. By varying the temperature, and humidity with in a conditioned space, you can be made to feel comfortable at higher, or lower temperatures, based on the humidity (amount of moisture in the air ).

For instance, you will most likely be comfortable at 70 degrees F, at about 80% RH ( Relative Humidity ) and you will most likely be comfortable at 80 degrees, and 20 % RH. So most people, not all will feel comfortable over a large temperature range if the RH is carefully controlled. Specifically more humidity is comfortable at a lower air temperature, and higher temperatures are more comfortable at lower RH.

That’s why people say it’s not the heat, it’s the humidity, or “ It’s a dry heat “. And the psychometric chart goes a long way in explaining how this relationship between temperature a,d humidity works. Mostly because people sweat, and their bodily temperature signals work over a fairly wide comfort range.

Most designers of air conditioning systems will use 50% RH as the sweet spot with the temperature at 75 Degrees F., since nearly everyone will be comfortable at that temperature, and that temperature is mostly energy efficient.

Now if we want to lower the temperature, and save on electrical power, we need to jack up the humidity in the conditioned space. But to do that we have to use energy to vaporize a certain amount of water to produce the wanted humidity, and of course, that uses energy as well. The same it true if we want to raise the temperature, then we need to remove more moisture to lower the humidity.

Large buildings, and very large retail spaces, like malls, used to have a resident refrigeration engineer who would oversees the control of these parameters. Now days there are energy management companies located many states away, and they control the environment in your local office, or shopping center remotely, by sensor measurements transmitted over the internet.

These companies use sophisticated computer algorithms to continuously optimize the interior environment not only for human comfort, but for maximum energy efficiency. Energy requirements will vary significantly, on the time of day, as well as the number of people in the controlled environment, as will weather.
