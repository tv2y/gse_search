Abstract
--------

In recent decades, global warming has significantly altered both the spatial and temporal distribution of rainfall patterns. This change has heightened the risk of rainfall-induced landslides, which are the most prevalent natural disasters in the mountainous regions of southwestern China. These events pose unpredictable and severe threats to the region, making it essential to forecast future rainfall trends and assess how landslide susceptibility will respond to these changes. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for developing effective strategies to mitigate and adapt to the changing rainfall patterns that influence landslides. This study focuses on Sichuan Province, China, and uses annual cumulative rainfall (ACR) as a key dynamic variable to create landslide susceptibility maps (LSMs). The goal is to explore the evolving relationship between rainfall and landslide susceptibility and use future rainfall projections to predict these risks. To achieve this, a historical landslide geospatial database was compiled across five temporal categories: 2000, 2001–2005, 2006–2010, 2011–2015, and 2016–2020. The extreme learning machine (ELM) was applied to generate LSMs for the years 2000 to2020, while an elasticity framework was used to assess how sensitive landslide susceptibility is to rainfall variations. To project future scenarios, a long short-term memory (LSTM) model was employed to project the ACR for 2030, using monthly rainfall data from 2000 to 2020. This projected ACR was then used to estimate future landslide susceptibility. Results showed a marked increase in high landslide susceptibility areas: 5.6% by 2005, 0.3% by 2010, 0.2% by 2015, and 12.9% by 2020, all relative to the year 2000. The elasticity analysis revealed that from 2000 to 2020, a 1% change in rainfall would cause an average 1.35% change in landslide susceptibility. Looking forward to 2030, the projected rise in ACR is expected to lead to a 2.44% increase in areas of high landslide susceptibility. Multiple validation techniques were applied to ensure reliability and robustness of these findings.

1. Introduction
---------------

Rainfall-induced landslides are among the most destructive natural hazards, particularly in regions with complex topographies and variable climates. Sichuan Province, China, is one such region, where, the combination of rugged terrain, heavy rainfall, and anthropogenic factors leads to frequent landslides. The variability in both rainfall patterns and geological conditions makes this region particularly susceptible to landslides, posing serious threats to lives, infrastructure, and the environment. Landslides result from complex interactions of both static and dynamic factors (Agboola et al., 2024; Tyagi et al., 2023). Static factors, such as the geomorphology, geological structures, and topography, play a crucial role in triggering landslides (Fang et al., 2024). Dynamic factors, especially rainfall, are external influences, with precipitation being the leading cause of landslides worldwide (Mondini et al., 2023). As climate and environmental conditions continue to shift, the risk of rainfall-induced landslides is expected to rise, heightening concerns for communities in vulnerable regions. (Mondini et al., 2023). This growing risk has fueled interest in predicting landslide susceptibility and understanding how rainfall contributes to landslide events. (Guzzetti et al., 2020; Piciullo et al., 2018). While much research has been done in this area, there is still a lack of comprehensive understanding of how these susceptibilities evolve over time in response to changing rainfall patterns. Existing predictive models generally fail to adequately capture the dynamic characteristic of landslide susceptibility, particularly the spatiotemporal variations driven by fluctuating rainfall (Oguz et al., 2024). Traditional approaches to landslide prediction tend to focus on static susceptibility maps that overlook temporal variability and fail to consider future climate scenarios (Tyagi et al., 2023). This limitation can lead to an underestimation of landslide risk, resulting in inadequate preparedness and mitigation efforts.

Existing studies on rainfall-induced landslide susceptibility have typically employed statistical or deterministic models based on historical data to predict future events. Many have used statistical or machine learning techniques to assess the factors that contribute to landslides triggered by rainfall. However, these studies generally overlooked the direct impact of rainfall and focused on geoenvironmental parameters including slope, land cover, and rock type (Reichenbach et al., 2018; Steger et al., 2023). Moreover, only a limited number of studies have quantitatively analyzed how landslide susceptibility responds to variations in rainfall within the context of climate variation (Bernardie et al., 2021; Hürlimann et al., 2022; Pham et al., 2021). Unlike static topographic conditions, rainfall is a dynamic factor that fluctuates over time, making landslide susceptibility a variable that also evolves (Kincey et al., 2024). This has been acknowledged in some studies, with Caine (1980) being one of the first to demonstrate the relationship between rainfall duration and intensity for landslide prediction. More recently, researchers have started to assess how multiple dynamic factors, including rainfall, influence landslide susceptibility (Hua et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2020b). For instance, Hua et al. (2020) analyzed the impact of rainfall, reservoir levels, and land use changes on susceptibility across different years. Similarly, Huang et al. (2023) explored annual variation in susceptibility by considering factors like the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) and annual cumulative rainfall (ACR). Although these studies recognize rainfall as a key dynamic factor in landslide susceptibility mapping (LSM) (Chowdhuri et al., 2022; Dikshit et al., 2021), there remains a lack of quantitative research on the direct relationship between specific rainfall variations and landslide susceptibility. Most existing also fail to account for projected rainfall changes in future scenarios. As a result, the literature lacks comprehensive approaches that integrate both current and future rainfall variations into landslide susceptibility predictions. To address these gaps, this study introduces an innovative approach that combines an elasticity model, typically used in economics, with deep learning algorithms. This methodology allows for the quantitative analysis of the dynamic response of landslide susceptibility to ACR, while also predicting future susceptibility scenarios in Sichuan Province, China. By integrating historical rainfall data, terrain features, and climate projections, this study aims to overcome the limitations of previous approaches and offer more accurate predictions of landslide susceptibility.

Numerous researchers globally have studied landslide susceptibility or risk, with many utilizing machine learning as an analytical neural network (Hussain et al., 2023; Saha et al., 2022). While effective, traditional neural network models often require extensive parameter tuning and iterative optimization, which can lead to inefficiencies in learning, problems with local optimality, and overfitting (Lye et al., 2021). The extreme learning machine (ELM) offers a more efficient alternative. It requires only the number of hidden layer nodes to be determined, without the need to adjust input weights or hidden layer biases during training (Huang et al., 2006). This streamlined approach differentiates it from conventional neural networks. The ELM has been successfully applied in various fields, including landslide displacement prediction (Liao et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2021) and susceptibility assessment (Huang et al., 2017). Given the necessity to analyze extensive long-term datasets, the ELM's advantages in training speed and generalization make it a potential tool for investigating spatiotemporal variations in landslide susceptibility.

Rainfall prediction has become a global research focus to address the impact of future climate change on landslides. Long-term rainfall forecast provide crucial insights for early disaster warning systems and guide regional water resource management (Hao et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2020). Previous studies have used both statistical and dynamic techniques to predict monthly rainfall outcomes (de Andrade et al., 2019; Hao et al., 2018), with general circulation models (GCMs) playing a central role in improving forecast accuracy (de Andrade et al., 2019). In climate prediction, shared socioeconomic pathways (SSP) have been widely used to explore climate impacts and strategies for mitigation and adaptation (O'Neill et al., 2017). However, a persistent challenge is the ability of climate models to produce accurate precipitation forecasts (Alizadeh, 2022; Liang et al., 2019). These models often operate at coarse resolutions, which limits their ability to simulate rainfall patterns effectively at regional or local scales (Kumar, 2023). To address this limitation, this study uses statistical methods based on historical data to establish empirical relationships between hydrometeorological variables and precipitation. Research have consistently shown that statistical models tend to outperform dynamic models in forecasting precipitation under different scenarios (Anochi et al., 2021; Qian et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2020). Moreover, deep learning methods have made significant progress in climate prediction due to their ability to analyze spatially and temporally interconnected geophysical data (Kim et al., 2021; Rahmani et al., 2021; Tang and Duan, 2021; Zhou and Zhang, 2022). Deep learning methods, particularly the long short-term memory (LSTM) model, excel at capturing and understanding long-term sequential patterns, including seasonal variations and the effects of prolonged climatic phenomena (Chen et al., 2017; Shevchenko and Shkliarenko, 2024). LSTM's capacity for memory retention makes it an ideal tool for analyzing climate data with time-series characteristics (Xu et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2020). In this study, an LSTM-based deep learning approach is applied to analyze climate data and improve the accuracy of rainfall predictions.

The key innovation of this study lies in combining an elasticity model with deep learning methods to quantitatively analyze the relationship between rainfall patterns and landslide susceptibility over time and space. The study specifically examines how changing rainfall patterns affect the landslide susceptibility in Sichuan Province, China. In addition to detailing this relationship, the study also considers how predicted rainfall could influence future landslide susceptibility. These insights are crucial for understanding how rainfall patterns impact landslides and for improving the management of landslide risks in the region. Herein, we set the following aims: (1) to evaluate the spatiotemporal variations in landslide susceptibility and ACR in Sichuan Province from 2000 to 2020, (2) to assess how landslide susceptibility responds to ACR using the elasticity model, (3) to forecast future rainfall scenarios using the LSTM model, and (4) to predict landslide susceptibility for 2030 based on projected ACR. These findings will enhance our understanding of the potential impacts of climate change on natural hazards and provide a scientific foundation for climate adaptation and disaster risk management. This study also contributes to the field of ecological informatics by offering a methodological framework that can be applied to other regions facing similar challenges. The integration of climate projections and machine learning in landslide susceptibility modeling represents a significant advancement, with broader implications for disaster risk reduction in a changing climate.

2. Study area and data used
---------------------------

### 2.1. General descriptions of the study area

Sichuan Province, located in southwestern China, spans latitudes 26°03′ N to 34°19′ N and longitudes 97°21′ E to 108°33′ E. It covers an area of approximately 48,600 km 2 (Fig. 1). The region's diverse topography leads to significant vertical climatic variations, resulting in uneven rainfall distribution and frequent meteorological disasters. The eastern region of Sichuan Province features a low-elevation basin characterized by a humid mid-subtropical climate. It receives an annual average precipitation ranging from 1000 to 1200 mm. Moving westward, the region transitions to the southern flank of the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau. It is distinguished by a colder climate owing to its high terrain. In this area, the average annual temperature ranges from 4 to 12°C, with annual precipitation levels between 500 and 900 mm. The mountains of southwestern Sichuan are characterized by a subtropical semi-humid climate, which is indicated by a potential dry season lasting seven months. The annual precipitation in this region ranges from 900 to 1200 mm, with 90% of the rainfall occurring between May and October. Sichuan Province is located in the upper reaches of the Yangtze River Basin and includes part of the Yellow River in its northwestern region. The combination of frequent earthquakes and heavy rainfall has made the region particularly prone to landslides. Notably, the “5.12” earthquake significantly lowered the rainfall threshold for landslides induced by intense rainfall in the province. This diverse climatic and geological context underscores the complexity of predicting landslide susceptibility in Sichuan, where both rainfall and seismic events contribute to the occurrence of these natural disasters.

### 2.2. Data used

#### 2.2.1. Data sources

This study utilized historical data on landslide events and causal factors to perform a spatiotemporal analysis of landslide susceptibility. Typically, various environmental factors and historical geological disaster records are used in analyses (Du et al., 2020). The data sources and details of the study are as follows:

##### 2.2.1.1. Road and water distance index factors

Extracted from the open-source road and river networks on OpenStreetMap (OSM). These datasets provide detailed information on the proximity of landslide events to transportation routes and water bodies. These are critical factors that influence landslide susceptibility. The data are available in a vector format and include attributes such as road type, river width, and flow direction.

##### 2.2.1.2. Geological data

Sourced from the National Geological Database to depict the stratigraphic lithology and fault index factor of geological structures. These data include information on the types of rocks and soils, fault lines, and other geological features that can affect slope stability. Geological data are available in both vector and raster formats with high spatial resolution.

##### 2.2.1.3. Topographic information

Obtained as a free digital elevation model (DEM) product (ground resolution=12.5 m) from Japan's Advanced Land Observing Satellite (ALOS). These data include slope, aspect, and other topographic factors essential for assessing landslide susceptibility. The DEM data are available in raster format and are processed to derive the slope steepness, slope aspect, and other terrain attributes.

##### 2.2.1.4. Historical monthly rainfall data

Sourced from the National Earth System Science Data Center. This dataset includes monthly precipitation records spanning several decades. It provides a comprehensive view of rainfall patterns that may contribute to landslide events. The data are available in both tabular and raster formats with spatial and temporal resolutions suitable for detailed analysis.

##### 2.2.1.5. Earthquake records

National Earthquake Data Center. These records include information on the location, magnitude, depth, and timing of earthquakes. This is critical for understanding the seismic triggers of landslides. The earthquake data are available in a tabular format and are integrated with spatial coordinates for mapping and analysis purposes.

#### 2.2.2. The landslide inventory

Landslide inventories form the basis of landslide susceptibility studies by providing essential data on the locations, sizes, and occurrence patterns. For this study, a comprehensive inventory of landslides in Sichuan Province from 2000 to 2020 was created, which included 17,395 landslide points. These points were categorized into five distinct periods: 2000, 2001–2005, 2006–2010, 2011–2015, and 2016–2020 (Fig. 1). The underlying hypothesis was that landslides occurring in the past under specific conditions would likely reoccur under similar conditions in the future. Due to limitations in technology and data availability, landslide events recorded before 2000 with temporal information were also included in the modeling. The inventory maps were generated using data on 731, 819, 3286, 6862, and 5697 landslide events corresponding to each time period. A five-year interval was used to define these categories, following the approach outlined by Tyagi et al. (2023). Historical landslide records were compiled through field surveys and the interpretation of satellite images. The temporal and spatial attributes of these events were incorporated into the official database of the Sichuan Geoenvironmental Information System (http://202.61.89.33:16003/DMGeoMIS/). This official database was created by government departments at the county level in Sichuan Province and managed uniformly by the Department of Natural Resources of Sichuan Province. The reliability of the landslide distribution data was confirmed by Chen et al. (2023). Landslides in Sichuan Province were classified into four size categories: small (91.07%), medium (8.44%), large (0.28%), and giant (0.21%).

Figure 1 presents a landslide inventory map of Sichuan Province, illustrating the region's diverse topography. This geographical diversity results in different landslide dynamics across regions. While highly mountainous areas are vulnerable to landslides owing to the steep slopes and geological activity, lower elevation areas including the Sichuan Basin also experience significant landslide activity, particularly rainfall-induced landslides. This is owing to the significant rainfall in the region, soft soil layers, and human activities such as agriculture and infrastructure development (Chen et al., 2023). The observation that landslides are more prevalent in low-mountain plain areas than in high-mountain canyon areas may be counterintuitive. However, it is essential to consider that low mountain areas with softer rock formations and higher population densities are more susceptible to landslides, particularly under intense rainfall and anthropogenic alterations. In contrast, although highly mountainous areas are vulnerable to landslides, the sparse population and rugged terrain may result in fewer recorded incidents. These characteristics are consistent with the observations of a spatial distribution study of landslides in Sichuan Province by Chen et al. (2023).

#### 2.2.3. Appraisal factors of landslide susceptibility

The selection of landslide condition factors was guided by data availability, the characteristics of the study area, the analysis scale, and a review of the existing literature (Tyagi et al., 2023). The stability of mountain slopes is continuously influenced by Earth's ecosystem processes (Grêt-Regamey and Weibel, 2020). Based on the geological context and previous research in the region (Li et al., 2017), 14 factors were identified for LSM. Thirteen of these are static features, including altitude, slope, aspect, curvature, plan curvature, profile curvature, relief amplitude, surface roughness, lithology, distance to roads, distance to rivers, distance to faults, and seismic density. The remaining factor, ACR, is dynamic. Monthly rainfall data for Sichuan Province were used to calculate ACR, with variations analyzed over five-year intervals. For spatial susceptibility mapping, the dynamic factor was substituted with the five-year mean total rainfall for each time interval (Huang et al., 2023). Lithology comprised categorical information, whereas the remaining 13 elements comprised numerical data. The lithology was categorized as hard rock, even harder rock, integrated hard-soft rock, soft rock, very fragile rock, and Quaternary sediments. This study also compiled earthquakes with Richter magnitudes higher than four to create an earthquake density map. Thematic maps for the static factors are shown in Fig. 2, while Fig. 3 displays the ACR distribution map for the study area from 2000 to 2020. All landslide conditioning factors were converted into a raster format with a grid size of 300×300 m for analysis and modeling.

3. Methods
----------

Figure 4 outlines the methodology used in this study. The process began with the preprocessing of both static and dynamic factors. ACR data for each year were divided into four equal intervals to analyze the rainfall trends. Subsequently, the ELM model was applied to generate LSMs for Sichuan Province from 2000 to 2020, considering the dynamic annual rainfall variations to estimate the spatial probabilities. This section also provides a brief overview of the ELM and its relevance to the current research. In the third stage, future monthly cumulative rainfall (MCR) scenarios for 2030 were projected using sequential data from 2000 to 2020, processed through the LSTM model. To manage the extensive data and ensure data consistency, historical rainfall data for specific landslide-prone locations in each county were extracted, and these values were interpolated to estimate rainfall across Sichuan Province by 2030. The projected ACR, combined with static factors, was then used to train the ELM model to predict the landslide susceptibility for 2030. To evaluate the performance of the prediction methods, metrics such as area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and accuracy parameter were used to validate the predicted maps and sequential data. Finally, an elastic model was applied to analyze the dynamic variations in landslide susceptibility and its dynamic response relationship with rainfall in Sichuan Province. The entire analysis was conducted using ArcGIS 10.8 software platform, while the algorithm was executed in MATLAB R2022b.

### 3.1. Construction of a spatial database

Before constructing the LSM model, it is essential to create landslides and non-landslides datasets. The landslide dataset comprised 17,395 recorded landslides, with a balanced ratio of 1:1 between landslide and non-landslide data. This balance is crucial for ensuring that the model treats both classes equally during training, thereby enhancing prediction accuracy and preventing skewed results. Areas with terrain slopes less than 5° were classified as “non-landslide zones” (Sun et al., 2021), from which an equal number of non-landslide points were randomly selected. This random sampling helped represent the true variability of the terrain and environmental conditions within the study area. Following the procedures described by Wang et al. (2021), a circular buffer zone of 150 m was established around the landslide and non-landslide points. The sample range was determined using the smallest external rectangle of the buffer zone. To improve model precision and convergence speed, data for each layer of conditioning factors were standardized to a 0–1 scale, with landslide points labeled as one and non-landslide points as zero. Many studies commonly divide landslide and non-landslide data into training and testing subsets in a 70 / 30 ratio (Arora et al., 2021; Dou et al., 2019; Guo et al., 2021). The 70% training data ensures that the model has sufficient examples to learn from, while the 30% test data allows for a robust evaluation of the model's predictive capabilities. In line with this method, 70% of the pixels were utilized as training datasets, and the remaining 30% were allocated for testing. ROC curves were used to validate the reliability of this method.

### 3.2. Deep learning methods

#### 3.2.1. Elm

ELM is a type of single-layer feedforward neural network (SLFN) developed by Huang et al. in 2006. The ELM structure was developed to feature a random initialization of the hidden layer neurons and weights. This ensures its autonomy from the training dataset. ELM calculates the output weights using a pseudo-inverse technique. With this framework, superior generalization performance and learning speed can be achieved. Using this approach, the necessity of adjusting various parameters in conventional algorithms is eliminated, and concerns regarding gradient descent techniques converging to local minima are mitigated. This is achieved through successive iterations to enhance the overall effectiveness. Here, ω L⋅x K denotes the inner product of ω L and x K. To minimize the cost function min β Hβ−T，the output weights were determined by obtaining the least-squares solution for the linear system Hβ=T:(3)β̂=H+T where **_H_****_+_** is the Moore–Penrose generalized inverse of the matrix **_H_**. A significant challenge in the development of an effective ELM is determining the preferable number of hidden nodes. A recommended method to address this challenge is a trial-and-error approach (Huang et al., 2017). The optimal selection of hidden neurons was determined by achieving the minimum root mean square error (RMSE) in the ELM.

In this study, the ELM was used to generate LSMs in three steps.

Step 1: The ELM model was trained to determine the optimal number of hidden neurons using normalized landslide condition factors and landslide and non-landslide datasets.

Step 2: The hidden layer output matrix was computed in conjunction with the user-selected activation function.

Step 3: The output weights were calculated using Eq. (3) to determine the output layer, i.e., the landslide susceptibility index (LSI).

#### 3.2.2. LSTM

The LSTM is a specialized form of recurrent neural network (RNN) specifically designed to identify and learn long-term dependencies within sequential data. Comprising input gates, forget gates, output gates, and memory cells, an LSTM model collaborates to control the data flow and enhance the capture of temporal patterns in the dataset (Shevchenko and Shkliarenko, 2024). (2) Calculation of the input gate: The degree to which a new input is integrated into the memory cell is determined by the input gate. The calculations were performed using a specific equation:(6)i t=σ W i∘s t−1 x t+b i

(3) The forget gate, denoted by f t, plays a crucial role in determining which information from the previous memory cell state c t−1 should be retained or discarded. The calculation is based on a specific equation.(7)f t=σ W f∘s t−1 x t+b f

(4) Memory cell update: The state of the memory cell c t is modified by the input gate i t, forget gate f t, and prior state of the memory cell c t−1. The memory cell state is adjusted according to the following formula:(8)c˜t=tanh W c∘s t−1 x t+b c(9)c t=f t∘c t−1+i t∘c˜t

(5) Calculation of the output gate: The output gate o t regulates the amount of memory cell state c t that influences the present hidden state s t. The output gate is computed using the following formula:(10)o t=σ W o∘s t−1 x t+b o

(6) Update of hidden state: s t, the hidden state, is modified according to the output gate o t and state of the memory cell c t. The hidden state is adjusted using the following formula:(11)s t=o t∘tanh c t

The ADAM optimizer is a learning rate optimization algorithm that adjusts adaptively. It was introduced by Kingma and Ba, 2015. Known for its effectiveness in handling noisy or sparse gradients, it is considered a favorable option for our model (Kingma and Ba, 2015). Hence, in the present study, the parameters were optimized using the ADAM algorithm (Wang et al., 2023). In this study, the LSTM model was structured around a dynamic factor, namely, rainfall. Initially, input data were extracted from the preprocessed rainfall map. Then, for the training phase of the LSTM model, the input data were categorized into training and testing sets. The number of neurons in the model structure was set to 100. Finally, the rainfall under the future scenario was output in the MATLAB R2022b environment.

### 3.3. Accuracy assessment methods

Validating a study is essential for establishing its scientific credibility and determining the proximity of the calculated results to true values. This study evaluated the accuracy of the ELM model in the LSM by analyzing the area under the ROC curve. The ROC curve visually depicts the performance of the binary classifier technique, with the area under the curve (AUC) indicating the precision of the LSM model. Moreover, the performance of the LSTM model was assessed using key metrics such as the RMSE, mean absolute error (MAE), and coefficient of determination (R 2). These metrics quantify the prediction accuracy and alignment of the predicted and actual values. Lower RMSE and MAE values signify an improved prediction accuracy, whereas a higher R 2 value indicates a stronger correlation between the predicted and observed outcomes (Wang et al., 2023). These parameters are defined as follows.

### 3.4. ACR analysis using dynamic degree indicator

The dynamic extent in this study is an indicator that reflects the speed and intensity of transitions between different precipitation categories in any given period. It is an important framework for assessing variations in regional precipitation patterns. We assessed the variations in rainfall over different periods using dynamic degree indicators that included both individual rainfall dynamics degree (IRDD) and overall rainfall dynamics degree (ORDD). The IRDD and ORDD were then calculated. ### 3.5. Sensitivity of landslide susceptibility response to ACR by using elasticity model

In economics, elasticity is a measure of the responsiveness of an economic variable to variations in another. Because our aim was to examine the responses of the landslide susceptibility index to ACR variations, we developed an elasticity indicator. Elasticity can be employed to reflect how the LSI responds to a 1% variation in ACR. A higher elasticity indicates that landslide susceptibility is more responsive to the ACR. This concept is represented by the following mathematical equations (Song and Deng, 2017):(17)ELSR=LSI end−LSI start/LSI start ORDD×100%where _ELSR_ is the elasticity of landslide susceptibility in response to rainfall, _LSI_ end is the LSI at the end of the study period, _LSI_ start is the LSI at the start of the study period.

5. Discussion
-------------

Significant fluctuations in climatic conditions are likely to increase runoff during the rainy season (Akmatov et al., 2024). Rainfall causes erosion of the surface of the slope. This results in the deterioration of the rock and soil mass and thereby, reduced shear strength and increased landslide risk (Li et al., 2020a). Hence, it is crucial to evaluate the dynamic response relationship between landslide susceptibility to rainfall and predictions. This study has provided insights into the driving mechanisms of landslide hazards and established rainfall thresholds for prediction purposes. It analyzed the variations in the LSM under dynamic ACR from 2000 to 2020 using the ELM model. In addition, LSM predictions for 2030 were generated using both ELM and LSTM models, with projected ACR maps serving as input data. However, certain aspects require further investigation.

The increase in the percentage of areas classified as having high and very high susceptibility from 2000 to 2020 aligns with studies that reported a correlation between intensified rainfall and increased landslide activity (Gnyawali et al., 2023). The increase in landslide susceptibility, particularly in areas with a higher ACR, emphasizes the sensitivity of the regional landscape to varying rainfall patterns. The observed increase in the average LSI over the study period corroborates the observations of other studies emphasizing the growing landslide risk in response to varying climatic conditions (Tang et al., 2024).

A sensitivity analysis of landslide susceptibility to variations in the ACR revealed that the elasticity varied significantly over different periods. The highest elasticity was observed during 2000–2005. This indicates that the response of the landscape to rainfall variations was most significant during this period. This could be attributed to the occurrence of extreme rainfall events or significant shifts in land use. The decrease in elasticity during 2005–2015 indicates that although rainfall continued to increase, the rate of increase in landslide susceptibility reduced. This was potentially because of the saturation of vulnerable areas or the implementation of mitigation measures. However, the subsequent increase in elasticity during 2015–2020 indicates the presence of a threshold beyond which additional rainfall causes a disproportionate increase in landslide risk. This observation is consistent with the concept of the threshold behavior in ecological systems. Therein, small variations in environmental conditions can cause abrupt shifts in system dynamics (Qin et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024).

Future projections for the ACR and LSMs indicate a continuation of the trends observed over the past two decades, with a likely increase in type III and IV rainfall and corresponding increase in landslide susceptibility. The predicted increase in high-and very high-susceptibility zones by 2030 emphasizes the need for proactive land-use planning and disaster risk reduction strategies in Sichuan Province. The reduction in low-to very-low-susceptibility areas further emphasizes the growing risk and shrinking buffer zones that could otherwise absorb the impacts of rainfall without inducing landslides. This aligns with the observations of other studies that projected an increase in landslide activity in response to future climate scenarios (Tang et al., 2024).

### 5.2. The relationship between ACR and landslide

The ACR aggregates the total rainfall over a year. It captures both frequency and magnitude of precipitation events. This metric is particularly effective for understanding long-term trends and patterns that contribute to landslide susceptibility. Unlike single rainfall events, the ACR provides a broader perspective on how continuous and accumulated rainfall impacts soil moisture levels and slope stability over time. Huang et al. (2023) demonstrated that the ACR is a significant predictor of landslide occurrence in regions with varying climatic conditions. The analysis revealed a significant shift in the ACR types, with types II (500–1000 mm) and III (1000–1500 mm) covering most of the region. However, over the past two decades, there has been a reduction in type II ACR and an increase in types III and IV (1500–2000 mm) ACR. This shift indicates a trend toward more intense rainfall, a pattern that has been observed in other regions globally and is generally linked to climate change (Alahacoon Zhang et al., 2021). The increase in areas experiencing higher rainfall is consistent with the observations of Ni et al. (2023). That study documented similar trends in other parts of China. This intensification of rainfall can exacerbate the risk of landslides, particularly in regions with steep topography and loose soil (such as Sichuan Province).

Fig. 17 illustrates the relationship between the number of landslide events and average ACR for 2000–2020. Most landslide events occurred in 2013 (2530), followed by 2018, 2008, and 2017. It is noteworthy that in July 2013 and May 2008, Sichuan Province experienced continuous and intense rain and a severe earthquake with a magnitude of 8.0, respectively. Both the extreme events resulted in a large number of landslides. Excluding the two catastrophic years, we observe that both number of landslides and ACR have been increasing. This indicates a linear relationship between these. Empirical studies and case analyses provide insights into the relationship between the ACR and landslides. For example, recent research in the Sichuan Province has shown a strong correlation between high ACR values and increased landslide incidence. By analyzing historical landslide data and the corresponding ACR values, it is evident that the years with unusually high cumulative rainfall coincide with a higher frequency of landslides (Huang et al., 2023).

### 5.4. Policy implications recommendations, limitations, and prospects

The occurrence of landslides is influenced by various factors, with rainfall typically being the primary consideration in LSM owing to data availability. In future research endeavors, we aim to enhance our methodologies by including the analysis of other influential factors such as human engineering activities, to better understand their dynamic relationships with landslide occurrence. This would enable a more comprehensive assessment of landslide susceptibility and improve our capability to predict and mitigate potential landslide hazards. In this study, a time series of 20 years of rainfall data was utilized to forecast rainfall patterns for 2030. The use of this extensive dataset helps mitigate potential biases in the predictive model. However, estimating the ACR for 2030 based on historical data presents significant challenges owing to temporal and spatial variability in rainfall. This discrepancy is a significant limitation of the research observations. The identification and visualization of forecasts play crucial roles in global surveillance initiatives, natural resource preservation, and strategic planning. We incorporated climate change scenarios from global climate models (GCMs) to enhance the robustness of our projections. These scenarios provide insights into how climatic conditions are likely to evolve under different greenhouse gas emission trajectories. By integrating these scenarios, our model can account for potential variations in rainfall patterns caused by climate change.

Although this study has provided valuable insights into the spatiotemporal dynamics of landslide susceptibility, it is essential to acknowledge the uncertainties inherent in this analysis (Huettmann and Arhonditsis, 2023). The ELM employed in generating landslide susceptibility maps has demonstrated high accuracy, with over 80% of landslide predictions being accurate each year. However, the reliance of the model on historical rainfall data and its extrapolation to future scenarios introduce potential sources of error. For example, the assumption that past rainfall patterns would continue into the future may not fully account for the potential variability and extremes caused by climate change. Additionally, although the Jenks natural break classification method used for reclassifying susceptibility zones minimizes intrazone variability, it does not entirely eliminate the risk of misclassification. This is particularly so for regions with complex terrain and microclimatic variations. The study also assumed a linear relationship between the ACR and landslide susceptibility. Although supported by elasticity analysis, this may not capture the nonlinear and threshold effects of rainfall on landslide occurrence. Another critical area of uncertainty is the spatial resolution of input data. Although this study utilized high-resolution data for both rainfall and landslide occurrence, the potential for small-scale local variations in landslide susceptibility owing to factors such as land-use variations, deforestation, and human activities may not be captured fully. Future studies should consider integrating finer-scale data and evaluating nonlinear modeling approaches to address these limitations.

The observations of this study contribute to the broader field of ecological informatics by providing a detailed case study on the dynamic relationship between climatic factors and geomorphological hazards. The study's methodological approach, which combines ELM with elasticity analysis and natural break classification, provides a robust framework for similar analyses in other regions. This also emphasizes the importance of considering both temporal and spatial dimensions while assessing the impacts of climate variability on landslide susceptibility. Future research should focus on integrating additional environmental variables such as soil moisture, vegetation cover, and land-use variations, to enhance the predictive accuracy of landslide susceptibility models. Moreover, adaptive strategies that incorporate real-time monitoring and early warning systems should be developed to mitigate the risks associated with landslides in highly susceptible areas. Collaboration between government agencies, local communities, and researchers is crucial for implementing effective landslide risk management practices in Sichuan Province and beyond. To conclude, this has study demonstrated the significant influence of rainfall on the spatiotemporal dynamics of landslide susceptibility in Sichuan Province. The results emphasize the increasing risk of landslides in the region and recommend urgent action to mitigate their potential impacts. By situating these observations within the broader context of the existing research and acknowledging the uncertainties involved, this study has provided a strong foundation for future work aimed at enhancing our understanding of landslide hazards and improving resilience to such natural disasters.

6. Conclusions
--------------

Initially, this study used the ELM model to conduct a long-term LSM in Sichuan Province, China, from 2000 to 2020. Second, the LSTM model was applied to predict the monthly rainfall. The future landslide susceptibility was then projected using the future ACR projection as the driving parameter. Finally, we evaluated the spatial and temporal variations in landslide susceptibility in response to rainfall using an elasticity model. The key observations of this study are as follows.
*   1.The spatial pattern of rainfall in Sichuan Province varied over 2000–2020. During the past two decades, the type IV rainfall areas have increased significantly (IRDD=47.19%). A larger increase occurred in type IV ACR (IRDD=77.26%) during 2000–1010 and in the area of type III ACR (IRDD=3.56%) during 2010–2020. Overall, the rainfall has shown an increasing trend from 2000 to 2020 (ORDD=0.35%). 
*   2.The overall LSI showed an evident decreasing trend from mountainous areas to basins, with “moderate” and “high” landslide susceptibility dominating the entire study area. During 2000–2020, the LSI increased in 37.46% of the research region, mostly in the cities of Guang'an, Bazhong, and Ya'an. An increase in extreme rainfall was the major cause of the increase in LSI. 
*   3.The ACR projections for 2030 show an increase of over 4% in the type III rainfall areas and of approximately 5% in the type IV rainfall areas. Meanwhile, the area of type II rainfall is projected to decrease by approximately 10%. This indicates that climate change exacerbates rainfall variations. Hence, emergency regulations for extreme rainfall events in the Sichuan region are required. 
*   4.Future landslide susceptibility map projections can contribute to the management and sustainable allocation of ecological services. The projected maps for 2030 show that as rainfall levels increase, the categories labeled as “high” and “very high” susceptibility to landslides also increase. Compared with 2020, the levels of high and very high landslide susceptibility have increased by 0.52% and 2.53%, respectively. This indicates a potential increase in landslide occurrence in the region. 
*   5.We also developed an elasticity indicator to assess the responses of the LSI to ACR variations. The results showed an overall increase in elasticity from 2000 to 2020, ranging from 0.88 to 1.98. The elasticity of landslide susceptibility variation to the ACR variation from 2000 to 2020 was 1.35. This indicates that a 1% variation in rainfall would result in an average variation in landslide susceptibility of 1.35%. With regard to the elasticities for the major administrative regions, approximately 50% of the cities in Sichuan Province had elasticities >1. Overall, the response of landslide susceptibility to rainfall variations was significant for different years. However, the differences in sensitivity were small. 

The observations of this study would assist decision makers in gaining a clearer understanding of the relationships between human activities and natural processes in Sichuan Province, China. Using this information, stakeholders can develop effective policies to mitigate and prevent landslides. In addition, efforts should be directed toward reducing the effects of UHIs, enhancing living conditions, advancing public health initiatives, and cultivating community welfare.

2025, Measurement Journal of the International Measurement Confederation  Show abstract This study introduces a hybrid model, PINN-LSTM (Physics-Informed Neural Network-Long Short-Term Memory), developed to enhance wave speed forecasting at depths of 1.5 to 11.5 m over forecast horizons of 6, 12, 24, and 48 h.
