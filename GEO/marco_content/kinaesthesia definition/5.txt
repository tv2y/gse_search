### Kinesthesia

Kinesthesia is the ability to sense motion of a joint or limb. This sense is primarily influenced by muscle spindles and secondarily influenced by skin receptors and joint receptors.63-68 In clinical practice, kinesthesia is measured as the smallest change in joint angle required to elicit conscious awareness of joint motion.48,69 The common terminology used in proprioception training is “threshold to detection of passive movement.”49 It has been suggested that the joint be placed at a certain angle and then slowly moved passively at a speed of 0.5° to 2° per second until the patient signals that limb motion occurs.49 The patient should be blinded during initial kinesthesia testing, because limb movement is greatly influenced by visual cues. Also, it may be advisable to use a professional training device (such as, Biometrics Upper Limb Exerciser∗) in order to precisely control the speed and degree of joint motion.49

### Kinesthesia

Writing is a motor skill and, as with other motor skills, efficient writing depends on kinesthetic input. Motor skills developed kinesthetically, such as riding a bike, keyboarding, or handwriting, are most permanent. In writing, an internal sensitivity that a letter movement feels correct reduces a child's need to visually monitor the fingers or pencil point while moving along the line. This security enhances speed in learning and confidence in cursive writing. Kinesthetic writing naturally accelerates over time to functional speed without the reduction of performance quality seen with visually guided writing. The visual system is far too slow and mechanical to monitor the serial chain of finger movements necessary for note taking much beyond mid third grade. Advising a child to slow down (allowing time to visually monitor the writing hand) temporarily results in more legible paperwork. However, this remedy fails in middle school and beyond, when greater speed is necessary in lecture settings. Therefore kinesthetic training is important whether or not a child has a visual motor or spatial problem. This is an area of training that should be explored and further developed by early educators.

Kinesthetic skill development is most beneficial for children experiencing visual motor deficits. Kinesthesia is an effective compensation for eye-hand coordination difficulties and can be a powerful builder of motor confidence. Kinesthetic training enables these children to bypass their problem area and become efficient writers by concentrating on kinesthetic feedback. If diminished kinesthesia is not enhanced, a child continues an over-reliance on visual monitoring, with a subsequent slowness in the production of writing. Kinesthetic activities are an essential aspect of both prewriting and writing programs.

Kinesthetic skills usually intrigue young children. Elementary kinesthetic activities can be done on desk tops, at the blackboard, or in the gymnasium. A sample for each location is demonstrated in Box 15-2.

As noted, kinesthetic writing should use limited visual motor control. Shape-copying tests such as the Test of Visual Motor Integration are useful in predicting the child's potential ease or difficulty in learning manuscript. Copy forms and manuscript letters require analysis and synthesis of the forms to duplicate them accurately. Skill in this area is less helpful in predicting the ease a student will experience in learning cursive writing.

A Production Consistency Sheet (Benbow et al., 1992) can be used to informally observe a child's kinesthetic aptitude in repeating and spacing cursive letters in words using the kinesthetic sense. Model shapes are displayed in the upper left-hand comer of a half sheet of unlined paper (5½ × 8½ inches). Each model is ½ inch high. The models include a square, a circle, a triangle, and a cursive capital A.

Instruct the students to duplicate the printed model using a fluid moving stroke(s) rather than a rigidly controlled stroke(s). The four shapes should be drawn in three evenly spaced rows of five figures. On completion of the fifteenth figure, the child is told to close the eyes or avert the gaze and complete a fourth row that looks like and is spaced like the rows above. The quality of the first three rows reveals the child's visual motor control of horizontal, vertical, diagonal, or circular lines. The consistency of the fourth row is a graphic demonstration of the child's kinesthetic learning potential for both configuring and spacing. The two examples selected in Figure 15-14 were drawn by 10-year-old boys who were classmates in a third-grade classroom. Consistency in shape, size, and spacing is a high indicator of potential for learning cursive writing. In comparing these two samples, one can predict that the child who drew Figure 15-14 A will learn to write with less difficulty than the child who drew Figure 15-14 B.

### Improve Proprioception and Kinesthesia

Knee proprioception and kinesthesia are negatively affected by injury.222 _Proprioception_ is defined as the conscious and unconscious recognition of position sense. _Kinesthesia_ is the sensation of joint motion or acceleration. Joint position neuromuscular control is where the efferent motor response to afferent (sensory) information occurs. Mechanoreceptors within the cruciate ligaments are destroyed during the ligament failure of MLKI and do not regrow into the grafts when the ACL and PCL are reconstructed. Receptors within the collateral ligaments and capsule are also always damaged to varying degrees.

Specific exercises that are safe for the healing structures early in the rehabilitation program may facilitate the remaining intact mechanoreceptors to compensate for those that are absent. The sooner the patient can improve joint proprioception, the better carryover she or he should have through the progression of the rehabilitation program. Facilitating proprioception during the initial stages of rehabilitation would be expected to minimize the subsequent risk of reinjury, normalize gait, and facilitate the progression to running, agility activities, and return to functional activities. Various exercises that can be used at this time include angular joint replication training, end-ROM reproduction training, and perturbation training.223 The perturbation training (submaximal) should be performed in a non-weight-bearing position during the initial phases by having the patient sit in a chair, place his or her foot on a single-dimensional tilt board, and then perturb the tilt board. Use of a tilt board is a low-intensity exercise with minimal motion that prevents unduly stressing the healing tissue. Initially, the patient begins with the eyes open so the patient can see the perturbation coming, which constitutes _proactive_ training. The majority of the training at this time is performed in a proactive mode. The patient progresses to partial weight bearing using a tilt board during this phase. In the next phase, progression increases to perturbations with the eyes closed so that the training becomes a _reactive_ response.

### Proprioception and Kinesthesia

Until recently, proprioceptive and kinesthetic abilities received more attention in rehabilitation of lower extremity injuries than upper extremity injuries. Proprioception is defined as the ability to perceive position, weight, and resistance of objects in relation to the body. Kinesthesia is defined as the ability to sense the extent, direction, or weight of body movement. In addition to visual, vestibular, and cutaneous input, receptors in the joint capsule, ligaments, and labrum provide proprioceptive and kinesthetic information.

Published studies on shoulder proprioceptive and kinesthetic testing have used specialized testing apparatus.35,36 Davies and Dickoff-Hoffman 37 advocate clinical angular joint replication testing with an electronic digital inclinometer. For normal males, they report average mean differences of plus or minus 2.4° to 3.0° for seven shoulder joint positions between known angles and subject replication of known angles.

Because proprioception and kinesthesia are compromised following anterior shoulder dislocation,35,38 exercises designed to improve proprioception and kinesthesia seem logical—at least in rehabilitation of macrotraumatic injuries that are likely to disrupt the capsular, ligamentous, or labral structures.

### Introduction

Bastian, in 1887, described ‘kinaesthesia’ as “the sense of movement” whereby “we are made acquainted with the position and movements of our limbs, we are enabled to discriminate between different degrees of ‘resistance’ and ‘weight,’ and by means of it the brain also derives much unconscious guidance in the performance of movements generally.” The term ‘proprioception’ is used synonymously with kinesthesia. It was first used by Sherrington in 1906 and can be thought of as referring to the sensations of the body. Although proprioception is a sense like vision, hearing, touch, taste, and smell, these more commonly recognized sensory systems give us information about the outside world, whereas proprioceptors are the sensory receptors which tell us what the body itself is doing. As Bastian suggested, kinesthesia encompasses a complex of sensations which include a sense of joint movement, a sense of joint position, a sense of muscle force or tension, and a sense of effort. Sensations derived from the vestibular apparatus are also sometimes included in proprioception, but these signals of the position and movement of the head relative to the external world will not be discussed here.

The sense of joint movement (movement sense) is the process by which we perceive movements of parts of the body relative to one another. It includes detection of movements and perception of their direction, velocity, distance, and timing. Both passive movements, which are imposed by an external force, and active movements, generated by the muscles, are perceived. The sense of joint position (position sense) is the process by which we perceive the current positions of parts of the body relative to one another. Again, relative positions can be perceived when the body parts are at rest and when they are held in position by muscle contraction. The sense of force or tension is the process by which we perceive forces generated through the muscles, whereas the sense of effort gives a perception of the strength of muscle contraction relative to the total strength of the muscle. Both of these signals are used in perception of applied forces, and in judging heaviness. All of these sensations are used separately and together to give knowledge of the body’s own actions and its interactions with the environment. Thus, proprioceptive sensations provide feedback of the consequences of motor output and are vital to the control of voluntary movement.

### Proprioception and Kinesthetic Testing

Kinesthesia is assessed by determining the threshold to detection of passive movement (TTDPM).14 TTDPM quantifies the ability to consciously detect shoulder motion using a custom-made device 21,31,44 or an isokinetic dynamometer.21 Visual and tactile cues are minimized as in joint angle replication testing.14,21,43–45 Testing often includes internal and external rotation motions both at mid-ROM and end-ROM.32,45 The arm is passively moved at a slow velocity from a preset reference angle. Speeds of 5 deg/sec have been shown to produce a more reliable test.14,45,46 Subjects are instructed to signal or stop the machine as soon as motion is noted (Figure 7-3). The amount of movement occurring before detection is recorded. Although testing at slow speeds has been shown to be more reliable, the results may not be clinically applicable to assess functional movements, because functional movements occur at faster angular velocities and in multiple planes.

### The Roles of Vision and Kinesthesis

Vision is essential to children learning to handwrite as they plan, execute, and monitor their attempts. Reliance on vision generally diminishes as skilled handwriting develops and feedback provided by the somatosensory system has greater influence in directing skilled and precise movement (Cornhill & Case-Smith, 1996). However, the visual sense is thought to remain active in children who are experiencing difficulties in mastering handwriting. Wann (1987) found that good and poor writers used different movement patterns when asked to reproduce letters and words. Wann recorded the performance of good and poor handwriters using an xy digitizer, and movement patterns were categorized according to their velocity and acceleration characteristics. Poor handwriters used more patterns of movement indicative of reliance on visual feedback as a major source of environmental information during handwriting. Although not suggesting that the more prooncient writers were not using visual feedback during letter production, Wann (1987) postulated that they were probably less dependent on it as a means of control. He went on to point out that deprivation of visual feedback resulted in the deterioration of even the most prooncient writer's performance. Other researchers (van der Muelin et al., 1991) have supported the view of Wann and suggest that children with Difficulty in visual-motor control compensate by adopting a greater reliance on visual monitoring and that this in turn results in slower performance. These issues warrant greater attention because they can influence the adoption of appropriate remedial strategies.

The role of kinesthesia is frequently discussed in relation to drawing and, particularly, handwriting. Kinesthesis relates to the information received from muscles, joints, and skin about body and limb position, and the direction, extent, and velocity of movement (Harris & Livesay, 1992; Sudsawad et al., 2002). An impairment of kinesthesis may influence the refinement of fine motor skills; children are not able to perceive and therefore monitor and correct errors of movement, particularly those of small amplitude, which are observed in handwriting (Harris & Livesay, 1992).

Much of the work around kinesthesis in relation to handwriting involves the Kinesthetic Sensitivity Test (KST). This norm-referenced test consists of two subtests: Kinesthetic Acuity and Kinesthetic Perception and Memory. Each subtest has specionc equipment that was designed to eliminate the need for motor control, thus allowing passive movement of children's hands and arms to determine kinesthetic ability. Laszlo and Bairstow (1985a) developed the test to identify kinesthetic deoncits and reported that training children using this test equipment resulted in improved drawing skills in children with poor kinesthesis. However, the relative importance of the role of kinesthesis in acquisition of prooncient handwriting remains unclear. This subject is elaborated on in the review of handwriting later in this chapter.

### Inverted Capture: Afterimages

Visual capture of kinesthesis is represented by these component orders: kinesthesis-vision, straight-curved, and perpendicular-arc or N:(k v), M:(s c), and P:(p a) which would be substituted for the general terms. This results in a braid representation of visual capture of kinesthesis at the terminal node—more correctly, visual “curved” capture of the kinesthetic “straight” neural response. What is needed is kinesthetic capture of the visual image, a reversal of the order within each pair above or N=(v k), M=(c s), and P=(a p). But the order in Figure 2 is fixed throughout by the constraints of the top frame or the first distal component which represents the dominance of the physical stimulus, and we cannot arbitrarily change it without getting rid of the constraint. We take a bold step and remove the stimulus (see Figure 5).

Figure 5. Topological representation of a capture reversal in a Gibson demonstration (_k_ kinesthesis, _v_ visual, _s_ straight, _c_ curved, _p_ perpendicular, _a_ arced). The physical stimulus is missing as in the after-effect experiments reported, and the events along the top frame are free to take on a different order.

Doing away with the stimulus accomplishes two things: First it frees the order fixed by the first distal event by virtue of the loss of the top frame. While liberating the sequence does not guarantee a reordering, it at least allows it to happen; freeing the order is a minimum requirement for this. Parsimony demands consistency of order within each pair, and the only alternative is N:(v k), M:(c s), and P:(a p).

Second, it erases the continuous support the stimulus offers, and removing the stimulus diminishes **dpd**=**pdp** to simply **pdp** (see Figure 5). This result is consistent with the topology of the model. It reduces the description to simple proximal cognition and its neurological substrate of which hallucination, alluded to above, is only one manifestation. There are others, and afterimages are among them. A lasting afterimage persists in the absence of stimulus support; we return, not to the distal beginning of **dpd**=**pdp**, but to the proximal beginning of **pdp**. This is most obvious when considering an afterimage created by a bright flash in total darkness where there is no stimulus, only the perceptual event; we continuously monitor the image with an autocognitoscope or its cortical trace with an autocerebroscope.

Swindle (1916) observed that if one experiences a very bright flash, one achieves a very powerful positive afterimage that may last over a period of hours. “Swindle’s Ghost,” as it is referred to occasionally (English & English, 1958), is a conscious image sustained purely by cortical activity; it is an image created by a stimulus that is not present during later observation. In spite of the absence of a distal stimulus, the image is very real. Observations by Gregory, Wallace, and Campbell (1959) and Davies (1973a,b) attests to just how real it is; if a Swindle’s Ghost image is a corridor, and one walks down it in total darkness, one seems to be walking, briefly, through his or her own afterimage.

Gregory et al. (1959) were the first to bring this motion effect to our attention. They found that if one obtains a strong positive afterimage of one’s outstretched hand against a patterned (wallpapered) surface, then, when the hand is shifted downward, it will appear to move downward leaving a black area in the shape of the hand where it occluded the pattern. This effect is so strange that Weintraub in his review of the perception literature asked, in what could be interpreted as a pique of desperation (Weintraub, 1975, p. 89), “Hypotheses anyone?”

This problem seems to be well suited for efference theories of perception. The connection between movement (efference) and conscious perception has a long history from Bell (1823) to Washburn (1916) to von Holst (1954) to Festinger, Ono, Burnham, and Bamber (1967). Most say that some sort of movement is necessary for conscious perception. Efference theory focuses specifically on the motor command leading to the movement. All of these are nicely summarized in a review by Coren (1986).

The **pdp** part of the theory described above appears suitable for afterimages, which, arguably, have something in common with hallucinations; both suffer from the absence of a physical stimulus for all to see, and both enjoy a state of conscious perception private to the individual. When your hand slips downward in the dark, and the afterimage hand shifts also, leaving a black hole behind, only you see your hand move; no one else in the room sees it, nor do they see the black hole. The movement of the afterimage pattern is at least correlated with, in fact it is isomorphic to, an objectively verifiable event; the black hole, however, is not, making the hole even more like an hallucination.

We report on a portion of two experiments here, both replications of Davies and Gregory et al. These are part of an extended series of studies on the afterimage effect, which were conducted by the first and second authors, a full report of which will appear elsewhere. First we wished to replicate the findings of Davies et al., and secondly, we wanted to substantiate our response recording procedure. This procedure, described below, consisted of a series of one-bit questions arranged in a hierarchy beginning with the most general of expectations, “Did you see your hand move?”

#### Frontal Plane Motion

Twenty undergraduate students were the observers in this experiment. They were brought into a photo dark room in pairs and seated at a table far enough apart that their outstretched hand and arm would not be blocked by the shadow of their bodies when the flash was triggered. Two red-light emitting table lamps provided the only illumination. Subjects remained in this dark adaptation environment for 10 minutes while the experimenter administered the informed consent documents and read the instructions.

The first trial required no movement of the arm; it served to show the observers the afterimage they would experience, and to demonstrate to the experimenters that they perceived it. The lights were turned off, immersing the observers in total darkness. Then a placard with a row of seven random letters was placed in front of the subjects. They were warned (“Ready?”), then exposed to a very bright flash of light (a professional photographer’s strobe unit—an AC studio strobe, Model SP 250—delivered a 250 watt-second flash). The strobe light was placed behind and slightly above the observers. A patterned sheet of dark blue and white horizontal stripes (ticking) was taped to the wall 1.5 to 2 meters in front of the observers, and the flash created a powerful afterimage of the placard, the patterned sheet beyond, and the surrounding room. The experimenter called out one of the five middle letters at random, and the observer on the left and the observer on the right were to identify, respectively, the letter to the left and right of the letter called out. Only one subject failed this test. This observer’s data were not included in the results thereby reducing the number of subjects to 19.

On subsequent trials, the observers were asked to extend their right hand in front of them, palm parallel to the frontal plane, ventral side outward. The lights were turned off, the ready signal given, and the strobe light was flashed. After achieving a strong afterimage himself, the experimenter told the observers to shift their arm downward 15–20 cm as demonstrated during the instructions. Following this, the red-light lamps were turned on, and a questionnaire was administered.

The questions on the response sheet during subsequent trials were one-bit yes/no questions: 1. Did you see your arm move? 2. If so, did your arm appear to move up or down? 3. Did you see a black filled outline of your hand? After the observers answered these questions, they were asked to write a paragraph describing their experience in as much detail as possible.

The answers to these questions were evaluated by binomial tests applied hierarchically. Some movement of the hand was observed by 14 participants; only 5/19 said they saw no movement at all (_p_=.04). None of these five saw a black hole in the shape of their hand. Of the 14 observers who saw movement, ten saw an unambiguous downward shift. One of the remaining four saw a black shadow move up (presumably along with the rest of the room), preserving the same relative motion as those who saw the hand move down. Two of the remaining observers saw no directional movement but reported the presence of a black shadow of the hand—evidence that movement within the afterimage actually occurred. One observer was not clear in her report of direction and saw no shadow. She was counted among those who observed no movement, even though the lack of movement was not unambiguously reported.

If the three participants who saw the shadow, implying an afterimage downshift of the hand, are included in the set of supporting cases, only one of the fourteen subjects could be said to have not supported Davies’ findings (1/14; _p_=.001). If we assume that the report of a shadow in the absence of a report of direction is enough to imply a downward shift, two observers would be disconfirming cases (2/14; _p_=.006). Finally, if we include only the subject demonstrating relative motion as a confirming instance of Davies’ findings, then there would be three unsupporting cases (3/14; _p_=.029). These results confirm the findings reported by Gregory and Davies, and further indicate that the phenomenon is easily replicated. More importantly, for our purposes, a kinesthetic capture of visual location seems to exist.

#### Sagittal Motion

Twenty undergraduate students participated in this experiment which was generally run the same way as the experiment above. In this experiment, however, the subjects held a 3X5 card glued to a popsicle stick, and at the right moment they were asked to move the card toward their nose. This is essentially a replication of a manipulation reported by Gregory (1974). Gregory simply had subjects move the hand in and out sagittally; he reported the demonstration with positive results but without frequency measurements of judgments as we are doing here.

There was no attempt to test for the creation of an afterimage since in the previous experiment 19/20 subjects showed that the flash procedure we used was effective. However, the first trial was a demonstration, without movement, of the kind of afterimage the subjects would be expected to see. Four test trials with movement followed The experimenter demonstrated the motion that was to be made before the red room lights were turned off (here as before the subjects were partially dark adapted). On each trial the instruction to move the card was given as before—when the experimenter achieved a strongly detailed afterimage of the visual field.

Each observer was asked to record whether the card appeared to move toward the nose, away from the nose, or not at all, and the number of observers reporting an _away_, _toward_, or _null_ response was ascertained for each trial. An _away_ or _toward_ response indicated a directional _change_, and a null response signified a _no_-_change_ trial.

As before, we relied on binomial tests to determine (1) if there was any movement seen by the observers when the arm was shifted sagittally (_change_ vs. _no_-_change_), and (2) if movement was seen, did the card appear to move toward or away from the observer. Unfortunately, there were no differences of any significance, and Gregory’s results were, for the moment, unsubstantiated. This was difficult for us to comprehend since both of us could detect movement. We suspected that there were other factors interfering with the subjects’ observations.

Our suspicions were prompted by two observations: First, there are patterns in the data for direction that are surprising; Table 1 shows these data. In spite of the lack of statistical significance between values per trial, the direction of the differences between _toward_ and _away_ are consistent across trials and run counter to expectation. (This approaches significance by a sign test; (_p_=.061). These data suggest that when the card is moved toward the nose, its afterimage appears to move away from the observer. Furthermore, it was the observation of many of the observers (and the authors) that, when the card is moved toward the nose, its afterimage appears to shrink in size. This was also reported informally by Gregory (1974).

This seems to be a confirmation of Emmert’s Law (Emmert, 1881). This law states that the apparent size of an afterimage varies directly with the distance through which it is “projected”; if an afterimage is formed of a pattern on a projection screen some distance away, it will appear to shrink to match-book size when one shifts one’s gaze to a blank sheet at reading distance. We repeated the procedure just described, but this time we asked our subjects about the change in the size of the card as it was pulled toward the nose; we wanted to know if there is an afterimage size change with sagittal movement in accordance with Emmert’s Law.

As in Experiment 2, binomial tests were used to assess differences between the number of _change_ and _no_-_change_ observers. Then the differences between reports of _smaller_ and _larger_ within the _change_ group were ascertained. These differences in change and size experienced by those who pulled the card toward them tell an interesting story (see Table 2).

While there were no detectable differences between those who detected a change in size and those who saw no size change as the card was pulled toward the nose, this difference improved drastically over the four trials until it approached significance on the last trial. For those who detected a change in size there seemed to be a powerful effect of direction of change; the difference between _smaller_ and _larger_ on Trial 1 approached significance, and the differences in the remaining trials were all highly significant.

Also, in Experiment 1 a dark outline of the hand was left behind as the hand was moved downward. The corresponding effect with sagittal movement is a dark ring that forms around the card as it shrinks during movement toward the nose. Ninety percent of those who reported a reduction in size of the afterimage card also reported the presence of a dark ring that surrounded the card.

Since there were significant differences found here, it was thought that it might be more conservative to account for any bias indicated by control measures. During the demonstration trials, the observers were asked to make judgments of the size (smaller or larger) of the stationary card. Of those who detected a change in the size of their demonstration afterimage, 56% reported “larger” and 44% stated “smaller.” Replacing the assumption of one-half (i.e., the binomial assumption) with these percentages would only improve matters for the test-trial data; the assumption of one-half is more cautious.

The observers’ responses seem to verify the effects of Emmert’s Law in this experiment. As the card is shifted toward the face, its afterimage seems to shrink in size, yet the apparent direction of image movement is away, not toward, the subject. Clearly a cognitive element is making itself known here; the judged movement of the image is consistent with the change in retinal size of a real object’s image as it recedes into the distance.

The important point to be made for our purposes as we survey these demonstrations, is that limb movement in the frontal plane seems to drive the apparent location of the image; kinesthesis captures visual location as our theory describes. The effect of limb movement in a sagittal direction, where cognitive factors can interfere, is ambiguous, indicating the fragility of the effect in the absence of any real stimulus support.

There are other perceptual configurations that seem to occupy a position somewhere between stimulus present and stimulus absent; one confronts a physical object, but one misinterprets it. In short, one sees an illusion or constancy.
