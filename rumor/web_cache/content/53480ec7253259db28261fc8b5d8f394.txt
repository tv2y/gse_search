# A New Look at Voter Turnout: Good News After All? 

Social Education Staff 

Many studies of United States voting have sought to explain why voter participation is declining. Recent research by Michael P. McDonald suggests that the much-lamented “decline in voter participation” is an artifact of the way in which turnout rates have been measured. McDonald is an assistant professor of government and politics in the Department of Public and International Affairs at George Mason University in Virginia, and is currently a fellow at the Brookings Institution. His figures show that voter participation fluctuates up and down, but winds up at 60 percent in the national elections of 2004, exactly where it was almost 50 years ago, in 1956 (see graph). 1Here is McDonald’s argument in a nutshell: The most typical way to calculate the turnout rate has been to divide the number of votes by what is called the “voting-age population” or VAP, which consists of everyone age 18 and older residing in the United States. However, this fraction gives an inaccurate result, because the number in the denominator, the VAP, includes persons who are ineligible to vote, mainly non-citizens and felons. In particular, the growth in immigration has significantly increased the number of people aged 18 and older who cannot vote because they are not citizens. This makes it necessary to have a more accurate measure of voter turnout than one that presumes that all persons aged 18 or over are possible voters. McDonald calculates turnout rates for those who are eligible to vote , calling this group the “voting-eligible population,” or VEP. When U.S. voter participation is calculated in this manner, there is no on-going decline. This argument raises many questions that teachers around the country pose to their students. First, as a math problem, it is an accessible example of the importance of accurate measurement. Second, it offers a simple explanation for what has widely been regarded as an enduring trend of declining voter turnout over the last three decades. Third, it opens a discussion as to who should be able to vote. For example, in recent years, state laws have been relaxed and legal challenges mounted against felony disfranchisement laws, which vary among the states from allowing felons in jail to vote to permanently disfranchising felons. Even considering the rosier figures, there is still plenty of room for improvement in voter turnout in the United States. Voter turnout in the presidential election of 2004, using VEP, was about 60 percent, which means that 4 out of 10 adults who could have voted did not do so. Voter participation for young adults (under 30 years old), while improving lately, still lags behind that of the larger eligible population by ten percentage points or more. At the same time, McDonald’s findings, which show an increase in turnout in the last two presidential elections, will encourage the many civic educators and organiza-tions who have stepped up their efforts to improve voter turnout in recent years. McDonald has laid out his position at “The United States Election Project” (elections.gmu.edu), a website that features a detailed Frequently Asked Questions section, links to academic papers, and discussions and resources about other voting and election issues such as redistricting, election fraud, and the prediction of election results.
