On this day, 26th October 1809, Jane Austen’s niece, Fanny Austen Knight wrote in her pocket book whilst at Chawton Cottage:

“G.M., Aunt J. & I called on Miss Beckford, & Charles spent the day there.”

So Fanny called on Miss Beckford with her grandmother and Jane. This means that they called upon Maria Beckford at Chawton House. She was the sister-in-law of John Charles Middleton, the tenant there. Maria had cared for the family following the death of her sister Charlotte Beckford Middleton.

In a post on the 21st October I had said that the Beckfords were an interesting family and they deserve their own post.

Charlotte and Maria were the daughters of Francis Beckford and Susanna Love of Basing Park and Froxfield in Hampshire. Their brother Francis Love Beckford had inherited the properties upon the death of his father in 1768. The Beckfords were an incredibly wealthy, making their money in the West Indies, particularly Jamaica, where they owned huge sugar cane plantations using slave labour.

Their cousin was the eccentric William Thomas Beckford, unfortunately known as ‘the Fool of Fonthill’. He inherited his father Alderman William Beckford’s fortune at the young age of ten years old as the only legitimate child of at least nine children, and was said, including by Lord Byron,to be the richest man in England. In his poem

Byron wrote:

“And yonder towers the prince’s palace fair:

There thou, too, Vathek! England’s wealthiest son,

Once formed thy Paradise, as not aware

When wanton Wealth her mightiest deeds hath done,

Meek Peace voluptuous lures was ever wont to shun.”

“Vathek” was in reference to the Gothic novel that William Beckford composed in French in 1782, and then translated into English by Reverend Samuel Henley. Beckford was fascinated by all things Gothic He lived a hedonist lifestyle, and as a young man he had caused a scandal and was ostracised by society. As a part of his withdraw al

from society, he had a grand Gothic revival house, Fonthill Abbey, built for him by the eminent architect of the day James Wyatt, taking 16 years. The renowned architectural historian Nikolaus Pevsner described the building as the “most prodigious romantic folly in England”. Sadly the house collapsed not too many years later.

Francis Love Beckford spoken of earlier receives a mention in some Austen books including “Jane Austen The Woman” by George Holbert Tucker, for marrying a “blood cousin” of Jane’s, Joanna Leigh. However Joanna is not from the same Leigh family as Jane’s mother, the Leighs of Adlestrop and Stoneleigh. This Leigh family traces back to the 1500s as the owners of North Court on the Isle of Wight.

However in a letter Austen wrote on 29th May 1811:

“Col. Orde has married our cousin, Margt Beckford, the Marchss of Douglas’s sister. The papers say that her father disinherits her, but I think too well of an Orde to suppose that she has not a handsome independence of her own.”

Jane wrote quite a few letters where she referred to seemingly unrelated people as “our cousin” or “our aunt”, however in this case there is a very tenuous link by marriage through Albinia Bertie. Her sister was Jane Bertie who was mother-in-law to Jane’s brother James. Albinia was the first wife of Francis Beckford, father of Francis Love, Charlotte and Maria Beckford.

“Our cousin”, Margaret Maria Elizabeth Beckford, was the daughter of William Thomas Beckford, “The Fool of Fonthill” was reputedly disowned by her father, for eloping with Lieutenant James Orde after her younger sister Susan married her cousin Alexander Hamilton, Marquess of Douglas in 1810. Some papers contradicted the report that it was an elopement as the marriage took place, by license, at St George’s, Hanover Square, London in front of “respectable witnesses”. However, none of those respectable witnesses were family members.

The Ordes are another family that crop up in Jane’s letters. On Christmas Eve 1798 Jane wrote of her partners at a ball at Manydown:

“Our ball was very thin, but by no means unpleasant. There were thirty-one people, and only eleven ladies out of the number, and but five single women in the room. Of the gentlemen present you may have some idea from the list of my partners—Mr. Wood, G. Lefroy, Rice, a Mr. Butcher (belonging to the Temples, a sailor and not of the 11th Light Dragoons), Mr. Temple (not the horrid one of all), Mr. Wm. Orde (cousin to the Kingsclere man), Mr. John Harwood, and Mr. Calland, who appeared as usual with his hat in his hand, and stood every now and then behind Catherine and me to be talked to and abused for not dancing.”

The William Orde with whom Jane danced was the cousin of the vicar at Kingsclere, John Orde, who was in turn the brother of James Orde who married “our cousin” Margaret Beckford.

#OTD 26th October
