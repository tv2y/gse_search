You just need to know the motherboard and graphics card (if you are planning to buy one). Motherboards come in mainly 3 sizes : micro ATX, mini ATX and full ATX. A smaller size motherboard( and the components) will fit in a bigger size case, but not vice-versa.

Similarly, low and mid range GPUs can fit in some slim cases, but high end ones (since they are lot bigger) like 1060 or above, need a wider case.

The way I see it there are now four classes of gaming PC. October 12 2022 witnessed the birth of a new era in computing with the rise of high refresh rate 4K gaming made possible by the RTX 4090. The 7900XTX and RTX 4080 do a pretty good job of approximating 4K-120 for significantly less money.

The second tier 1440 gamer is ruled by the RTX 3080. The 3060 Ti and 6700XT each have a toe in this camp, but these will barely crack 60fps at the low end. We also see brutes like the 6950X and 3090 Ti. But these do not go substantially beyond the RTX 3080, and thus make up a very small slice of the pie.

Thirdly we have the “mainstream” gamers dominated by the quickly rising RTX 3060.

The RTX 3060 and 3060 mobile sit at #6 and #4 on the Steam Hardware survey. If the two 3060’s were combined, as the 1650, 1060, 1050, and 1050 Ti are, the 3060’s would lead by a landslide—together accounting for over 8% of the GPU’s on Steam.

The downfall of the mighty GTX 1060 after six years reflects the transition from desktop gaming to mobile gaming. The mobile 3060 outpaces the desktop 3060 by a fair margin. This being said, the RTX 3060 is not a 1440p graphics card. In fact, only one GPU in the top ten here could rightly be considered a 1440 graphics card. And in actual fact, the RTX 3070 barely cracks the top ten.

This tells us that the heartbeat of the gaming world are 1080p gamers who use a laptop.

The lowest tier of the gaming PC hierarchy are what I would classify as the Potato. Due to its 2GB of VRAM, I would call the GTX 1050 the top echelon of the Potato PC army. The 1050 Ti, to its credit does at least have 4GB of VRAM.

With CPU and GPU prices so low, I would strongly recommend steering clear of the Potato PC build as we sneak over into 2023. Avoid the GTX 1060 3GB. Avoid the RX 6400 (unless you have an old SFF HP or Dell). The GT 1030 is becoming a terrible value, and there is always the risk of accidentally getting the dreaded DDR4 version of it. The Radeon RX 560 is a Potato GPU, but at least you can get one for $55 and it absolutely demolishes a GT 1030.

The sweet spot GPU IMO is the Radeon RX 6600XT. The sweet spot CPU is the Radeon 5500, which just dropped back to $99 after a week at $105. The ASUS Prime B450M Mark II has held a spot at $79 as the budget B450 board to get in 2022. For a 1TB NVMe drive in the final week of 2022, I would probably go with the Mushkin Tempest Enhanced at $59 or the WD Blue SN570 at $65. You have a ton of good options for DDR4–3200 CL16 gaming RAM for under $45.

Cooling on a low tier gaming PC is not critical. The Ryzen 5500 comes with a Wraith cooler which will be completely adequate for the low wattage CPU. I’m an enthusiastic fan of this clever new compact case from GameMax. It has mesh sides and a mesh top. I think they spent the money in all the right places with this design.

While being a budget case, I would comfortably recommend this for a RTX 3080 or 4080. Three USB sockets on top as well as separate headphone/mic jacks make this case a strong recommendation for creators as well.

Adding a $35 Bronze-rated Enermax Cyberbron PSU to this bang-for-buck build brings the cost of this system to $659 assuming we go with the Radeon 6600XT ASRock Challenger GPU. It will allow you to play some games at 1440p, but largely limited to around 100fps at 1080p for the recent generation of demanding AAA titles. AMD cards do extremely well in Warzone and Far Cry 6.

You can build a $300 potato PC that will be at or near the end of its useful lifespan. Or you can spend $2000 on a RTX 4080 gaming PC that will give you a fantastic 4K gaming experience. But I feel like the 6600XT will put you right in the thick of things along with 80% of the gamers on Steam.

Stepping up your game to a 6700XT and Ryzen 5600 would get your feet squarely into 1440p gaming territory for about $790, which honestly could be an extremely worthwhile investment. Especially when you consider that $790 in Jan 2022 wouldn’t even have gotten you a graphics card.

My experience building my first PC:

I had thought about building a PC for a year and I decided that when I turned in my last final exam and got out for the summer, I would work all summer and build me a really nice computer. So I worked very hard all summer and after a lot of research and talking to some more knowledgeable friends: I had the money and a list of parts.

I went on Amazon, ordered everything, and invited a very experienced friend to oversee my progress. Things went pretty well, installing the CPU, settling the motherboard into the case, installing ram and so on. I haul the massive computer over to my monitor, plug up the power cable and HDMI cable, press the power button and presto, the screen came to life! Except it lasted a total of four seconds and immediately shut off, I tried again, same result. The fans would spin up, the screen would come on, and then four seconds later shut off as if someone yanked the power.

It was getting rather late so my friend said he had to go and we could look at it the next day, but I was horrified. Here I was with the fruits of an entire summer’s work and something was very much wrong. I figured one of the parts had to be defective but I had no idea how to find out which one was and I was ready to send it all back. Defeated, I open the case one more time before calling it a day and I immediately see the problem. In the top left corner of the motherboard was a slot that said ‘CPU’ and sure enough, there was a power cable I had left out that also said ‘CPU.’ I plugged it up and everything worked fine and it turned out to be a killer rig, still running strong.

There's a good chance you will miss something, especially if it's your first time around. The most common thing I hear is after building it and pressing the power button, nothing happens. Don't freak out, trace your steps, be thorough and the solution will more than likely become really obvious like it happened to me. Just be patient and soon enough it will be running, you may even enjoy the experience, I know I did even with that little scare.

Good luck!

Sort of … but not really. Some … yes … not “all”.

Both in terms of what the case may allow for, as well as what possible motherboards are available. There’s basically an “infinite” (at least near so) set of combinations possible. But, very often, some set of common board forms do fit into the majority of cases.

Talking computer “cases”. It’s generally into size classes. Ranging from Small Form Factor (SFF) stuff, usually intended to make a small computer, something like a box next to a TV to handle a media centre. Through some mid-tower or “sleek” yet largish, thing. Through to full tower, large desktop, intended for numerous components and/or cooling. On to huge server boxes, even to rack mountable stuff.

Each of those classes tend to have different form factors for their motherboards. But, there are overlaps.

E.g. you could fit some Mini-ITX, Micro-ATX, etc. board (intended for an SFF case) into some full tower cases. Some huge server cases can fit a normal ATX board, even though it usually tends to use an extended EATX board. And all sorts of combinations may be possible. Sometimes it only requires you to unscrew one (or more) of the stand-off nuts and screw it back into a different hole - so it gets located in a place where the other form factor motherboard needs it.

It’s rather unlikely that you can fit a large form factor board into a small case, though. The thing has to (at least) physically fit in there. So, generally, you’d not be able to fit something like a ATX or EATX into a small form factor case - there just isn’t space, no matter how you arrange stand-off nuts.

Unfortunately, there isn’t a clear rule to follow. It’s whatever the case manufacturer chose to allow for.

Even more “unfortunate”. Some pre-built computers tend to have custom boards. Companies prone to making such include Dell, HP and Apple. You’re highly unlikely to find a standard board to fit into those cases, and their motherboards seldom fit into other manufacturer’s cases.

The standard format is ATX. If you have a regular size ATX case, it’s always better to get ATX versus the smaller formats, because the ATX always has more ports for upgrades and also easier to work with, because you have more room. The only time you don’t want to get ATX is if you are building a small form-factor PC with a micro-size case.

So that part’s easy. The harder part is deciding which motherboard you want.

I’ll explain this as simply as possible.

The motherboard you buy must have the appropriate CPU socket for the CPU you will buy. Once you know that you can filter out all the motherboards that simply will not be able to fit your CPU.

Given the socket type, there are various Chipsets. These chipsets come in series, like the example below is the Intel 200 series of chipsets. You can see what the differences are. One of them is always the low-end, which is made for limited PCs, then there’s at least one mainstream and then the higher end ones are for enthusiasts, like the PCIe 3.0 lanes in the higher end chipsets are for motherboards that can do two graphics cards running together. Most people don’t need the enthusiast chipsets, they’re for those who want these advanced features. Note the motherboard manufacturer has some latitude in some parts of the chipset specs below, they may not actually include all those things, and they may add extra things.

Generally you should always get a chipset from the latest series. The only times you want earlier series is exceptions, like you have an older CPU already, or they’re discounting the series going out.

Once you decide on socket and chipset, you can look at those motherboards and pick one from basically any brand. Some gamer motherboards have things like LED lights and better audio chips, these are not included in the chipset, so the higher cost is because they add these things. Motherboards also differ in what ports they include. Some may have USB-C (the newer format) others have only old-style USB, some may have DisplayPort others won’t, so decide what you need from those things.

You would be better off buying a complete ready assembled PC as a beginner, but if you want to learn, you will have to spend some time reading about differences when assembling an AMD or Intel system. Or you can send me a msg and I can help you find the right parts.

When buying a pc, there are a couple of basic components:

CPU - the processor you want to use in your build, either AMD or Intel. This will determine what kind of motherboard you need to buy (Because the CPU is intended for a specific socket, you will know the socket type your motherboard needs to have). If the CPU does not come with a cooler or even if it comes with one, generally you want to also buy an AIO cooler. When buying an AIO cooler you have to make sure that the case has enough room to fit the radiator of the AIO cooler.

RAM - the memory, these days the most common ram used in desktop computer builds is DDR4 and recently also DDR5. For AM4 you would want at least 3600 MHz module and at least 16 GB, for Intel 3200 MHz or 3600 MHz, but for the newer generation 13th gen CPUs you might want to get DDR5.

When buying RAM, you have to take into account RAM frequency supported by the motherboard (and of course module type supported by the board: DDR4 or DDR5). Higher frequency is better, but not all boards support every module or every frequency. It is not an issue if a RAM module has a higher frequency than the motherboard can support. In the worst case scenario the RAM module will run with the lowest frequency supported by the motherboard. On boards where Overclocking is supported, you will be able to overclock the RAM frequency and use a higher one, but that is already advanced stuff where you have to read about frequencies and settings. Sounds more difficult than it is. DDR5 will run best when having 5200 - 6400 MHz for Intel CPUs but for AMD it is 6000 MHz due to the CPU architecture. For DDR4 AMD works best with 3600 MHz and Intel 3200 to 3600 MHz, but you can overclock intel.

Motherboard - for AMD or for Intel CPUs motherboards come in different form factors (sizes for the case you have chosen- ATX, mini ATX etc.) and with different CPU socket types. Socket type determines what kind of CPU you can use with a specific motherboard. For example an AM4 board can accept Ryzen 5000 series CPUs. However, you have to always check with the seller or double check on the website of the manufacturer if the board really supports a specific CPU, sometimes they are still working on BIOS updates which enable the use of a specific CPU model at a later time. Motherboards also will have different price categories depending on their intended use (office, gaming etc.). The best boards are gaming, as they are designed for high performance. For this reason even professionals will choose a gaming board.

GPU or the graphics card - just buy one with at least 4 GB of RAM. For serious gaming these days you want something with more than 6 GB, especially for higher display resolutions.

Hard disk - your storage where all the files go. These days you can buy SSD drives and M2 SSD drives. If your motherboard has an m2 slot and most modern ones do, this is the best kind of SSD you can buy due to the exceptionally higher speeds. When choosing any drive you have to pay attention to write and read speed. Higher is always better.

PSU - power supply: at least 500 W should be enough for most entry level 1080p systems, but it depends on the parts you are using in your system. There are power supply calculators which can help you calculate the wattage you need. Just googling will bring up multiple choices. A lot of GPUs these days will require a higher wattage, so you have to google it. For example, a 6800XT requires a minimum of 750W. Add to the wattage fans, AIO cooler, LED lighting, hard drives and other components and you get your total consumption. Generally you want to have spare room for overclocking and upgrades, so minimum is just the minimum. It is also advisable to purchase a PSU with 80+ gold rating as this will increase the efficiency of a PSU.

Case - you need to choose the case that will fit the form factor of your motherboard and eventually also the cooler you have chosen for the build
