Here is the main useful information extracted from the text, focusing on the key findings and conclusions about protein supplementation during endurance training:

### **Key Findings**  
1. **Body Composition**:  
   - Protein supplementation had **no significant effect** on body weight or body fat.  
   - A **small, non-significant increase** in lean body mass was observed (SMD = 0.13, *p* = 0.07).  

2. **Physiological Adaptations**:  
   - **No significant improvement** in maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂ max) or anaerobic peak power.  
   - Untrained individuals showed a **potential marginal benefit** in VO₂ max (*p* = 0.06) compared to trained athletes.  

3. **Performance**:  
   - **Significant improvement** in time to exhaustion (TTE) (SMD = 0.45, *p* < 0.01).  
   - **No significant effect** on time trial (TT) performance or sprint speed.  

### **Practical Implications**  
- Protein supplementation **may enhance endurance performance** by prolonging TTE, likely due to improved aerobic metabolism, fat oxidation, or muscle-tendon adaptation.  
- The benefits for lean body mass and aerobic capacity are modest, with greater potential in untrained individuals.  
- Optimal protein intake for endurance athletes ranges from **1.2–2.3 g/kg/day**, aligning with current sports nutrition guidelines.  

### **Limitations**  
- Heterogeneity in study designs (e.g., protein types, training protocols).  
- Limited long-term studies and underrepresented female participants.  
- Small effect sizes for some outcomes (e.g., lean mass, VO₂ max).  

### **Conclusion**  
Protein supplementation during endurance training **improves time to exhaustion** and may slightly increase lean mass but does not significantly alter body fat, VO₂ max, or TT performance. Untrained individuals may experience greater aerobic benefits. Further research is needed on long-term effects and individualized strategies.
