Under the following assumptions:

*   a 30" display has an intrinsic resolution of 2,560 x 1,600 pixels = 4.096m pixels
*   a 24" display has an intrinsic resolution of 1920 x 1080 pixels * 2 = 4.174m pixels

Why you should use 1 x 30" monitor:

*   Less desk-space occupied
*   No neck-strain from moving your head side-to-side
*   No struggling to find your mouse-cursor
*   Greater pixel-height means less scrolling
*   If gaming is your thing: Greater frame-rates, Better 3D gaming compatibility and no gaps in the 'viewport'

Why you should use 2 x 24" monitors:

*   Only slightly more 'screen real-estate'
*   Ability to 'dock' windows to each monitor making it potentially a more 'functional' setup - see below...

I think the core reason people end up using Dual-monitor setups is for primary/secondary use. The secondary monitor is great for displaying complimentary info such as console output, or real-time monitoring graphs while they're actively using the main monitor for their primary work. The secondary monitor becomes a point of reference for the activity in the primary monitor.

(A good example above of a Primary/Secondary desktop setup - image from: http://www.unplggd.com/unplggd/flickr-finds/dual-27-screens-on-imac-cheap-multimonitor-setup-139015 )

What about the practical applications?

'Office work'

If you maximised a windowed program like Excel in each one of the setups, you'd get the following scenario:

*   On the 30" monitor, your Excel window would occupy 2560x1600 pixels.
*   On the dual 24" monitors your Excel window would only occupy one of the monitors: 1920x1080 pixels.

The 30" monitor setup would win here.

Why wouldn't you want to stretch the Excel window across both monitors? Because the fascia gets in the way.

If you were copy/pasting data from another source or sheet, how would these two compare?

*   On the 30" monitor, you'd have to Alt-Tab a lot, but your gaze would be fixed and your mouse is already over the working area.
*   On the dual 24" monitors, you'd have to be active with the mouse across both screens, and move your head sideways to see the values on the secondary monitor.

What about gaming?

The appeal of multi-monitor setups for 3D gaming is all about immersion.

*   On the 30" monitor, it's gaming as normal, but with potentially higher frame-rates.
*   On the dual 24" monitors your viewport would be split right down the middle, and the fascia for each monitor would break the viewport. In 3D games, the center of the viewport is the most important part of the screen, and now it has been bisected by 1-2 inches. Not great.

What about watching a movie?

Same thing here... The dual-monitor setup cuts the screen in half (if it can stretch across both monitors) else you're watching a movie in one of the two monitors, and that has less screen real estate than using one big monitor.

What about a compromise?

Too expensive right now...

Image from: http://www.ubergizmo.com/2009/06/nec-crv-43-now-on-sale/

My experience

I've tried both setups, for work, playing games and movies, and I've settled on the "One-big monitor" setup. (Could you tell?)

If you can't get two identical monitors, and have them sitting right next to one another, then your results with dual-screen setups would be even less impactful.

If you're not going to use a dual-monitor setup in a primary/secondary pairing then I don't think it's worth it.

Computer monitors have a variety of different features, some of which are more important than others. You should know how you plan to use your computer monitor before making a purchase decision.

Screen size: The size of the screen is one of the most important factors in choosing a computer monitor. If you want to be able to see everything on your screen, then you need a 24" or larger monitor. A smaller monitor will be less comfortable for viewing, and may make it difficult to do certain tasks.

Resolution: Resolution refers to how many pixels are on each inch of the screen. The higher the resolution, the sharper and clearer your images will appear. For example, 1920x1080 (Full HD) resolution is considered high-end because it offers more detail than lower resolutions like 1280x1024 (Standard HD).

Refresh rate: Refresh rate refers to how many times per second each pixel can change color. The higher the refresh rate, the faster images will appear on your screen because they don't have time to switch colors twice as often as they do at lower rates such as 60Hz or 30Hz.

Contrast ratio: Contrast ratio refers to the difference in luminance, or brightness, between the darkest and brightest color on a screen. This is measured in number of contrast ratio candela per square meter (cd/m2).

The higher the number, the better. For example, an LCD monitor with a contrast ratio of 1,000:1 will show 1 million candela per square meter on its display.

Contrast ratios are important because they can affect how well your eyes can see details on the screen. Some monitors have high contrast ratios that allow you to see more detail on the screen than other monitors with lower contrast ratios.

These are different forms of communication, but the process of bringing them to fruition has a necessary, sometimes overlooked step: editing! Unless you’re a professional writer, it’s unlikely that you have an editor who can review your writing regularly. Here are some tips to help you review your own work.

1.   Give your writing some space. Have you ever felt a mix of pure relief and joy when you’ve finished a draft of something? Don’t downplay that feeling and the ability to walk away from your work before you start editing it. You may need minutes, hours, or days, but once you sit back down with what you originally had on the page, you’ll have the thrill of looking at it with fresh eyes. You’ll notice errors you may not have seen the first time. You’ll come to new realizations about its overall tone and structure. If it’s a text or email, maybe you only need a few minutes away from it. If it’s a story or essay, perhaps you’ll need longer. Regardless of what type of work it is, it will help your writing tremendously.
2.   Don’t use overachieving synonyms. Looking at your work for the second, third, or fourth time around may inspire you to spice up your language with longer, more uncommon words. There’s nothing wrong with having a thesaurus nearby, but try to limit the repetition of long, pretentious-feeling words so your work flows well and doesn’t feel too bogged down. At the end of the day, you want it to feel true to you and the message you’re conveying.
3.   Remember who the reader is. Don’t forget your own voice as the writer—but don’t forget who your reader is. Many writers get too close to their work; editing is a chance to try to get out of your own head. Who is your ideal reader? What do you want them to take away from the writing? It’s a unique time to step in their shoes, to make sure your communication is as effective as you’d like it to be.
4.   Kill your darlings. Don’t be scared to remove chunks of your work, even if it feels precious to you. If it’s a passage that’s really tough to part with, try saving it somewhere else, so you can return to it later in your piece or for another work.

After using 2, 3, and 4 monitors over the past 15 years, I’m strongly considering moving to 1 large monitor.

Here’s my personal pros and cons:

1.   Single large monitor

    1.   Less desk footprint
    2.   Simpler for software to handle, especially when using a docking station and a OS which tends to suck at handling multiple monitors (cough.. Linux…) paired with certain video cards.
    3.   More flexible positioning (if you have two side by side monitors, your windows are going to be either left or right, not in the middle.)
    4.   Similarly, with two monitors, you are looking to the left or right. With one monitor, you are looking straight.
    5.   Less cables
    6.   Less trouble synchronizing the colors (I have two identical fairly high end monitors — for their day — and I never managed to synchronize the color settings so things look identical. Of course, they are not Dreamcolor monitors and I’m not an animator, so it doesn’t bother me that much.. but if I photoshop something on one screen and I move it to the other, the difference can surprise)

2.   Multiple monitors

    1.   You can full-screen on only one monitor
    2.   The form factor tends to be more pleasant (the ultrawide monitors feel wide, but squished down… I am not crazy about the ratio they come in)
    3.   You can mentally focus on one monitor, while keeping distractions on another.. your brain seems to adjust better to that design.
    4.   You can simulate a “curve” without buying a curved monitor.

First decide on the screen resolution you want. This should be dependant on your computer’s graphical capabilities, if you have a low, mid or high tier computer. Purchasing a monitor may be challenging, particularly when considering alternatives for businesses. There are several versions available at vastly various price points, and each one excels in certain aspects while falling short in others. Here are a few things to consider before you buy monitor online.

1. Monitor size

You may get displays as little as 20 inches and as large as 70 inches and larger. There isn't a single, perfect solution to this. You only need to make sure you choose the display size that best suits your requirements.

The majority of visual specialists concur that if you are seated at a desk and are facing your screen at a regular distance, 32 inches provides the best distance-to-size ratio. For the majority of PCs, it is now the accepted monitor size.

Due to size restrictions, some individuals will have to purchase something smaller, while others may need to purchase bigger screens. As a general rule, purchasing a monitor that is too big will never make you regret it.

2. Monitor resolution

A monitor's display resolution is frequently marketed as a selling factor. Better if it is higher. Although a high resolution will provide crisper images, there may also be some drawbacks.

One issue is how large text and icons appear to be. When the resolution of a display is increased without expanding its physical size, everything on that display seems smaller. While for most individuals this is not a problem, users with impaired vision may experience issues with a display that has a high resolution and a tiny display size.

3. Specs of the monitor

The following are some crucial monitor specifications to take into account:

● Refresh rates: This term describes how frequently a monitor changes its display each second. Do not think of going below 75Hz. Gamers want a minimum of 140Hz.

● Response time: The time it takes for a display to turn a single black pixel into a white one. The lowest possible rate for premium displays is 0.5Hz.

● Aspect Ratio: The 16:9 ratio is most frequently observed in retail settings. While 16:10 is also quite popular among desktop users, 3:2 screens are becoming increasingly common on laptops.

● Curvature: The recommended viewing distance for a monitor with 1500R is 1.5 metres, and its radius is 150 cm. The curvature increases as the number decreases.
