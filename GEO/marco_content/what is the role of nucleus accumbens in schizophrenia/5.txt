ABSTRACT 

Schizophrenia is a mental illness affecting 1% of the population worldwide. Treatment options are limited for patients, due in part to the lack of understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms of schizophrenia. In order to improve treat-ment, it is vital to gain a better understanding of the underlying pathology of the disorder. 

One region of particular interest is the nucleus accumbens (NAcc). Part of the ventral striatum, this region is thought to play a role in schizophrenia pathology for multiple reasons: afferent input of many brain regions implicated in schizophre-nia is integrated here; the dopamine and glutamate systems, both known to be dis-rupted in schizophrenia, are highly interactive in the NAcc; and animal models with disruptions in the NAcc suggest abnormalities in this region could lead to symptoms of schizophrenia. Despite these reasons, there is little evidence from human studies to support the role of the NAcc in the pathology of schizophrenia. 

Ultrastructural studies in the NAcc of postmortem human tissue analyzed synaptic density in schizophrenia and control subjects. There was an increase in the density of asymmetric axospinous synapses, characteristic of excitatory input, in the NAcc of schizophrenia subjects. Consistent with this finding, a western blot analysis measuring protein levels of the vesicular glutamate transporter found increased iii 

levels of vGLUT2 which is localized in glutamatergic axon terminals. These findings suggest the NAcc receives elevated excitatory input in schizophrenia. 

The NAcc receives dense dopaminergic projections and elevated striatal do-pamine is a hallmark characteristic of schizophrenia. Within the NAcc however, the ultrastructrual analysis found no difference in symmetric synapses in schizophrenia, the type formed by dopaminergic input. Further, no difference was found in protein levels or staining density of tyrosine hydroxylase, the rate-limiting enzyme in do-pamine synthesis. These results suggest that the NAcc has normal levels of dopa-mine synthesis in schizophrenia. 

These findings suggest the NAcc receives excessive glutamatergic input to the region. These studies provide the first ultrastructural evidence in human tissue to support the role of the NAcc in schizophrenia, and are highly consistent with hy-potheses regarding the role of the NAcc in the pathophysiology of the disorder.

INTRODUCTION 

Anatomy of the Nucleus Accumbens 

Structural Organization 

The striatum is a complex subcortical structure of the basal ganglia that con-sists of multiple levels of structural and functional divisions. The dorsal striatum consists of the caudate nucleus and putamen, which are separated by a bundle of white matter fibers called the internal capsule. Regions of the ventral striatum, de-scribed originally by Heimer and Wilson (1975) consist of the nucleus accumbens (NAcc), olfactory tubercle, and ventral pallidum. Of these ventral striatal regions however, the research discussed here will focus only on the NAcc. Though the bor-der between the dorsal and ventral striatum is ambiguous, distinctions between the dorsal and ventral striatum can be made most readily by their functional properties. Further heterogeneity unique to each region is discussed briefly below. 

Core and Shell of the Nucleus Accumbens 

While neurochemical heterogeneity exists within in the ventral striatum, the pattern is not consistent with the patch and matrix organization present in the dor-sal striatum. The NAcc can be divided into subregions called the core and shell based on its neurochemical properties. These subregions, described originally by Záborszky and colleagues (1985) in rats, have been identified across species, includ-2

ing monkeys and humans (Meredith et al, 1996; Brauer et al, 2000). The inner core region is cytoarchitecturally most similar to the dorsal striatum; the outer shell re-gion, which extends from the lateral edge of the NAcc medially to the lower tip of the lateral ventral, has been associated with the extended amygdala based on its cytoar-chitectural organization and function (Heimer et al, 1991). Some immunohisto-chemical markers display different staining intensities in the core and shell and are useful for distinguishing between the regions; for example, the core exhibits dense immunoreactivity for calbindin which is much weaker in the shell (Zahm and Brog, 1992). 

Input to the Nucleus Accumbens 

A comprehensive review of the inputs and outputs of the NAcc is outside the scope of this dissertation, but a brief overview of relevant projections will be dis-cussed in the next two sections. For reviews of NAcc connections, see Groenewegen et al. (1999) and Haber et al. (1999). 

The NAcc core and shell receive many overlapping afferent connections. The largest source of the input arises from cortical glutamatergic projections from re-gions of the medial and orbital prefrontal cortex including anterior cingulate cortex, medial and lateral orbital cortex, and agranular insular cortex (Kunishio and Haber, 1994; Haber et al, 1995). The core and shell both receive glutamatergic input from the basal nucleus of the amygdala, as well as the thalamus and hippocampus (Groe-newegen et al, 1987; Berensde and Groenwegen, 1990; Wright et al, 1996). Each of these project topographically to the subregions of the NAcc. Input from the anterior 3

Both subregions of the NAcc receive dense dopaminergic input from the substantia nigra and ventral tegmental area (SN/VTA); both the core and shell receive input from the dorsal tier of the SN pars compacta, while the core receives additional in-put from the ventral tier of the SN pars compacta and the shell receives input from the VTA (Lynd-Balta and Haber, 1994). Dopaminergic input tends to form symmet-ric synapses onto spine necks or dendritic shafts (Fig 1; Pickel et al, 1981; Bouyer et al, 1984). The DA and glutamate systems are highly interactive in the NAcc, with re-ciprocal modulation of release (Morari et al, 1998; O’Donnell et al, 1999; Nicola et al, 2000). This modulation of afferent input primarily occurs indirectly where dopa-minergic and glutamatergic axon terminals tend to make convergent input onto the same spine. Occasionally this input occurs via directly axo-axonic connections, or 5

indirectly through spillover or release from axonal varicosities. (Fig. 2; Pasik et al, 1988). 

Within the NAcc, local interneurons also provide inhibitory input to the me-dium spiny projection neurons. Similar to the dopaminergic input, these synapses are typically symmetric and localized to dendrites and spine necks (Fig. 1). 

Output of the Nucleus Accumbens 

GABAergic medium spiny projection neurons of the NAcc primarily project within the basal ganglia to the ventral pallidum and SN/VTA. GABAergic neurons of the ventral pallidum then project to the neurons of thalamus. Thalamic neurons provide excitatory input to the cortex, closing the cortical-striatal-thalamo-cortical loop and illustrating the key role that the NAcc has in cortical signaling (Fig. 3). Out-put to the dopaminergic neurons of the SN/VTA allow for reciprocal feedback of the mesolimbic and feedforward influence of the nigrostriatal dopaminergic pathways. Efferents of the NAcc project throughout the midbrain, influencing the firing of do-paminergic neurons that project to the dorsal striatum. These connections project directly to the dopaminergic neurons as well as to midbrain GABAergic interneu-rons, creating a balance of inhibition and disinhibition to regulate dopamine output in the striatum. Via these feedforward connections, the NAcc has widespread influ-ence over dopaminergic signaling in the dorsal striatum (Haber et al, 2000). 6

Functional Roles of the Nucleus Accumbens 

The diversity of input to the striatum reflects the wide array of functions this structure is involved in. Connections between the striatum and other brain regions are organized into multiple parallel basal ganglia-thalamocortical loops (Alexander et al, 1986; Parent and Hazrati, 1995; Haber, 2003) which engage the striatum in a variety of functions including motor planning and execution, cognition, emotional processing, and motivation. In a review of the primate basal ganglia, Haber (2003) describes how the input of each of these loops is organized topographically, and the corresponding spectrum of functional properties. This topographical organization of striatal input has been used as the basis for defining its distinct functional regions (Parent, 1990), in addition to the anatomical divisions discussed above. 7

The functional subdivisions of the striatum are the sensorimotor, associative, and limbic regions. The NAcc is part of the limbic striatum, along with the ventral caudate and putamen. Innervated by the hippocampus, amygdala, and cortical lim-bic areas such as orbitofrontal and anterior cingulate cortex, the limbic striatum is involved in reward and motivation, and goal-directed behaviors (for reviews, see Parent and Hazrati, 1995; Joel and Weiner, 2000). The functional roles of the core and shell are largely overlapping, but some variation does exist due to the topo-graphical organization of input. For example, the shell responds more robustly to pharmacological stimulants such as phencyclidine (PCP) and MK-801, as well as sensitization to psychostimulants compared to the core (Zahm, 1999). The robust response to antipsychotic drugs in the shell has also led many to conclude that the shell, rather than the core, is implicated in the pathophysiology of schizophrenia (Heimer et al, 1997). 

The distinct functional properties of the NAcc, versus the dorsal striatal re-gions, makes it critical to study this region separately, in order to better understand the functional consequences of pathology that may be localized to the NAcc. Further, the variation in projections to and from the NAcc core and shell warrant an interest in studying these subregions separately. 

Schizophrenia 

Definition and Epidemiology 

The meaning of the term “schizophrenia” (SZ), introduced in 1908 by Paul Eugen Bleuler, is derived from the Greek translations of schizein (“to split”) and 8

phren- (“soul, spirit, mind”). It is a heterogeneous clinical syndrome, diagnosed by the presence of an array of symptoms, and by impairment in social and occupational functioning. 

The risk of this illness ranges from 1% in the general population to approxi-mately 50% in monozygotic twins. A systemic review of SZ epidemiology found the incidence in males to be higher than females, with a male to female ratio of 1.4 (McGrath et al, 2008), however no significant difference in prevalence. Some studies report the rate of SZ in the US varying across race, with African-Americans nearly twice as likely to be diagnosed with SZ as whites in one study (Bresnahan et al, 2007), though a difference across race has not been found in all studies (Arnold et al, 2004). 

Schizophrenia is regarded as a neurodevelopment disorder, with adverse pre- and peri-natal events being associated with increased risk of the disorder, and a relative lack of neurodegenerative processes in patients (Lewis and Levitt, 2002; Rapoport et al, 2012). It is thought that an environmental insult during a critical time in development, in combination with genetic risk, produces a cascade of altered development that ultimately leads to the development of SZ during adolescence (Lewis and Levitt, 2002). Researchers investigating the potential cause of SZ ap-proach the question from both directions, with either animal models of neonatal in-sults, or by working backward from the pathological mechanisms identified in pa-tients and postmortem tissue. We have learned a lot about the pathology of SZ and developed hypotheses to explain mechanisms that could lead to symptoms of SZ, all of which is discussed below. However, much of these hypotheses remain specula-9

tive. There is a need for a better understanding of pathophysiological mechanisms in SZ, as well as consideration of cause versus consequence, before its cause can be pinned down. 

Symptoms of Schizophrenia 

The symptoms of SZ generally cluster into three categories: positive, nega-tive, and cognitive. Positive symptoms are an excess or distortion of normal func-tioning, including hallucinations and delusions. Negative symptoms are a loss or def-icit in normal functioning, such as anhedonia, flat affect, and avolition. Cognitive symptoms, most debilitating for patients, involve numerous forms of cognition in-cluding working memory, executive functioning, and verbal memory. Heterogeneity within SZ is apparent in the wide range of symptom severity that patients experi-ence within these three categories. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) 5 addresses this heterogeneity by the use of a 5-point scale of se-verity for each symptom (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Other psychotic (schizoaffective, schizophreniform), mood (bipolar), and personality (schizotypal, schizoid) disorders have many overlapping symptoms with SZ but vary in key as-pects, such as the duration of the illness, persistence of psychotic symptoms, or oth-er concurrent symptoms. Thus for this dissertation, only subjects with a DSM diag-nosis of SZ were studied, subjects with other related diagnoses were not included to reduce further heterogeneity already present in the disorder. 

The psychotic features of SZ typically develop in the early-twenties for males, and late-twenties for females (McGrath et al, 2008). Over the course of the illness, 10 

patients may experience periods of active symptoms alternating with periods of re-mission, however cognitive deficits tend to remain persistent even during remission of psychotic symptoms. Early age of onset, cognitive impairments, and negative symptoms are all associated with a worse prognosis (Tamminga et al, 1998). 

Neuropathology 

Pathology in SZ has been found at all levels, from whole brain volume down to synaptic organization. Abnormalities however, tend to be subtle, with no large scale central point of pathology. Decades of research in SZ pathology has led to an understanding that the disorder is caused by miswiring between various brain re-gions, rather than one primary region of pathology (Bunney et al, 1995; Friston, 1999; Stephan et al, 2009). 

While the origin of the miswiring remains unknown, many well-validated findings have provided evidence of SZ pathology that supports this concept. Strong evidence for reduced brain volume, the same number of neurons but increased neu-ronal density in the cortex, as well as a reduction in pre- and post-synaptic markers, supports a hypothesis of reduced neuropil in cortical pathology in SZ (Selemon and Goldman-Rakic, 1999); reductions in cortical volume are due to a loss of synaptic connections and dendritic arborization that make up neuropil, rather than neuronal loss. The reduced neuropil hypothesis is consistent with the concept of SZ as a dis-order of dysconnection between brain regions with pathology occurring at the level of synaptic connections. 11 

Functional correlates of miswiring have been widely reported in SZ; studies with positron emission tomography (Volkow et al, 1988; Buchsbaum et al, 1992a; Friston et al, 1992, 1996; Siegel et al, 1993; Andreasen et al, 1996; Meyer-Lindenberg et al, 2005) and fMRI (Weinberger et al, 1992; Lawrie et al, 2002; Had-ley et al, 2014) in patients implicate abnormal connections between brain regions, some of which correlate with specific symptoms. As discussed in Functional Roles of the Nucleus Accumbens , the dorsal striatum and NAcc play important roles in multi-ple functional loops in the brain, and disruptions in functional connectivity between the striatum and other brain regions have been shown in SZ (Zhou et al, 2007; Sal-vador et al, 2010; Diaconescu et al, 2011; Hoffman et al, 2011; Orliac et al, 2013; Quide et al, 2013). 

Miswiring of the brain should also result in morphological differences at the level of synapses and dendritic spines. Indeed, subjects with SZ have a reduced den-sity of cortical dendritic spines (Garey et al, 1998; Glantz and Lewis, 2000; Sweet et al, 2009). While ultrastructural studies offer the means to examine this directly, few studies have been done to assess pathology at this level in SZ. Within the striatum, quantitative analysis of structural pathology at the synaptic level has found reduced spine size (Roberts, 1996), abnormalities in the postsynaptic density (Uranova et al, 1996), and alterations in synaptic density (Kung et al, 1998; Roberts et al, 2005a,b, 2008) in SZ. Structural correlates of dysconnection have also been found in the stri-atum based on treatment response (Roberts et al, 2009). These studies provide di-rect evidence for miswiring in a key region of the brain in SZ. 12 

Pathology of the NAcc. Within the basal ganglia, studies of pathology in SZ have largely focused on the dorsal striatum, with relatively few studies in the mid-brain and NAcc (Perez-Costas et al, 2010). The few studies that have investigated pathology within the NAcc were inconclusive. A stereological study by Beckmann and Lauer (1997) found an increase in neuronal number in the NAcc, consistent with evidence for increased volume of the region (Lauer et al, 2001; Spolentini et al, 2011). Other stereological studies however, found a 48% reduction in neuronal number (Pakkenberg, 1990) and no difference in neuronal number (Kreczmanski et al, 2007). There was great initial interest early on in analyzing neurochemical pa-thology in the NAcc, specifically dopamine (DA) and DA release. These studies, all done in postmortem SZ subjects who were on medication prior to death, either found no differences (Farley et al, 1977; Owen et al, 1978; Toru et al, 1982; Hetey et al, 1991) or increases (Bird et al, 1979; Mackay et al, 1982) in DA measures. 

Despite the support for the role of the NAcc in SZ, the neuropathology of the NAcc has largely been overlooked in SZ research. Furthermore, the few anatomical and neurochemical studies in the NAcc of SZ subjects have not investigated its dis-tinct subregions, the core and shell, which are structurally, neurochemically, and functionally distinguishable as discussed above. Meredith et al. (1995) showed that the core and shell have very different responses to DA depletion; within the core there was a loss of spines on projection neurons, while in the shell there was an in-crease in the tortuosity of dendritic trees. These profound differences in the struc-tural consequences to DA depletion in the subregions of the NAcc show the necessi-13 

ty of studying disease-related effects and pharmacological consequences on a re-gional basis. 

Pathology of connected regions. Support for the NAcc playing a key role in SZ is apparent in that all of the major regions it is connected to, including the cortex, thalamus, hippocampus, and midbrain exhibit pathology in SZ. Though no studies have been able to directly analyze efferent projections to the NAcc in SZ, a study from our laboratory in the SN/VTA analyzed specific regions in postmortem tissue that are known to house either mesolimbic/mesocortical or nigrostriatal projecting DA neurons. Interestingly, they reported reduced tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) levels in the region of mesolimbic/mesocortical DA neurons, and no difference in the re-gion of nigrostriatal DA neurons (Perez-Costas et al, 2012). The region with reduced TH levels projects both to the cortex and ventral striatum, and is consistent with ev-idence for reduced TH levels in the cortex (Akil et al 1999, 2000). 

A review of the pathology of each of the regions projecting to the NAcc is out-side the scope of this dissertation, however the interconnection of affected regions and their link to each other through the NAcc is highlighted by Grace et al. (2000) where the authors summarize evidence for the role of the limbic system in the path-ophysiology of SZ. 

Antipsychotic Treatment of Schizophrenia 

The primary treatment for patients with SZ is antipsychotic medication. A drug called chlorpromazine was discovered to have antipsychotic properties in 1954 and became the first antipsychotic drug (APD) to treat psychiatric patients. 14 

After its introduction, several more APDs followed, all with similar binding proper-ties to chlorpromazine. This group of APDs is referred to as first-generation antipsy-chotics, characterized by their high affinity for DA D2 receptors. These drugs often result in extrapyramidal symptoms due to the chronic blockade of DA neurotrans-mission in the striatum. Additionally, these drugs are only effective at treating the psychotic symptoms in SZ and have no therapeutic effect on negative symptoms or cognitive impairments. Thus, there was a search to find a drug capable of treating a wider array of symptoms in SZ and with fewer side effects. Clozapine was developed in 1990 and started the wave of second-generation APDs. A relatively loose binding to D2 receptors and antagonism of central serotonergic receptors are characteristic of this class of APDs. While the lower affinity for D2 receptors produces fewer ex-trapyramidal symptoms, second-generation antipsychotics are associated with their own set of side effects, particularly metabolic complications. Besides the reduced extrapyramidal symptoms, second-generation antipsychotics as a whole have not offered a substantially improved treatment option for patients compared to the first-generation drugs (Lieberman et al, 2005; Leucht et al, 2009). In addition to the limited therapeutic effect of both first- and second-generation APDs, a major short-coming of this treatment option is that many patients see no improvement in symp-toms from the antipsychotics. 

Despite these shortcomings, APDs remain dominant as the treatment for SZ today, and D2 receptor occupancy continues to be most correlated with antipsychot-ic efficacy (Seeman and Lee, 1976; Creese et al, 1976). This illustrates the current need for more effective treatment options for patients. The unknown cause of the 15 

disorder however, remains a barrier to further progress. Thus, a better understand-ing of the underlying pathology in SZ is imperative to making advancements in treating the disorder. 

Regions of action. Based on their mechanism of action, it is not surprising to find APDs targeting the striatum (Buchsbaum et al, 1992b; Holcomb et al, 1996; Lahti et al, 2005), a region with high expression of D2 receptors and associated with elevated levels of DA in SZ (discussed below). The NAcc has long been implicated in APD action based on studies characterizing neuronal activation in response to treatment (Deutch et al, 1992; Robertson and Fibiger, 1992; Merchant and Dorsa, 1993; Robertson et al, 1994). By analyzing activated regions that overlap between different APDs, clinical and preclinical studies have sought to tease apart therapeu-tic effects of APDs verses their side effects (Chiodo and Bunney, 1983; White and Wang, 1983; Robertson and Fibiger, 1992; Merchant and Dorsa, 1993; Robertson et al, 1994; Rodriguez et al, 2001; Lahti et al, 2005). These studies have consistently implicated activation in subregions of the striatum as areas for both therapeutic and side effect responses to APDs. The evidence for activity of APDs within the NAcc makes it necessary to consider chronic APD treatment as a potential confound when studying human tissue. The potential effects of both classes of APDs, versus altera-tions intrinsic to the disorder, must be taken into account when interpreting find-ings in this region. 

Addressing APD treatment in SZ research. An advantage to imaging studies in SZ patients is the potential to study patients at the onset of their symptoms, prior to medication with APDs. However, studies in postmortem tissue, such as the research 16 

in this dissertation, are limited to chronic SZ cases, most of which have been medi-cated for their entire lives. After a lifetime of D2 receptor antagonism, it is likely that synaptic and neurochemical changes may have occurred. Thus, we use multiple ap-proaches to address the potentially confounding factor of APD treatment. One is by treating rats with APDs, which will be discussed below in the section on Use of Ani-mal Models in Schizophrenia Research . Another is studying off-drug SZ subjects. 

Though medication-naïve subjects are unheard of in postmortem research, some subjects have been off medication prior to death. Different lengths are used in research to define “off-drug”, however we use a 6 month period of no antipsychotic medication to define the off-drug criteria. This is based on neurochemical, ultra-structural, and imaging studies of APD withdrawal in patients and animal models. Rats treated with APDS maintain ultrastructural changes in the dorsal striatum for at least one month after withdrawal (Roberts et al, 1995; Kelley et al, 1997), and changes in TH in the SN for at least 2 weeks after withdrawal (Mazurek et al, 1998). An imaging study analyzing D2 receptor binding after withdrawal from haloperidol recommends a 6 month withdrawal for receptor occupancy levels to return to nor-mal (Nyberg, 1997). Thus, we require subjects to be off APDs for at least 6 months prior to death to be considered “off-drug”. While off-drug doesn’t allow the same power as medication-naïve, it allows us to compare between those on- and off-drug to interpret the potential effect of APD treatment on our results. 17 

Neurochemical Hypotheses of Schizophrenia 

We have learned increasing amounts of information about SZ over the past decade however, the cause, or primary source of its pathology, remains unclear. While research continues to determine the origin of this disorder, it remains a pos-sibility that multiple paths lead to the set of symptoms diagnosed as SZ in the clinic. In other words, it is possible that the term “SZ” actually refers to multiple disorders in the brain, all which have overlapping outcomes for the patients who suffer from them. From the knowledge gained however, several hypotheses of SZ and its origins in the brain have been formed. These hypotheses include neurochemical origins, dysconnectivity, interneuron synchronization, or developmental origins. A full re-view of all hypotheses of SZ is outside of the scope of this document. This section will discuss evidence for two prevailing neurochemical hypothesis that are relevant to this dissertation. 

Dopamine hypothesis of SZ. The oldest hypothesis of SZ is the DA hypothesis. The original DA hypothesis emerged from the discovery that APDs affect DA levels in the brain, work done by Carlsson and Lindqvist (1963). This hypothesis was fur-ther developed by the work of Creese et al (1976) who discovered that the efficacy of APDs were directly related to their affinity for DA receptors. Since these initial discoveries, much has been learned about the role of DA in SZ, including numerous studies which have linked aspects of the DA system with symptoms of the disorder. The sum of the past few decades of findings have refined the DA hypothesis into its current form, as Howes and Kapur (2009) explain nicely in their review of the up-dated DA hypothesis. 18 

As a result of numerous imaging studies in recent years, in combination with biochemical and genetic studies, it is now understood that DA abnormalities in SZ are regionally specific. Two regions with abnormalities in particular are focused on: striatal hyperdopaminergia and cortical hypodopaminergia. These two regions of dopaminergic dysfunction in SZ do not exist independently, but for the purpose of this work we will focus on striatal hyperdopaminergia. 

General over-activity of the DA system throughout the brain was thought to be the cause of SZ based on those initial findings that APD action was connected to blockade of DA signaling, particularly of D2 receptors. It is now known that hy-perdopaminergia in SZ is localized to the striatum. Further, this over-activity is oc-curring presynaptically at the axon terminals of DA projections within the striatum, both by elevated levels of DA synthesis and by increased release (Miyake et al 2011). There is strong evidence implicating the striatal DA system in the etiology of SZ. Elevations in striatal DA are present in prodromal (Howes et al 2009, 2011) and first-episode (Hietala et al, 1995; Abi-Dargham et al, 2000) patients, implicating it early on in the disorder and ruling out medication effects. Further, higher levels of striatal DA are correlated with more severe symptoms in patients with SZ (Howes et al, 2009, 2011), and imaging studies continue to support the striatum as a region critical for the therapeutic action of APDs (Lahti et al, 2003, 2005, 2009). 

Though the NAcc has long been implicated in the etiology of SZ, and often as-sumed to be the locus of elevated striatal DA in SZ, recent imaging studies have be-gun to challenge this idea. With improvements in imaging resolution, studies (Howes et al, 2009; Kegeles et al, 2010) have found the elevated striatal DA to be 19 

localized to the associative striatum (caudate and rostral putamen), not the limbic striatum (ventral caudate and putamen, and NAcc). This has led to a reconceptual-ization of the DA hypothesis of SZ (Kuepper et al, 2012). These imaging findings have yet to be substantiated in postmortem tissue, which allows for more detailed analysis of the distinct striatal subregions. 

Glutamate hypothesis of SZ. With the limited therapeutic effect of APDs, the SZ research field began searching for alternative drug targets that could treat a wider array of symptoms and be beneficial for more patients. With the DA hypothesis still driving most of the research however, alternative hypotheses to explain the etiology of SZ were necessary. An interest in the role of glutamate in SZ originated from dis-coveries that PCP, a drug which produces symptoms mimicking SZ, inhibits N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) neurotransmission (Javitt and Zukin, 1991). Further, PCP, as well as other NMDA-antagonists such as ketamine, mimic more domains of the illness, including negative and cognitive symptoms (Krystal et al, 1994; Jentsch and Roth, 1999), in contrast to dopamine-specific drugs such as amphetamine, which primarily induce only psychotic symptoms (see Jones et al, 2011 for review). Though the effects of PCP were documented in the early 1960’s (Luby et al, 1962), and a hypothesis involving glutamate was first proposed in 1980 (Kim et al, 1980), the glutamate hypothesis of SZ did not begin gaining ground until the past couple decades. 

It is now known that glutamatergic abnormalities are present in the disorder which has been shown with postmortem, genetic, and imaging studies (Goff and Coyle, 2001). These findings led to the development of a hypothesis for SZ which fo-20 

cuses on glutamate as the central neurotransmitter of the disorder (Moghaddam and Javitt, 2012). Within the striatum, increased levels of glutamate were found in the caudate of prodromal and first-episode SZ patients (de la Fuente-Sandoval et al, 2011). Consistent with the imaging findings, Roberts et al. (2005b) found an in-crease in asymmetric axospinous synapses in the caudate of SZ subjects, the type of synaptic contact made by glutamatergic input to the striatum (French and Totter-dell, 2004). No imaging studies of glutamate have been done in the NAcc in SZ. Sev-eral studies investigating glutamate receptors in the NAcc in SZ have found no dif-ference in NMDA, AMPA, mGluR, or kainate receptor binding or expression (Meador-Woodruff et al, 2001; Gupta et al, 2005; Aparicio-Legarza et al, 1988; Noga et al, 1997; Freed et al, 1993; Healy et al, 1998). 

Integrating the DA and glutamate hypotheses. The DA and glutamate hypothe-ses were originally considered to be competing ideas. However, as the body of liter-ature on SZ grows, and our understanding of the complexity and heterogeneity of the disorder develops, it has become clear that the answer to the question regarding the cause of SZ will not be a single hypothesis. Indeed, the glutamate and DA sys-tems are intricately linked and their interaction with each other has been well de-scribed in the context of SZ (Carlsson and Carlsson, 1990; Konradi and Heckers, 2003; Laruelle et al, 2003). More comprehensive hypotheses of SZ consider the in-teraction between the two neurochemical systems. Specifically, within the NAcc, a hypothesis by Grace and colleagues has emerged to explain how the interaction be-tween glutamate and DA in the region could lead to symptoms of SZ (Lodge and Grace, 2011). This hypothesis is based on an animal model of SZ that utilizes a de-21 

velopmental disruption with administration of mitotoxin methylazoxymethanol ace-tate (MAM). Administration of MAM during gestation produces a reduction in GA-BAergic interneurons in the hippocampus of the adult rats, resulting in hyperactivity of glutamatergic neurons in the ventral subiculum (Lodge and Grace, 2008). The in-creased excitation of the ventral subiculum in this model results in a hyperrespon-sive DA system in the NAcc which has been hypothesized to lead to symptoms of SZ (Lodge et al, 2009; Lodge and Grace, 2011; Boley et al, 2014). 

While this hypothesis is promising, it is primarily based on electrophysiolog-ical studies in animal models. There is evidence to support a reduction in interneu-rons (Zhang and Reynolds, 2002; Knable et al, 2004; Benes et al, 2007; Konradi et al, 2011; Wang et al, 2011) and a hyperactive state (Malaspina et al, 1999; Medoff et al, 1999; Schobel et al, 2009) of the hippocampus in SZ. However, there is little conclu-sive evidence for abnormal circuitry in the NAcc, or that DA is indeed elevated in this region in SZ. To move forward with a better understanding of the pathophysio-logical mechanisms of SZ, a more fundamental understanding of the deficits and ab-normalities in each of the neurotransmitter systems in the must be achieved. Thus, studying both the glutamatergic and dopaminergic systems in the NAcc will be nec-essary to elucidating the pathological mechanism of SZ. 

Use of Animal Models in Schizophrenia Research 

Animal models are widely used in SZ research and provide a useful tool for studying specific aspects of the disorder. These models typically fall into one of sev-eral categories for SZ research: genetic, pharmacological, or developmental (lesion 22 

and environmental). While a discussion of each of these models is outside the scope of this document (for reviews, see Lipska and Weinberger, 2000; Nestler and Hy-man, 2010; Fernando and Robbins, 2011; Jones et al, 2011), pharmacological animal models for studying the effects of chronic APD treatment in the brain are particular-ly relevant. APD treatment poses a major difficulty when studying postmortem hu-man tissue in SZ since most patients have been chronically medicated with APDs at their time of death. Studying patients before they start taking medications is not fea-sible for postmortem studies, and patients who were off medication at the time of their death are rare. The use of a pharmacological animal model allows precise con-trol over drug treatment to directly study the effects APDs may have on different measures in the brain. 

Many studies have investigated the effects of chronic APD treatment on DA levels in the striatum and NAcc, and have found either decreases (Rastogi et al, 1982; Blaha and Lane, 1987; Ichikawa and Meltzer, 1991; Chen et al, 1991; Marche-se et al, 2002; Tejedor-Real et al, 2003) or no change (Tsutsumi et al, 1982; Ichikawa and Meltzer, 1991; Patterson and Schenk, 1991; Yamamoto and Cooperman, 1994; Invernizzi et al, 1995; Marchese et al, 2002; Roberts et al, 2002) in various DA markers. Inconsistencies in the literature could arise from the use of different routes of administration and different doses of drugs used. Kapur and colleagues (2000, 2003) have highlighted that APD doses commonly used in the literature had D2 re-ceptor occupancy levels that did not align with the clinically relevant occupancy for treatment in patients. Further, the doses did not maintain receptor occupancy be-tween repeated treatments due to a much shorter half-life of the drugs in rats (Ka-23 

pur et al, 2000, 2003). The authors suggest these issues could have confounded re-sults in past studies and led to the discrepancies seen throughout the literature. One technique that has been established to achieve steady state D2 receptor occupancy levels that are clinically relevant is administration through drinking water (Perez-Costas et al, 2008). This administration technique provides many benefits over oth-er commonly used methods, including decreased stress associated with treatment, accurate modeling of administration for patients, and provides more stable dosing in the rat than is achieved with other methods (Perez-Costas et al, 2008). Although this is a validated and widely used method, few studies investigating APD effects on the DA system in the striatum have used the technique (Ichikawa and Meltzer, 1991; Patterson and Schenk, 1991; Sakai et al, 2001; Roberts et al, 2002), and only one in the NAcc (Ichikawa and Meltzer, 1991). 

In addition to the issues with drug doses, chronic APD studies typically use a 3 week treatment paradigm. Studies in our laboratory and others however, have found vastly different results after 3 weeks (Uranova et al, 1991; Kerns et al, 1992) and 6 months (Roberts et al, 1995; Kelley et al, 1997). The further changes that oc-cur after 3 weeks of treatment indicate that this time point may not be accurately representing the effects of chronic treatment, and therefore may not be suitable for use of modeling chronic treatment in SZ patients which typically lasts for a majority of their lives. Thus, with the confounding issues of APD dose, administration, and length of treatment, it is still not clear what effects APDs have long-term on the DA system in the NAcc. There is a need to determine the effect when administered chronically at doses that are verified to be clinically relevant. 24 

Summary and Goals of Dissertation Research 

It is evident that gross structural deficits are not the primary cause of SZ, but that the underlying pathology more likely resides at a finer level, in brain circuitry and synaptic connections. Electron microscopy (EM) is the only way to study anat-omy at this level, but it is rarely done in human postmortem brain outside of our la-boratory. 

Despite the support for the role of the NAcc in SZ and its treatment, the neu-ropathology of the NAcc has largely been overlooked in SZ research. Only a handful of postmortem studies have been done to analyze DA levels in the NAcc of SZ sub-jects directly; these studies found conflicting results, and none have analyzed gluta-mate levels. Furthermore, these studies have not been replicated in the past 30 years with improved techniques and more standard boundaries of the NAcc, and with consideration of APD effects. Lastly, the few anatomical studies in the NAcc of SZ subjects have not investigated its distinct subregions, the core and shell, which are structurally, neurochemically, and functionally distinguishable. 

The goal of the research in this dissertation was to provide an examination of the DA and glutamate systems in the NAcc in SZ, as well as an examination of the circuitry in the NAcc core and shell. This was achieved by: 

1) Determining if subjects with SZ have increased dopaminergic input to the NAcc core and shell compared to normal control cases, by measuring the density of immunolabeling of TH with optical densitometry, and measur-ing protein levels using western blot assays. 25 

2) Analyzing the effects of chronic APD treatment on the DA synthesis path-way in the NAcc, by measuring the density of TH immunolabeling in ro-dents that have been chronically treated with APDs. 

3) Determining if the NAcc receives abnormal glutamatergic input in SZ by analyzing vesicular glutamate transporters with western blot assays. 

4) Determining if subjects with SZ have abnormal synaptic input to the NAcc core and shell, by quantifying the density of synapse types using stereo-logical analysis at the EM level. 26

Abstract 

The cause of schizophrenia (SZ) is unknown and no single region of the brain can be pinpointed as an area of primary pathology. Rather, SZ results from dysfunc-tion of multiple neurotransmitter systems and miswiring between brain regions. It is necessary to elucidate how communication between regions is disrupted to ad-vance our understanding of SZ pathology. The nucleus accumbens (NAcc) is a prime region of interest, where inputs from numerous brain areas altered in SZ are inte-grated. Aberrant signaling in the NAcc is hypothesized to cause symptoms of SZ, but it is unknown if these abnormalities are actually present. Electron microscopy was used to study the morphology of synaptic connections in SZ. The NAcc core and shell of 6 SZ subjects and 8 matched controls were compared in this pilot study. SZ sub-jects had a 19% increase in the density of asymmetric axospinous synapses (charac-teristic of excitatory inputs) in the core, but not the shell. Both groups had similar densities of symmetric synapses (characteristic of inhibitory inputs). The postsyn-aptic densities of asymmetric synapses had 22% smaller areas in the core, but not the shell. These results indicate that the core receives increased excitatory input in SZ, potentially leading to dysfunctional dopamine neurotransmission and cortico-striatal-thalamic stimulus processing. The reduced postsynaptic density size of asymmetric synapses suggests impaired signaling at these synapses. These findings enhance our understanding of the role the NAcc might play in SZ and the interaction of glutamatergic and dopaminergic abnormalities in SZ. 28 

Introduction 

The exact pathophysiology for the origin of schizophrenia (SZ) is unknown, however evidence from decades of research implies that interactions between mul-tiple brain regions and multiple neurotransmitter systems are likely at play. One re-gion that is implicated in SZ pathology is the nucleus accumbens (NAcc). The NAcc integrates signaling from multiple regions of the brain, receiving input from areas including the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, amygdala, thalamus, and midbrain. 1Importantly, all of these areas have been associated with SZ, making the NAcc a prime region for integrating multiple disrupted areas to provide a comprehensive understanding of SZ pathology. 2 Reciprocal connections between the NAcc and sub-stantia nigra/ventral tegmental area (SN/VTA) indicate further importance of this region. Via these connections, the NAcc modulates dopaminergic input to the dorsal striatum, 3 and striatal dopamine (DA) dysfunction is a hallmark characteristic of the disorder. 4 Though many studies have implicated the NAcc in SZ, none have been able to describe its circuitry. 

The purpose of this study was to provide the first ultrastructural analysis in postmortem human NAcc, and examine the neurocircuitry in the NAcc in SZ to es-tablish the role this region may play in the disorder. We used stereological analysis of electron micrographs in postmortem SZ to analyze the organization and composi-tion of synapse types in the NAcc. 29

Striatal tissue was then sectioned at a thickness of 40 μm with a Vibratome into six free-floating series.

Micro-graphs were cropped and adjusted for brightness and contrast for presentation in the figures. Criteria for distinguishing a synapse were the presence of (1) parallel pre- and postsynaptic membranes, (2) a postsynaptic density (PSD), and (3) synap-tic vesicles at the membrane in the presynaptic terminal. Overview of Human NAcc Ultrastructure 

The ultrastructure of the human NAcc was similar to that described in non-human primates and rodents. 14–19 The elaborate multi-perforated synapses, howev-er, have not been reported in other species or in other regions of the human brain. Increased spine volume, synapse length, and perforations are all associated with in-creased synaptic activity. 20–22 Thus, these synapses could be a manifestation of the complex interconnectivity of the NAcc with many other brain regions. Since they were present in both groups they seem to be a unique characteristic of the human NAcc, independent of the disease state. 38 

Elevated Excitatory Input in SZ 

The increase in AS synapses indicates an increase in glutamatergic-type input to the region. This is consistent with findings in the dorsal striatum which also found an increase in AS synapses in SZ. 6 In agreement with this finding is evidence for an increased expression of the excitatory PSD protein SAP90/PSD-95-associated protein 1 (SAPAP1) in the NAcc in postmortem SZ. 23 Rodent models of SZ also exhib-it increased indices of glutamatergic-type input in the NAcc; rats treated with psy-chotomimetics have increased mRNA expression of excitatory PSD genes, 23,24 and increased dendritic spine density in the NAcc, 25–27 which is tightly correlated with the density of AS synapses. 28 A likely source of this elevated input in SZ and its functional implications are discussed below. 

Reduced PSD Size 

Our findings indicate a reduction in the PSD size of excitatory synapses in SZ. The PSD consists of many different proteins for scaffolding and signaling at the syn-apse. 29 The size and composition of PSDs are dynamic, changing with activity of the synapse 13,30,31 and are proportional to the area of the spine head. 32–35 

A reduction in the size of asymmetric PSDs could indicate abnormalities in membrane receptors or signaling molecules of glutamatergic synapses. Several studies have investigated glutamatergic receptors in the NAcc in SZ, and have con-sistently found no difference in mGluR, 36,37 NMDA, 36,38,39 AMPA, 36,39–41 or kainate 36,39 receptor expression and binding. Thus, it is unlikely that the reduced PSD size found in this study is due to a reduction in glutamate receptor expression. Very few stud-39 

ies have investigated signaling molecules of glutamatergic synapses in the NAcc, 23,42 and do not report conclusive findings to explain the reduced PSD size found in the present study. 

It is possible that the reduced PSD size is a consequence of overstimulation from the excessive glutamatergic afferents also found in this study. If elevated glu-tamatergic input to the NAcc is indeed involved in the pathophysiology of SZ, it is possible that this excessive excitation could result in the degeneration or reorgani-zation of synaptic structure over time. While no morphological signs were observed in the tissue that indicated excitotoxic degeneration, many PSD proteins are in-volved in the mechanism of excitotoxicity, 43 and this could provide an explanation for the reduced PSD size at excitatory synapses reported here. 

Similar Inhibitory Input in SZ 

A similar density of symmetric synapses (axodendritic and axospinous) was found in the control and SZ groups. Within the striatum, symmetric synapses are formed by interneurons and dopaminergic projections from the SN/VTA. DA inputs form symmetric synapses on both dendrites and spines. 15,44 Thus, the similar densi-ty of these synapses suggests that the NAcc does not have increased inhibitory input from intrinsic interneurons, nor does it receive increased dopaminergic input from the SN/VTA in SZ. While this suggests that dopaminergic input to the region is nor-mal in SZ, it remains possible that DA is being abnormally produced in the same number of axon terminals, or that abnormal modulation by glutamatergic afferents could result in the dysregulation of its release, as discussed below. 40 

Similar Morphological State of Mitochondria 

The structural integrity of the mitochondria in the NAcc does not indicate drastic abnormalities in metabolic demand of the region in SZ. This finding contrasts previous ultrastructural studies in the dorsal striatum which found differences in the number and distribution of mitochondria in SZ subjects compared to controls. 45– 48 Few studies have considered the structure and function of mitochondria in the NAcc in SZ. 49–52 Differential alterations in the basal ganglia have been reported in SZ, including increased mitochondrial activity in the NAcc of paranoid SZ subjects that was attributed to APD effects. 51 Despite the evidence for mitochondrial pathology in dorsal striatum in SZ, our structural findings do not indicate this pathology is pre-sent in the NAcc. 

Differences in NAcc Core and Shell 

An interesting finding to emerge in this study was that group differences were primarily found in the core. The shell of control and SZ subjects was similar across nearly all measures. The afferent and efferent connections of the core and shell regions have been identified in rats and non-human primates. 1,53,54 While many of the connections overlap, there are distinct differences that lead to different func-tional roles of the two subregions. 1,55 Studies in animal models have found the re-gions respond differently to APD, and have often concluded that the shell must play a larger role in SZ due to the strength of APD action here compared to the core. 56–58 It is interesting then that our findings are sequestered to the core. The afferent and efferent connections of the core parallel those of the dorsal striatum, while the shell 41 

has been considered a transition zone between the striatum and the extended amygdala. 59–61 Thus, our findings localized to the core further support the redefined hypothesis for the role of the associative striatum, rather than the limbic striatum, in SZ. 62 

Evaluating the Role of the NAcc in SZ 

The NAcc has long been implicated in the etiology of SZ, and is often assumed to be the locus of elevated striatal DA characteristic of SZ. This assumption was typi-cally founded on evidence for antipsychotic action in the NAcc, 56–58,63 as well as its functional role in the brain which provided a logical link between the pathology and symptomatology of SZ. Recent imaging studies however, have found the associative striatum, not the limbic striatum, to be the locus of elevated striatal DA. 64,65 The morphological findings in postmortem tissue from the present study support the imaging findings by suggesting that the NAcc does not receive elevated dopaminer-gic input. 

While this is an interesting development in understanding the critical role that striatal DA plays in both the pathology and treatment of SZ, it does not exclude the NAcc from the equation of SZ etiology. The evidence for elevated glutamatergic-type input to the NAcc in the present study indicates abnormal signaling in the re-gion. The findings reported here are highly consistent with the hypothesis for exces-sive glutamatergic input to the NAcc driving symptoms of SZ. 2,66–69 

The NAcc receives glutamatergic input from the cortex, thalamus, hippocam-pus, and amygdala. Afferent connections from each of these regions have similar ul-42 

trastructural morphology in the NAcc, typically forming AS synapses. 70 Thus, the el-evated input seen in SZ could be arising from any of these regions. A possible source for the increased input is the hippocampus. There is evidence for a reduction in GA-BAergic interneurons in the hippocampus in SZ 71–74 as well as an increase in hippo-campal activity at rest. 75,76 In this context, one might expect to see elevated input in the shell rather than the core since the shell receives denser hippocampal input than the core. However, anterograde tracing studies show that the hippocampus projects to all of the NAcc in a topographical manner, 77,78 so it is possible that the increased input results from abnormalities of a specific hippocampal subregion projecting to the core. 

Excessive excitatory input to the NAcc would result in significant conse-quences for the flow of information through this region. DA is released in the NAcc upon stimulation of the ventral subiculum of the hippocampus, 79–81 and increased activity in the ventral hippocampus drives DA dysfunction in an animal model of SZ. 69 Further, glutamatergic input from the hippocampus and amygdala provide a gating mechanism for information flow through the NAcc; input from either of these regions is required for signals from the prefrontal cortex to continue its flow through the cortico-striato-thalamic loop. 82 Thus, elevated glutamatergic input to the NAcc could result in increased dopaminergic release and/or a hyperresponsive system. 2

These hypotheses implicate glutamatergic dysregulation of NAcc in positive symptoms of schizophrenia, however it is also well established that the NAcc is involved in reward and motivation, the loss of which constitute some of the negative 43 

symptoms of SZ. It has been hypothesized that a loss of gating in the NAcc, possibly due to elevated glutamatergic input and subsequent DA dysregulation, could result in increased signal noise in the region; this would effectively diminish the response to stimuli otherwise eliciting reward or motivated behavior. 83,84 The elevated glu-tamatergic-type input and similar density of dopaminergic-type input provide the first structural data in the NAcc of SZ subjects to support the hypothesis that exces-sive hippocampal input to the NAcc could be a pathological mechanism of SZ. 2,66–69 

Limitations 

While the functional consequences of these structural findings can only be speculated, this study provides vital insight for understanding the structural pathol-ogy that leads to symptoms in SZ. Forming a link between pathology of the neurocir-cuitry in SZ and the functional outcomes will be a powerful tool for moving forward in our understanding of the cause, as well as improving treatment for SZ. 

The small sample size should be taken into consideration with the interpreta-tion of these findings. With the heterogeneity of SZ, it is likely that the cohort ana-lyzed for this study does not represent all aspects of the disorder. However, the re-sults agree with studies of synaptic density in the dorsal striatum in SZ 6 and are consistent with hypotheses of SZ. Postmortem SZ subjects suitable for electron mi-croscopy are rare, so the cohort collected for this study, although small, provides a unique analysis of postmortem SZ. 

All but one of the subjects in the present study were chronically medicated at the time of death, thus it is possible that our results are due to APD treatment. Ultra-44 

structural studies of APD effects in the striatum primarily focus on the dorsal stria-tum, where chronic (6 months or longer) haloperidol treatment results in reduced asymmetric synapse density 85,86 and reduced spine number. 87 Other studies of APD treatment have reported an increased percentage of perforated synapses in the cau-date with no change in total number, and no differences present in the NAcc. 88–90 These studies do not indicate our results are due to APD treatment. Past studies also indicate that APDs increase striatal volume; 91–95 thus, our finding of increased AS synaptic density is likely not an effect of medication, since a larger volume would reduce synaptic density rather than increase it. Rather, it is possible that an in-creased striatal volume in SZ subjects could result in false negatives or dampen measured increases in density in our study. 

Stereological studies provide the advantage of estimating total object num-ber and thus avoiding volume discrepancies. The estimation of total number, how-ever, requires distinct regional boundaries, and it is well established that no distinct boundary exists between the NAcc core and ventral caudate/putamen regions. 54,60,96–98 Thus, an analysis of total object number would have required in-troducing arbitrary and nonreplicable region boundaries due to the variability be-tween human brains. This issue was addressed by using unbiased stereology to measure object density paired with measuring striatal volume. The striatal volumes did not significantly differ between the two groups which further confirms that our findings are not due to volume differences. 45 

Conclusions 

This study provides not only the first ultrastructural quantitative analysis performed in human NAcc, but also an analysis of the NAcc ultrastructure in SZ. Our findings indicate miswiring of the neurocircuitry in the NAcc core in SZ, specifically an increase in glutamatergic-type input. We argue that this is unique to the etiology of the disorder and may provide a morphological link between glutamatergic pa-thology and striatal DA pathology in SZ.

UNCOVERING THE ROLE OF THE NUCLEUS ACCUMBENS IN SCHIZOPHRENIA: A POSTMORTEM ANALYSIS OF TYROSINE HYDROXYLASE AND VESICULAR GLUTAMATE TRANSPORTERS 

by 

LESLEY A. MCCOLLUM, ROBERT E. MCCULLUMSMITH, ROSALINDA C. ROBERTS 

In preparation for Schizophrenia Bulletin 

Format adapted for dissertation 56 

Abstract 

The nucleus accumbens (NAcc) is often implicated in schizophrenia patholo-gy, but with little evidence to support its role. The purpose of this study was to ex-amine the neurochemical profile of the NAcc in postmortem human tissue, and de-termine if abnormalities are present in the dopaminergic or glutamatergic systems in this region in schizophrenia. We compared: 1) the immunohistochemical localiza-tion of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH), the rate-limiting synthesizing enzyme of dopa-mine, in 6 SZ subjects and 7 demographically matched controls, and 2) protein lev-els of TH and vesicular glutamate transporters vGLUT1 and vGLUT2 in 6 schizo-phrenia on-drug (SZ-ON), 7 schizophrenia off-drug (SZ-OFF), and 7 matched control subjects. To study the effects of chronic antipsychotic drug (APD) treatment on TH levels in the NAcc, rats treated for 6 months via drinking water with either haloperidol or olanzapine were analyzed with immunohistochemistry. Compared to control subjects, TH protein levels and immunolabeling were similar in the NAcc of SZ subjects. TH protein levels did not differ between groups when schizophrenia cases were separated by APD-treatment status (ON- vs. OFF-drug). APD-treated rats had similar TH optical density levels as vehicle-treated rats. Protein levels of vGLUT1 were similar in the NAcc of control and SZ subjects, and there was no dif-ference in vGLUT1 protein levels between SZ-ON and SZ-OFF subjects. In contrast, vGLUT2 protein levels were significantly elevated in SZ (25% increase, p = 0.006). Protein levels of vGLUT2 did not differ between SZ-ON and SZ-OFF subjects. The similar levels of TH suggest that the NAcc may not be a region of elevated dopamine levels in the disorder. Elevated levels of vGLUT2 suggest the NAcc receives in-57 

creased glutamatergic input from subcortical origins in schizophrenia. The lack of effect with APD-treated rats or between the SZ-ON and SZ-OFF groups indicates that our results are not due to medication effects. These findings agree with recent imag-ing studies in schizophrenia patients, and challenge previously held assumptions regarding the role of the NAcc in the dopamine hypothesis of SZ. 58 

Introduction 

Schizophrenia (SZ) has been described as one of the most complex and dev-astating of mental illnesses. Affecting approximately 1% of the population world-wide, it is characterized by psychosis, cognitive impairments, and negative symp-toms such as blunted emotion and loss of motivation. Unfortunately, treatment op-tions are limited to antipsychotic medications which only treat psychotic symptoms, have serious side effects, and are ineffective for many patients. Although its cause remains elusive, decades of research have revealed common pathologies in SZ, some of which are now hallmarks of the disorder. 

One of these hallmarks is abnormalities in the dopamine (DA) system in the striatum of patients with SZ. 1 Of special interest is a subregion of the ventral stria-tum, the nucleus accumbens (NAcc), which has been assumed to be a prime location for the elevated DA levels in SZ, based on its functional properties and evidence of antipsychotic drug action here. 2–5 Until recently, imaging studies have had to ana-lyze the striatum as a single region, without the ability to distinguish between these key areas. Thus, not only has the role of the NAcc has never been confirmed, but re-cent studies with improved imaging techniques suggest that this subregion may not be implicated as previously thought. 6–8 Additionally, conclusions surrounding the DA abnormalities in the striatum lack solid support from postmortem studies, which offer the ability to study these subregions individually. Previous studies in postmor-tem NAcc were conducted before the introduction of improved tissue preservation techniques and established boundaries for striatal regions, leading to conflicting re-sults. 9–14 59 

Another, more recent hypothesis of SZ is a causal role of glutamate abnor-malities. Glutamate was first implicated with the finding that NMDA antagonists, such as phencyclidine and ketamine, produce SZ-like symptoms. 15–17 Within the striatum, a hypothesis emerged that pathology of the glutamate system could drive striatal DA dysfunction. 18,19 Preclinical studies 20 and imaging studies in healthy vol-unteers 21 have demonstrated the ability of NMDA antagonists that mimic SZ to en-hance DA efflux in the striatum. And stimulation of glutamatergic input to the NAcc of rats causes elevated DA release in the region. It is not known however, if glutama-tergic abnormalities are actually present in the NAcc in SZ. 

The purpose of the present study was to determine if neurochemical abnor-malities are present in the NAcc of postmortem SZ. To study the dopaminergic sys-tem, we analyzed tyrosine hydroxylase (TH), the rate-limiting synthesizing enzyme of DA, using immunohistochemistry and western blot analysis. To study the effects of antipsychotic drugs (APDs) on TH levels in the NAcc, rats were chronically treat-ed with either haloperidol or olanzapine, and analyzed using immunohistochemis-try. To study the glutamatergic system in postmortem SZ, we analyzed the vesicular glutamate transporters vGLUT1 and vGLUT2 which are essential for the uptake and storage of glutamate into synaptic vesicles. They have complementary localization patterns in cortical and subcortical structures, respectively, 31–33 providing infor-mation on the source of the glutamatergic afferents. The western blot analyses were performed in SZ subjects that were on medication at the time of death, and in SZ subjects that were off medication at the time of death. 60 

Methods 

Postmortem Tissue 

Postmortem human brain tissue was obtained from the Maryland Brain Col-lection. DSM-IV diagnosis of SZ was confirmed by two psychiatrists based on patient medical records, family interviews, autopsy reports, and neuropathologic assess-ments. Schizophrenia subjects were considered off-drug if they had been untreated with APDs for at least 6 months prior to death. Control cases had no history of psy-chiatric or neurological disease. Pairs of control and SZ cases were chosen based on the best match of age, race, sex, postmortem interval (PMI), and tissue pH level. 

Two separate cohorts of postmortem tissue were used for two different experi-ments (Table 1). There was no overlap between cases for the two different experi-ments. Animal Tissue 

The SZ cases used for studying the optical density of TH immunohistochemi-cal labeling were all chronically medicated with APDs. Thus, to assess the potential effect of chronic APD treatment on TH immunolabeling in the NAcc, rats were chronically treated with APDs and also processed with immunohistochemistry. Tis-sue from thirty-six male Sprague-Dawley rats was used for this study. The animals were housed and treated at the University of Alabama at Birmingham under IACUC 62 

approval. Three groups of 12 animals each were administered either vehicle (VEH), haloperidol (HAL; 2mg/kg/day), or olanzapine (OLANZ; 6mg/kg/day) orally via drinking water for 6 months. The dosage and administration route has been shown to achieve steady state D2 receptor occupancy levels that are within the therapeuti-cally relevant range. 22 The drugs were dissolved in glacial acetic acid in distilled wa-ter and adjusted for pH. The VEH group received water with glacial acetic acid only, adjusted for pH. Animals were weighed weekly and water consumption was moni-tored to adjust drug dosages as needed. At the time of euthanasia, rats were given a lethal dose of anesthesia (ketamine and xylazine) by IP injection. When the rats were deeply anesthetized, cardiac blood was drawn, followed by intracardiac perfu-sion fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, 0.1% glutaraldehyde in 0.1M PB. The tis-sue was sectioned in 6 series of 40 μm thickness on a vibratome. During the tissue collection and processing, the NAcc of some animals did not remain intact so they were excluded from the study, resulting in the final group sizes: VEH ( n = 11), HAL (n = 11), and OLANZ ( n = 10).

Analysis 

The density of immunohistochemical labeling of human and rat striatum was assessed using optical densitometry. Using the calbindin-stained sections as a pat-tern (mask) for delimiting the boundaries of the core and shell of the NAcc, regions of interest for analysis in adjacent TH-stained sections were determined. Each sec-tion was digitized on a light box with a digital camera and analyzed using NIH Im-ageJ software to determine the grayscale optical density of TH immunoreactivity the NAcc core and shell. Three sampling areas per subregion were placed using a grid and avoiding boundaries; mean gray values for each section were calculated by av-eraging measurements from the sampling areas. This was repeated and averaged across three sections per case to obtain regional mean gray values for each case. 65 

Western Blotting 

Protein extraction. Protein extracts from human tissue were obtained as de-scribed previously. 23 Briefly, one series of tissue was sonicated in a cold lysis buffer (1:5 w:v) containing 50mM Tris (pH 8.0), 5 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 1% SDS, and 10 μl/ml of Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Sigma; P8340). Tissue homogenate was cen-trifuged at 13,500 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. Total protein concentration of the result-ing supernatant was measured using a modified Lowry technique (Bio-Rad; 500-0113, 500-0114). 

Gel electrophoresis and western blotting. Western blot analysis was used to measure protein levels of TH, vGLUT1, and vGLUT2 (Table 2). The gel electrophore-sis and western blot assays were performed as described previously 23 with one var-iation; samples used for vGLUT assays were not heated since boiling causes aggrega-tion of the protein. Samples of 60 μg of total protein were resolved by sodium do-decyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis on 4–20% polyacrylamide gradient gels (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland; 58505), then transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes for 21 h at 4°C. One set of membranes was blotted for TH. The separate set of membranes with non-boiled samples was blotted for vGLUT2, stripped and reblotted for vGLUT1. Final reblots for actin were performed for all ex-periments as a control for tissue preservation and protein loading. All three groups were run together in the same experiment which required simultaneous incubation and development of two western blot membranes. The experiments were per-formed in duplicate. Preadsorption with the vGLUT1 peptide completely eliminated the signal. 24 66 

Analysis. Films were scanned using a flatbed scanner and optical densities of the bands were measured using NIH ImageJ. An optical density standard curve was created using a step calibration tablet (Stouffer Industries Inc., Mishawaka, IN, USA; T2120, series #130501), and each measurement was calibrated to the standard curve. A background subtraction in ImageJ was performed for each film. Calibrated optical density was measured using the ImageJ rectangle tool to outline each band. 

Statistical Analyses 

For protein measures, TH and vGLUT values were normalized to actin. The normalized values for each duplicate were then averaged for the analysis. All data sets were assessed for normality with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. A Grubb’s out-lier analysis was used to identify potential outliers in each data set. Outliers were excluded only in situations where methodological considerations warranted exclu-sion. Presence of outliers, their in/exclusion, and the impact on the results is noted in the Results section. For the human immunohistochemistry experiments, the cor-responding unpaired parametric ( t-test) or nonparametric (Wilcoxon) tests were performed. Welch’s correction was used in cases of unequal variance. Animal exper-iments and western blot experiments were analyzed with One-Way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests. Core-shell comparisons were analyzed with paired tests. All statistical tests were two-tailed with significance of p < 0.05. 67

Discussion 

In this study we found that TH levels did not differ between control and SZ groups in the NAcc. There were no differences in TH between the rat treatment 71 

groups or in TH protein levels between the SZ-ON and SZ-OFF SZ subjects. vGLUT2 protein levels were significantly increased in SZ subjects compared to controls, whereas levels of vGLUT1 were similar. There were no differences in vGLUT1 or vGLUT2 protein measures between the SZ-ON and SZ-OFF subjects. To our knowledge, this is the first study to analyze TH and vGLUT protein levels in post-mortem NAcc in on-drug and off-drug SZ subjects. Further, it is the first study to as-sess chronic (6 month) APD effects on TH immunohistochemistry in the rat NAcc core and shell. 

TH Levels in NAcc 

The results from the immunohistochemistry and western blot experiments suggest that TH levels do not differ in the NAcc of SZ subjects compared to controls. Since TH is the rate-limiting enzyme in DA synthesis and correlates well with DA in the striatum, 25,26 these findings suggest that DA synthesis is normal in the NAcc in SZ. Previous studies of DA in postmortem SZ tissue primarily reported similar find-ings the NAcc, 9,10,12,14 though not all. 11,13 Our finding is also consistent with neu-roimaging studies in patients that do not detect differences in presynaptic DA syn-thesis capacity in the ventral striatum. 6–8 Additionally, our recent ultrastructural study in postmortem human tissue reported similar densities of symmetric synap-ses, the type dopaminergic input forms to the region, in the NAcc core and shell of SZ and control subjects. 27 

It does not appear that APD treatment confounded or masked our results. First, the SZ-ON and SZ-OFF groups had similar TH protein levels. Second, chronic 72 

APD treatment of rats did not significantly alter TH levels in the core or shell. An-other study in rats also found no difference in TH in the core or shell after haloperi-dol treatment. 28 In contrast, a rat study with clozapine treatment found decreased TH protein levels in the NAcc. 29 Conflicting results between studies could be from differences in drug type, administration method, or length of treatment. Kapur and colleagues 30 studied various APD treatment methods and found that daily injection does not achieve stable D2 receptor occupancy levels. Further, many doses used in past studies do not achieve D2 receptor occupancy levels that are consistent with therapeutic ranges in patients. 30 Tejedor-Real and colleagues 29 treated rats with daily injections of clozapine, a method that Kapur and colleagues 30 concluded was not able to achieve clinically comparable effects. Serum levels were not analyzed in the present study, but our treatment paradigm was based on doses and administra-tion route that achieve steady state receptor occupancy within the therapeutic range. 22 

Vesicular Glutamate Transporter Levels in NAcc 

Protein levels of vGLUT2 were elevated in the SZ group compared to controls but vGLUT1 levels were similar between groups. vGLUT1 and vGLUT2 comprise largely complementary localization patterns in the brain. vGLUT1 is found at highest concentrations in glutamatergic neurons of the cortex and hippocampus, while vGLUT2 is most concentrated in subcortical structures such as the thalamus, amyg-dala, and brainstem. 31–33 Within some regions however, both forms are expressed. Though vGLUT1 dominates in these regions, vGLUT2 is also present in some layers 73 

of the neocortex, cingulate cortex, and regions of the hippocampus. 34 Within the NAcc, vGLUT2 positive glutamatergic input could be coming from the thalamus, amygdala, cingulate cortex, or subiculum of the hippocampus. The synaptic inputs from all these regions make a morphologically similar type of synapse, 35 which we recently reported to be increased in density the NAcc in SZ. 27 There is little evidence that indicates the thalamus or amygdala have excessive glutamatergic activity in SZ. A likely option is the hippocampus which sends dense projections to the NAcc. 36,37 Previous studies of the hippocampus report fewer GABAergic interneurons in SZ, 38– 41 and elevated measures of glutamate metabolites in unmedicated patients with SZ. 42,43 Functional implications are discussed below. 

Similar levels between the SZ-ON and SZ-OFF groups do not indicate that APD effects are impacting our results. Tissue from the APD treated rats in this study were not processed for vGLUT1 or vGLUT2 immunohistochemistry, and no other studies have investigated the effects of APD treatment on protein levels of these transporters in the NAcc. 

Understanding the Role of the NAcc in SZ 

Though the findings reported here are static in nature, it is worth consider-ing their potential functional implications in SZ. Complex processing of cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical signals occurs within the NAcc. Glutamatergic input from the amygdala and hippocampus provide affective valence and contextual gating, re-spectively, by facilitating the probability of cortical input to evoke an action poten-tial in the NAcc. 44,45 Excessive glutamatergic input from either of these regions 74 

would result in a loss of the gating mechanism, allowing otherwise weak cortical in-put to elicit a response in the NAcc. 44 Many hypotheses of hippocampal dysfunction in SZ focus on hippocampal modulation of DA release in the NAcc. 46–49 To support this hypothesis, one would expect to find increased glutamatergic input to DA ter-minals. However, a recent ultrastructural study in postmortem SZ found elevated excitatory input on dendritic spines in the NAcc, 27 suggesting that increased glu-tamatergic input to the NAcc is modulating excitability of NAcc neurons, rather than presynaptic modulation of DA release in the region. Loss of gating due to enhanced input to NAcc neurons could result in hyperexcitability of these neurons, making them more likely to respond to otherwise weak stimuli. 44 Though other hypotheses focus on DA dysregulation, the excessive glutamatergic input to the NAcc reported here supports hypotheses suggesting that disruption of the brain’s ability to effec-tively gate information flow through the NAcc could lead to some of the symptoms of SZ. 46–49 

Additionally, elevated glutamatergic input to the NAcc could enhance GA-BAergic drive to the midbrain. Dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and ventral tegmental area (SN/VTA) are inhibited by GABAergic interneurons in the midbrain. 50–52 NAcc neurons project to the GABAergic interneurons of the SN/VTA, 53,54 thus stimulation of this pathway would disinhibit midbrain DA neurons. 54,55 Due to the feedforward organization of striatonigrostriatal pathways, increased ex-citation of NAcc neurons could drive enhanced DA release in the dorsal striatum. 56,57 This could be a source of elevated DA levels in the dorsal striatum in SZ. 6–8 75 

Methodological Considerations 

The results of this study should be interpreted in the context of their limita-tions. First, the NAcc is a complex and heterogeneous region; subregions referred to as the core and shell have been identified across species 58–60 and further heteroge-neity exists within each subregion. A recent ultrastructural study in postmortem SZ found an increased density of excitatory type synapses only in the core of the NAcc, not the shell. 27 Since the western blot experiments in this study were performed on the NAcc as a whole, it is possible that unique variations within the NAcc territories have been missed. 

Second, the small sample sizes of these groups should be taken into consid-eration. SZ is a heterogeneous disorder, and there is evidence that additional charac-teristics, such as severity of positive versus negative symptoms 6 and treatment re-sponse, 61,62 can affect DA levels in the striatum. While one should be cautious to generalize these findings to all of the SZ population, our findings are consistent with imaging and postmortem studies in the NAcc, as well as hypotheses of SZ discussed above. Postmortem off-drug SZ subjects are rare, so the cohort collected for this study, although small, provides a unique analysis of postmortem SZ. 

Conclusions 

Our findings suggest DA synthesis is normal in the NAcc in SZ, but that the region receives increased glutamatergic input. They further indicate that these find-ings are not due to antipsychotic medication. This study provides insight into the 76 

role of the NAcc in SZ, highlighting potential abnormalities in interaction between the dopaminergic and glutamatergic systems in the region.

DISCUSSION 

Summary of Dissertation Goals and Reported Findings 

The goal of this dissertation work was to examine the neurocircuitry and neurochemical profile of the nucleus accumbens (NAcc) in schizophrenia (SZ). The striatum has been a brain region of interest in SZ for decades. However, it is a large subcortical structure consisting of several distinct regions, each of which has unique structural, neurochemical, and functional properties. Past research indicates the striatum is an area of pathology in SZ, a locus for antipsychotic drug action, and a source of side effects of antipsychotic drugs as well (see Regions of action in the In-troduction). Elevated striatal dopamine (DA) is a hallmark of the disorder (Miyake et al, 2011), and the ventral striatum, specifically the nucleus accumbens, has been assumed to be a region of these DA abnormalities based on evidence for antipsy-chotic drug action here (Deutch et al, 1992; Deutch and Cameron, 1992; Robertson and Fibiger, 1992; Merchant and Dorsa, 1993) and its functional roles in the brain. However, the most recent imaging studies have found DA levels in the associative striatum (caudate and precommissural putamen), not the ventral striatum, to be el-evated in SZ (Howes et al, 2009, 2011; Kegeles et al, 2010). Further, hypotheses im-plicating the limbic striatum in the etiology of SZ symptoms (discussed in Integrat-ing the DA and glutamate hypotheses in the Introduction) are primarily based on an-imal models and lack anatomical and neurochemical support in SZ tissue. For these 84 

reasons, there is a need to more fully understand the circuitry and neurotransmitter systems of the NAcc in SZ. 

Studying the Circuitry of the NAcc in Schizophrenia 

Historically, attempts to find an origin of SZ began by studying the gross anatomy of the brain in order to identify an area of primary pathology (Bogerts, 1985; Brown 1986; Pakkenberg, 1987; Crow, 1989; Bruton, 1990). While this ap-proach confirmed structural deficits in SZ, the macroscopic abnormalities present in the disorder were unable to completely explain its cause. As technology advanced, findings emerged of alterations in neuronal density (Benes et al, 1991; Pakkenberg 1993; Selemon et al, 1995, 1998), synaptic density (Garey et al, 1998; Glantz and Lewis, 2000), and expression of synaptic proteins (Glantz and Lewis, 1997; Woo et al, 1998; Volk et al, 2000). These findings were significant advancements in the field as they revealed that the anatomical correlates of SZ pathology are subtle structural deficits, and provided a better understanding of the biological roots for symptoms of SZ. Multiple hypotheses emerged to explain a subtle pathology that could lead to the significant functional consequences seen in SZ. These include the reduced neuropil hypothesis, based primarily on evidence from anatomical studies of the cortex (Selemon and Goldman-Rakic, 1999), and the dysconnectivity hypothesis based on evidence from neuroimaging studies (Friston, 1999; Stephan et al, 2009). While these hypotheses arose from different findings in the field, the consensus of both is that the pathology of SZ lies in the circuitry and synaptic connections between neu-rons. The NAcc is a region highly interconnected with other brain areas implicated 85 

in SZ, making it a prime region of interest for studying synaptic connections and neurotransmitter systems in SZ. 

To test the hypothesis that the circuitry of the NAcc could be altered in SZ, I used electron microscopy to measure synaptic densities and synaptic characteristics in postmortem human tissue. I found an increase in the density of asymmetric axo-spinous synapses in the NAcc core. Asymmetric synapses are typically regarded as excitatory (Gray, 1959). Within the NAcc, ultrastructural studies of afferent input have found that glutamatergic input from the cortex, thalamus, hippocampus, and amygdala form asymmetric axospinous synapses (French and Totterdell, 2004). Thus, the elevated density of asymmetric axospinous synapses suggests that the NAcc core receives elevated glutamatergic input from one of these regions. The densities of other synapse types were the same between groups. This includes symmetric synapses which are formed by dopaminergic input, as well as GABAergic interneurons and local collaterals of GABAergic projection neurons (Pickel et al, 1981; Bouyer et al, 1984). The finding that the density of symmetric synapses is normal in SZ suggests that the region does not receive elevated dopaminergic input to the region. 

I also found a reduction in the size of the postsynaptic density (PSD) of asymmetric axospinous synapses. Postsynaptic densities consist of a complex ma-trix of scaffolding proteins and signaling cascades. PSD size is directly proportional to the number of postsynaptic receptors (Nusser et al, 1998) and is dynamic, under-going remodeling of its size and composition with changes in synaptic activity (Dosemeci et al, 2001; Bosch et al, 2014; Meyer et al, 2014). This reduction in PSD 86 

size could be primary pathology intrinsic to SZ. A smaller PSD of asymmetric axo-spinous synapses could indicate abnormal signaling at these synapses compared to in controls. Very few studies have analyzed synaptic proteins in the NAcc in SZ, as reviewed in the Discussion of “Elevated Excitatory Input to the Nucleus Accumbens in Schizophrenia: A Postmortem Ultrastructural Study”. Additionally, it could be a compensatory response to the increased excitatory input to the region reported above. Receptor internalization due to overstimulation has been demonstrated in other situations (Böhm et al, 1997; Gainetdinov et al, 2004; Mizuno et al, 2010; Sur-yanarayanan et al 2011) and could explain the reduced PSD size reported here. Glu-tamate receptor binding and expression does not appear to be altered in the NAcc in SZ (see Discussion of “Elevated Excitatory Input to the Nucleus Accumbens in Schiz-ophrenia: A Postmortem Ultrastructural Study”). However, many PSD proteins are involved in the mechanism of excitotoxicity (Forder and Tymianski, 2009), so it is possible that excessive excitatory input could be resulting in reorganization of the PSD. 

These studies provide the first ultrastructural analysis of the NAcc in healthy or diseased brain. Though many studies have implicated the NAcc in SZ, none have been able to describe its circuitry. For example, based on a MAM-treated rodent model of SZ, Grace (Grace, 2000) hypothesized that reorganization of glutamatergic and dopaminergic inputs to the NAcc may be occurring in SZ. However, this is based on physiological phenomena in a rodent model and lacks the support of anatomical findings in SZ. The elevated excitatory type synapses reported here are the first find-87 

ings of abnormal synaptic organization in the NAcc in SZ and support the hypothesis of reorganization of glutamatergic input in the NAcc. 

Studying the Neurotransmitter Systems of the NAcc in Schizophrenia 

Circuitry of the NAcc is incredibly complex; DA has modulatory control of glutamate release and glutamate has modulatory control of DA release (Morari et al, 1998). Further, both modulate the excitability of NAcc neurons (O’Donnell et al, 1999). Because of this highly interactive relationship between the two neurotrans-mitter systems, it is likely that abnormalities in one could result in abnormalities in the other. The dopaminergic system of the NAcc has been implicated in the patholo-gy of SZ for decades, however it has not always been assumed to be a source of pri-mary pathology. Some hypotheses suggest that glutamatergic input to the NAcc dysregulates the DA system (Lodge et al, 2009; Lodge and Grace, 2011; Boley et al, 2014). Regardless, dysregulation of the DA system in the NAcc has not been estab-lished, and imaging studies have challenged the assumption that DA synthesis is ele-vated in this region in SZ (Howes et al, 2009, 2011; Kegeles et al, 2010). Thus, due to the outstanding hypotheses of glutamate and/or DA system dysregulation in the NAcc in SZ, my goal was to determine if the DA system or glutamatergic input are abnormal in the NAcc in SZ. 

I used immunohistochemistry for the purpose of analyzing tyrosine hydrox-ylase (TH) staining intensity, as it allows for detailed analysis of the NAcc, including distribution of TH-immunoreactivity in the individual subregions of the NAcc. By measuring the optical density of TH-immunoreactivity, I found that both the core 88 

and shell subregions of the NAcc had similar TH levels between SZ and controls. The cases used for this analysis were all chronically medicated with APDs at the time of death, thus determining the effect that APD treatment has on TH levels in the NAcc was a critical part of this study (see below). I also used western blot analysis to measure TH protein levels in the NAcc of a separate cohort of subjects. Similar to the findings with immunohistochemistry, protein levels were similar between the con-trol and SZ groups in the NAcc. These findings agree with, and build upon, the ultra-structural findings I reported above. The similar density of symmetric synapses in-dicates that the NAcc does not receive elevated dopaminergic input to the region, however it remains possible that DA synthesis was elevated in the same number of terminals. The similar levels of TH in control and SZ subjects found with IHC and protein analyses further suggest that the NAcc is not a region of elevated DA synthe-sis in SZ. 

To analyze the glutamate system within the NAcc, I used western blot analy-sis to measure protein levels of the glutamate transporters vGLUT1 and vGLUT2. The NAcc receives dense glutamatergic input, and the hypotheses discussed above theorize that input to the region is abnormal. Thus, I analyzed protein levels of the vesicular glutamate transporters vGLUT1 and vGLUT2. The presence of vGLUT in the presynaptic terminal establishes its glutamatergic phenotype, as vGLUTs are necessary and sufficient for the transport of glutamate into vesicles, and are the best markers for glutamate axon terminals (Takamori et al, 2000, 2001). Using western blot analysis, I found that vGLUT1 protein levels were normal in the NAcc in SZ, but vGLUT2 protein levels were significantly higher compared to controls. These find-89 

ings agree with the ultrastructural finding reported above that the NAcc has a higher density of glutamatergic-type synapses in SZ subjects than controls. Together, the elevated density of glutamatergic-type synapses and elevated vGLUT2 protein levels suggest that the NAcc receives increased glutamatergic input in SZ. As discussed previously (see “Uncovering the Role of the Nucleus Accumbens in Schizophrenia: A Postmortem Analysis of Tyrosine Hydroxylase and Vesicular Glutamate Transport-ers”), vGLUT1 and vGLUT2 have largely complementary expression patterns in glu-tamate neurons throughout the brain (Fremeau et al, 2001; Herzog et al, 2001; Kaneko and Fujiyama, 2002). vGLUT1 is typically associated with cortical and hip-pocampal input, and vGLUT2 is typically associated with subcortical regions such as the thalamus and amygdala. However, more intricate distribution of these two transporters exists within each of those brain regions (Kaneko et al, 2002). The NAcc has denser vGLUT2 expression than vGLUT1, likely due to its dense input from limbic and subcortical regions such as the ACC, subiculum, amygdala, and thalamus. The analysis of vGLUT2 protein levels does not allow for further detail on the origin of the elevated input, but a likely region is discussed below. 

The findings reported here are the first to report TH and vGLUT levels in the NAcc of the same subjects with SZ. Though the hypothesis for glutamatergic dysreg-ulation of DA in the NAcc in SZ emerged 10 years ago, this glutamate system has been largely overlooked in the NAcc. The western blot analysis here supports the hypothesis that the NAcc receives abnormal glutamatergic input in SZ. Further, the immunohistochemical and western blot analysis of TH agree and suggest that TH (and thus presumably DA synthesis) is normal in SZ. These findings corroborate the 90 

imaging findings in patients with SZ (Howes et al, 2009, 2011; Kegeles et al, 2010) and challenge preexisting assumptions regarding the role of the NAcc in the DA hy-pothesis of SZ. While a neurochemical analysis alone would not be able to tease apart primary pathology versus a consequence of that pathology (for example, DA dysregulation due to elevated glutamatergic input), neurochemical findings com-bined with the structural findings above help elucidate the possible pathology in-trinsic to SZ in the NAcc. 

These findings provide the first analysis of vGLUT levels in the NAcc in post-mortem SZ, as well as analysis of TH levels in the same subjects. Although the NAcc has been assumed to be a region of elevated DA synthesis in SZ, some hypotheses have suggested that instead, DA release is dysregulated by abnormal glutamatergic input to the region. The neurochemical findings in the NAcc reported in this re-search support that hypothesis by suggesting that the NAcc has elevated glutama-tergic input, and normal levels of DA synthesis. 

The Effects of Antipsychotic Drugs in the NAcc in Schizophrenia 

As discussed previously in Addressing APD treatment in SZ research , it is nec-essary to consider the potential confounding effects of antipsychotic drug treatment when studying SZ. This is particularly critical when analyzing regions of the stria-tum, which is known to be a source of both the therapeutic action and side effects of these drugs. The NAcc in particular, has been implicated as a locus for APD action in both preclinical (Deutch et al, 1992; Deutch and Cameron, 1992; Robertson and Fib-iger, 1992; Merchant and Dorsa, 1993) and clinical (Lahti et al, 2003, 2005, 2009) 91 

studies. Animal studies of DA typically either report reductions (Blaha and Lane, 1987; Ichikawa and Meltzer, 1991; Chen et al, 1991) or no change (Ichikawa and Meltzer, 1991; Invernizzi et al, 1995) in basal DA release in the NAcc after APD treatment. Animal studies of glutamate have found no change (See and Lynch, 1996; Bustillo et al, 2006) or increases (Yamamoto and Cooperman, 1994) in glutamate levels after APD treatment. These findings should be interpreted with caution how-ever, as none of the studies used drug doses and administration methods that have been shown to achieve clinically relevant treatment (See Use of Animal Models in Schizophrenia Research ). Further, only two studies have analyzed the effects of APDs on TH levels in the NAcc (discussed below), and none have analyzed the effects on vGLUTs, thus my goal was to determine if APD treatment was a factor in my neuro-chemical findings reported above. 

The approach used in this research was to study subjects with SZ who had been off-drug prior to death. Using western blot analysis, I found that the NAcc had similar protein levels of TH, vGLUT1, and vGLUT2 across the control, SZ-ON, and SZ-OFF groups. The similarity between levels in the SZ-ON and SZ-OFF groups suggests that comparisons to the control group are not confounded by APD-treatment. Only two other studies had analyzed the effects of APD treatment on TH levels in the NAcc (Marchese et al, 2002; Tejedor-Real et al, 2003) and none on vGLUT levels. The studies of TH found conflicting results which, again, likely stem from the drug treatment paradigms used. 

While studying protein levels in off-drug subjects was possible for the west-ern blot analyses, it was not feasible for the TH localization study due to the lack of 92 

off-drug cases suitable for IHC. Thus, all of the cases used for TH IHC were chronical-ly treated at the time of death. It is still not clear what effects chronic APD treatment has on TH levels in the NAcc (See Use of Animal Models in Schizophrenia Research ), so my goal was to determine how chronic treatment with these drugs affects TH immunolabeling in the NAcc. Analyzing the optical density of TH immunolabeling in the NAcc of APD treated rats, I found similar levels between the three treatment groups. These findings suggest that chronic APD treatment does not affect TH levels in the NAcc, consistent with the finding that SZ-ON and SZ-OFF subjects had similar TH protein levels, reported above. 

These findings should be interpreted with consideration of the experiment design. The APDs were administered to normal rats with a normally functioning DA system. The DA system is incredibly resilient; studies in Parkinson’s disease models show the highly adaptive compensatory response to DA neuron loss in the substan-tia nigra (Hornykiewicz, 1993; Deumens et al, 2002). Thus, it is possible that admin-istration of APDs to an animal model of SZ with a dysregulated DA system would produce different results. 

For the ultrastructural analysis of the NAcc in postmortem SZ, all subjects except one were chronically medicated with APDs. The rarity of postmortem human tissue suitable for EM in combination with the rarity of off-drug SZ postmortem cas-es makes it unfeasible to perform this type of study with both on- and off-drug cas-es. Thus, it remains possible that APDs could be confounding the synaptic density findings reported here. However, as reasoned in the Discussion of “Elevated Excita-tory Input to the Nucleus Accumbens in SZ: A Postmortem Ultrastructural Study”, it 93 

is not likely that APD effects would be the source of the differences between control and SZ groups. 

The lack of differences between the SZ-ON and SZ-OFF groups, or between the rat treatment groups, suggests that the findings in this research are not due to APD effects. Thus, I conclude that the findings in postmortem SZ subjects in this re-search are due to abnormalities intrinsic to the disorder. 

Redefining the Role of the NAcc in Schizophrenia 

Although previous hypotheses of SZ pathology have assumed the NAcc was a region of elevated DA in the disorder, the findings reported here support a different story. The results provide evidence that the NAcc is a region of pathology in SZ, but that this pathology is found in the glutamate system, not the DA system. Multiple functional consequences of excessive glutamatergic input to the NAcc can be consid-ered. 

Hyperresponsive Cortico-striato-thalamic Gating 

One consequence of excessive glutamatergic input to the NAcc could be a hy-perresponsive gating system of cortical-striatal-thalamic signaling. The NAcc re-ceives dense glutamatergic input from multiple brain regions. Input from the hippo-campus is considered to provide contextual gating, while input from the amygdala is considered to provide emotional gating for cortical input (Grace, 2000). This occurs by maintaining the principle output neurons of the NAcc, medium spiny neurons (MSNs), in either a hyperpolarized or depolarized state (Fig. 1). When glutamatergic 94

drive from either the hippocampus or amygdala primes the MSNs by shifting them to an upstate, cortical input is able to elicit an action potential in the MSN. When the MSN is in the downstate, cortical glutamatergic input is not able to elicit an action potential, and the signal stops there. In this way, the hippocampus and amygdala are considered as gating mechanisms to establish salience of the cortical input. Hypoth-eses of SZ theorizes that positive symptoms of SZ could emerge if this gating mecha-nism is hyperresponsive (Grace 2000, Kapur 2003), which could happen in the case of elevated hippocampal input. Rather than the usual gating mechanisms that estab-lish salience based on the emotional and contextual aspects of the signal, the NAcc is 95 

aberrantly creating salience for input that would otherwise be considered “unim-portant”. Kapur (2003) then describes that positive symptoms of SZ, such as hallu-cinations and delusions, are top-down cognitive processes used to make sense of this newly salient input. 

Dysregulation of NAcc Dopamine 

A second consequence could be a hyperresposive DA system in the NAcc. The hypothesis that glutamate abnormalities could drive striatal DA dysfunction in SZ is not new (Olney and Farber, 1995; Moghaddam et al, 1997). Preclinical studies (Mil-ler et al 1996) and imaging studies in healthy volunteers (Kegeles et al, 2000) have demonstrated the ability of NMDA antagonists to enhance DA efflux in the striatum. Glutamatergic input modulates DA release in the NAcc (Blaha et al, 1997; Floresco et al, 1998; Glowinski et al, 1988). As discussed in “Uncovering the Role of the Nucleus Accumbens in Schizophrenia: A Postmortem Analysis of Tyrosine Hydroxylase and Vesicular Glutamate Transporters”, the NAcc receives glutamatergic input from sev-eral brain regions, but there is evidence to suggest that the hippocampus could be a source of this increased input. Stimulation of the ventral subiculum increases DA release in the NAcc through direct input (Blaha et al, 1997; Brudzynski and Gibson, 1997; Taepavarapruk et al, 2000), and indirectly via connections with the VTA (Legault et al, 2000; Floresco et al, 2001). DA is a critical component of the complex processing that occurs within the NAcc; it acts to decrease system noise by focusing activity to MSNs that are receiving the most important (highest convergent excitato-ry) input (Nicola et al, 2000). Increased DA release would cause the system to re-96 

spond to otherwise unimportant (weak) stimuli. It is this dysregulation of DA in the NAcc then, that has been hypothesized to cause symptoms of SZ (Lodge et al, 2009; Lodge and Grace, 2011; Boley et al, 2014). 

Enhanced Disinhibition of Nigro-striatal Dopaminergic Input 

A third consequence of excessive glutamatergic input to the NAcc could be elevated DA release in the dorsal striatum. MSNs of the NAcc modulate DA release in the dorsal striatum via projections to the substantia nigra (Haber et al, 2000). Alt-hough the NAcc receives relatively restricted dopaminergic input from the mid-brain, it sends GABAergic projects to GABAergic interneurons across a wide area of the substantia nigra. Through this feedback loop, stimulation of NAcc MSNs disin-hibits dopaminergic input to the dorsal striatum. Thus, elevated glutamatergic input to the NAcc could increase excitation of MSNs, which would disinhibit the dopamin-ergic input to the dorsal striatum, and could result in the hyperdopaminergic state in SZ (Howes et al, 2009, 2011; Kegeles et al, 2010). 

Conclusions 

The findings presented in this dissertation provide insight into the pathology of the NAcc in SZ. The increased excitatory input to the NAcc found in these studies provides evidence from postmortem human tissue that is highly consistent with the animal model-based hypothesis of SZ that suggests enhanced glutamatergic input to the NAcc could disturb stimulus processing in the region and lead to positive symp-toms of SZ. These studies are the first to provide an analysis of the circuitry of the 97 

NAcc in SZ, as well as an analysis of both the glutamatergic and dopaminergic sys-tems in the region. The findings provide a comprehensive explanation for the role of the NAcc in the etiology of SZ. 98
