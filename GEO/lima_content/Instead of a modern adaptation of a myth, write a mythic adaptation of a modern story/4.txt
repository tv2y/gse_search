You mean, is it a good idea to do so? As almost always with writing, It Depends. Do not change it in a way that will confuse the reader unless confusing them is your aim (and bearing in mind that most people don’t like being confused unless the confusion is resolved later, as in a standard mystery novel).

If you are mapping the mythology into something else—as in John Updike’s The Centaur, where a high-school teacher is mapped to Chiron, the principle is Zeus, the teacher’s son is Heracles (IIRC)—you can play around with the plot (though Updike didn’t) and people may be a little confused but may just say, “Oh, so this version is different.”

Also, you can modify the plot if you explain why, either implicitly or explicitly. It is now a common gag to portray Persephone as going willingly with Hades to the underworld to escape her over-controlling mother Demeter. That implicitly explains why Persephone could have been “abducted” and yet have the happiest, most stable marriage in the pantheon.

Neil Gaiman’s novel American Gods runs on mythology and alters it all over the place, but the gods are always explaining to the protagonist why things have to be done differently here.

But if you just have Prometheus strangle the eagle the second time it comes for his liver, and escape, end of story, people will mostly just go “Huh? What?”

Yes, it is. You’re writing fiction. Not literary criticism to religious books.

Tolkien was obviously inspired by Christianity and good and evil too.

Try to remember this: the modified mythology and religion you use needs to be written without it sounding like there is a sense of judgement in it. So when you write it make it sound like the narrator doesn’t condemn. Or doesn’t think it’s bad or good. It’s just there. And it’s a fact.

Characters can feel strongly about it ofcourse (just like humans might feel strongly opposed or strongly in favor of a religion). Just don’t make it sound like you, as an author, think the religion is silly or phony.

Take it seriously. And flesh it out as deeply as you can. Change some of the names. Borrow from more than one mythology. And make your religious lore big, impactful and serious with the regards to the story it’s in.

That depends. Let’s say you’re writing a fictional story about how John McHypothetical suddenly discovers a portal to ancient Greece and goes through it to wind up hanging out with the Greek gods. He finds out that most of the mythology is true, but it turns out Ares actually rather enjoys taking care of his bonsai garden, Poseidon has a gambling problem at horse races where his horses always race, and Hades is actually a stoner who is high all the time. Then, sure, it’s a fictional story, and the mythology is a backdrop to the world you’re creating.

On the other hand though, if you’re aiming to write a non-fiction book, where the elements of mythology are primarily meant to be presented as a factual reference regarding ancient beliefs, then no, you can’t just add a few things that you feel would spice things up. If it’s a reference book, informing people about those ancient mythologies, then you can’t simply add “and Zeus kept getting it on with other women because he couldn’t stand Hera’s taste in pottery” unless you’ve got some sources that can back that up.

The answer depends on whether the author is a plotter, a pantser, or a mixture of both.

A panster will, in most cases, begin writing their story with only a rough idea of how it will flow. Perhaps they have the core of an idea, and a vague notion of the events or characters involved in the process.

For instance, I began my novel ‘A Construct of Angels’ with a single question in my head: What would happen if a dead body came back to life in a mortuary (morgue)?’

Three books and a short story emerged from there.

A plotter, on the other hand, is likely to have a beginning, middle, and end written down, as well as character names, their jobs and backstories, and their personalities figured out before they type ‘Chapter One.’

I have tried to do this at least once, and while I'm usually able to work most of these aspects out, I end up hating the part that follows (writing the actual story), because the rigidity of the plotted narrative gives me no room to improvise or expand. For me, the joy lies in the discovery of possibility, and learning how my character will react to unforeseen obstacles.

A mixed writer will appear somewhere on the line drawn between the two types, and their method will use some pantser and some plotter techniques to varying degrees, depending on the way they create their narrative.

So, in answer to your question, it varies from writer to writer.

The primary advantage of using a myth is that you can alter it to suit what ever point you were trying to make. I will use Greek examples because that is what I am most familiar with. When the story of Oedipus is mentioned in the Iliad, there is no hint of the infamous incest. There is a risk of changing the myth into an entirely different myth if you change too much. If you do alter the details of a myth, you have to be sure that your writing skills are good enough that people will not assume that you are simply making an error. If you are using Greek names in a story that is slightly different than the one that is considered canon, readers will assume that you are adding a twist. If you are writing a slice of life story in which one of the characters spout mythology “incorrectly”, readers will assume that you have not done your research - unless the character can defend themselves by citing a particular source that it differs from what is considered canon. The versions of the paternity of Athena that make her daughter either of Pallas or Poseidon is an example. Robert Graves’ Greek Myths is a great resource for alternate takes.

If your twist is not found in any extent source, citing a real but no longer extant work is a possible solution. But don’t use Plato or Aristotle – if one of their works had survived, it might have changed the entirety of philosophy. Pick someone more middling. Perhaps have the version you are using be a later retelling. That way, you can acknowledge the change out of the universe, but also have it as a legitimate traditional interpretation with in the universe.

If you have renamed everybody in the myth but it is still recognizable, then any variation will be seen as a significant departure from the traditional telling but also as relevant to the in-universe setting. He will have to make that significant departure count!

So, go ahead and write the myth the way you want to, but be aware of the potential consequences from the perspective of the reader.

Is this a fiction novel you’re writing?

Well, there are no rules for writing. You can pretty much write whatever you want. As long as your plot is intriguing and you’ve written well (by which I mean the grammar) I don’t think you need to worry about the bits you’re including or leaving out. Just keep the book interesting!

I recently read the book Gods Behaving Badly by Marie Phillips, which doesn’t have Gods from different mythologies (unlike your question) but only covers Greek Gods. The author has weaved a story around what the Gods would do in the 21st century. The 12 Greek Gods, with fleshed personalities, live together in a house in London and often have immortal fights between each other.

While the book was an interesting read and makes you burst into a laughter, I realized that only if you know the Greek Mythology would you be able to enjoy the novel. I don’t know it so well and a couple of God names were new to me, which eventually lead me to put down the book.

If you are including beasts from different mythologies, the downside would be that

1.   Your readers might not have a complete idea about all mythologies, which might prove to be a hindrance.
2.   Beasts are less popular than the gods, which might further the impact a bit.

So, make sure you include proper descriptions for people to understand the plot better.

By the way, I would definitelly look forward to reading a book with beasts from different mythologies. You could include some ‘Asuras’ from Indian mythology as well. They sure were evil!

Good luck with the book!
