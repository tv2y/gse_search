Call them and ask for your transcript as I did. Despite a gifted Student education the letters IQ were never used or even spoken about. Instead we were just told our percentile we we in. For instance being in the 99th percentile means your IQ is in the top one percent of the population. It is the only way to compare intelligence levels across the different IQ tests used that measure from 150 to 200 or higher.

I did not send for my school transcript until my forties when I was told by a new employer who had me tested that I had the highest IQ in the corporation worldwide. I knew I was in the 99th percentile but thought that was among students in my high school grade. My parents were uneducated so I never heard about IQ back in the 50’s and 60’s. I did not know that the admissions test for the private high school whose gifted program I was placed in plus a scholarship, was the Wechsler test for children.

I had an impressive list of major accomplishments and always felt that knowing my IQ would change anything. Do not use any online service that charges you to get it. Just call the school and ask for your transcript. They may just email you a copy for free. That is how I did it. I actually thought my IQ was much lower than it was for some reason as I could not believe it was what it was.

Only for California, as I do not know about other states, although since I was told that this is based on federal law perhaps it would be about the same elsewhere. Strangely enough, I found out my IQ score from school, and it only took me 14 years.

When I moved from a small town in another province at age 15 to Calgary, the only academic information I took with me was a piece of paper saying I had passed Grade 9. When my mother enrolled me in Grade 10 in a Calgary high school, she insisted that I be put in the courses required for university entrance.

Assuming the worst, the school put me in the class with the lowest ability students who were expected to complete the university entrance courses in three years. In those days before computerized individual scheduling, students were grouped by ability and option. (I was in Band.) The top academic students were in 10A. I was in 10M.

Early in the school year, we were all given a multiple choice IQ test. When the results came back, I was called into the office of the school counsellor and asked if I wanted to transfer into 10B, the high academic, band option, class. By then I had made some friends in 10M, and said, no thanks. Apparently my test score had caused them to re-evaluate my potential.

However, they felt they had to do something, and figured I should be in a higher achieving science class. In order to make that schedule work, I was also moved to a low-achieving English class of students who were expected to need four years to meet university entrance standards. I suppose the assumption was that a high potential male would go into some area of science. English would be less important to me.

The next year I was in 11B (high academic Band option students) and in Grade 12, having dropped band, I was in 12A, the top academic class. That year I achieved the second place academic achievement award for Grade 12 boys.

I defied their expectations by enrolling in an Honours English program at the University of Calgary.

Fast forward about 14 years. By then I had a master’s degree in counselling psychology and worked as a school counsellor at another Alberta school. I was able to get time off to attend a conference in Calgary, and during some free time, I popped into my old school to say hello to a couple of my old teachers who were still there. I also stopped by the counselling office to meet the counsellor, definitely not the one from my time there. I mentioned the sequence of events above, and the counsellor went into the files and found my records, which included my test score.

And that’s how I found out my IQ score from Grade 10. (I have long since forgotten the score, and in my professional opinion, such scores are only marginally useful.)

Schools sometimes administer IQ or cognitive assessments and keep records, but access and availability vary. Use this step-by-step approach to locate your score efficiently:

1.   Identify the test and timeframe

- Remember roughly when and where the test was given (school name, year/grade, special program such as gifted services, special education, or psychological evaluation).
2.   Contact the school district records office or your former school

- Request student records or “cumulative file” (sometimes called cumulative record, cumulative folder, or student permanent record).
3.   If the school no longer has records

- Ask the district about its records retention policy and where older records are archived. Many districts keep records for a set number of years and then transfer to a central archive or destroy them; others keep them indefinitely.
4.   Legal rights and ID verification

- Adults requesting their own records generally must provide photo ID and sign an authorization form. Parents/guardians need proof of guardianship and the child’s identifying information.
5.   Request format and fees

- Ask how the district prefers requests: phone, email, online form, or written letter. Use written requests when possible to create a paper trail.
6.   If only a summary or placement decision exists

- Some files contain only placement decisions (e.g., “eligible for gifted program”) without an explicit IQ score. If the raw score isn’t present, request the full psychological evaluation that produced the decision.
7.   If the school refuses or records are missing

- Request a written explanation of denial or destruction date. If necessary, escalate to district records custodian or the state education agency.
8.   Alternatives if you cannot retrieve the original score

- Arrange a current, professional IQ or cognitive assessment: licensed psychologist or school psychologist can administer contemporary tests (WISC-V, WAIS-IV/WAIS-V, Stanford-Binet 5, Woodcock-Johnson).
9.   Interpreting scores and context

- IQ tests are standardized with a mean of 100 and SD typically 15; different instruments and older editions are not perfectly equivalent.
10.   Practical checklist to start now

- Locate school/district contact info.

Examples of typical outcomes

*   You receive a full evaluation report including WISC IQ (e.g., FSIQ = 112) and index scores. 
*   You receive only placement documentation (e.g., “met criteria for gifted services”) without numeric IQ; the district points to retention policies. 
*   Records were destroyed/transferred; the district provides the destruction date or archive location.

Note on confidentiality and sensitivity

*   IQ results are educational and medical-like records; share them selectively. If using the score for services or diagnoses, prefer a current evaluation from a qualified examiner.
