So, you think you’ve drafted a tweet, an email, a short story, or even a novel. These are different forms of communication, but the process of bringing them to fruition has a necessary, sometimes overlooked step: editing! Unless you’re a professional writer, it’s unlikely that you have an editor who can review your writing regularly. Here are some tips to help you review your own work.

1.   Give your writing some space. Have you ever felt a mix of pure relief and joy when you’ve finished a draft of something? Don’t downplay that feeling and the ability to walk away from your work before you start editing it. You may need minutes, hours, or days, but once you sit back down with what you originally had on the page, you’ll have the thrill of looking at it with fresh eyes. You’ll notice errors you may not have seen the first time. You’ll come to new realizations about its overall tone and structure. If it’s a text or email, maybe you only need a few minutes away from it. If it’s a story or essay, perhaps you’ll need longer. Regardless of what type of work it is, it will help your writing tremendously.
2.   Don’t use overachieving synonyms. Looking at your work for the second, third, or fourth time around may inspire you to spice up your language with longer, more uncommon words. There’s nothing wrong with having a thesaurus nearby, but try to limit the repetition of long, pretentious-feeling words so your work flows well and doesn’t feel too bogged down. At the end of the day, you want it to feel true to you and the message you’re conveying.
3.   Remember who the reader is. Don’t forget your own voice as the writer—but don’t forget who your reader is. Many writers get too close to their work; editing is a chance to try to get out of your own head. Who is your ideal reader? What do you want them to take away from the writing? It’s a unique time to step in their shoes, to make sure your communication is as effective as you’d like it to be.
4.   Kill your darlings. Don’t be scared to remove chunks of your work, even if it feels precious to you. If it’s a passage that’s really tough to part with, try saving it somewhere else, so you can return to it later in your piece or for another work.

Just as in any skill or profession, I would recommend you learn the rules before you break them. Learning to recognize and write with meter and structure will train your ear to write with rhythm intuitively. A poem of chopped-up prose will always sound messy, even if the ideas and imagery contained within are powerful and original. One does not need to write in structured meter to write with rhythm, but your free verse poem is more likely to be rhythmic if you understand these concepts and train your mind to write this way. Repetition of rhythm also helps emphasize key words, and creates expectations in the reader. When those expectations are met or subverted, the effect you are trying to instill becomes more striking. When working with structure, you are also forced to make choices- what to say, how to say it, and what to leave out. This economy of language over time will generally yield higher quality poetry. There is no shortage of incredible free-verse poetry, but you must remember two things: most of best-remembered poetry is indeed written in classical meter, and most of the best free-verse poetry was written by poets who had the ability to write in structured verse anyway.

Personally, I think poets choose to write some of their work in blank verse because it allows them to use the features and familiarity of the iambic pentameter’s rhythm without completely restricting their stanzas with rhyme.

As for why you need to stick with a rhythm at all: rhythm is and always will be one of the most important components of a poem. The rhythm you choose to follow for the piece that you write can subtly affect how a reader will receive the ideas and feelings you’re trying to communicate through your work.

I believe the iambic pentameter, having originally evolved as a means of establishing the English language as “just as literary as Latin, thank you very much” lends a poem a serious, intellectual vibe. That, in turn, encourages a reader’s mind to approach the poem’s concepts as deep, philosophical, and intellectually weighty.

So if a poet wishes to convey ideas that they want people to chew on without becoming overly lyrical, he may choose to use a classical measured meter like iambic pentameter.

You’re the poet, so you can decide.

Unless you’re really skilled, however, you should avoid such lapses whenever you can— if what you’re writing has a defined meter. In blank and free verse, pretty much anything goes. Word choice in these forms is dictated by the images and emotion the poet is writing to evoke rather than upon a specific rhythmic pattern.

When writing poetry with defined and specific meter, you do need to be precise. Feminine (that is, unstressed) final syllables are acceptable; used too frequently they can sap the energy of your poem. And where the word you want ruins the meter, find another word that doesn’t :) Sometimes the effort to find a different word can lead you to a more powerful image; sometimes you realize the entire line could be structured more effectively.

And yes, even modern poets sometimes choose to write sonnets, or iambic tetrameter, or whatever :)

I've made this suggestion many times and state it again: never, ever, intentionally rhyme anything. But when rhymes appear spontaneously in your text, use them.

An injured bee

came to me

by landing on my towel at the beach.

It stumbled across

pink terrycloth

toward a small rock of granite.

While clambering up

to a granite ledge,

it fell fifteen inches to the sand.

Poor bee! Lifting off, falling back:

always moving forward

unto death.

This is a poem I wrote at 10:00 am this morning, and it rhymes. I always let my poems rhyme when they want to, because spontaneous rhymes are charming.

Here, the first rhyme is obvious, but the second, not so much.

The first rhyme (of “me” with “bee”) is called a perfect, full, exact, or true rhyme. The second rhyme (of “terrycloth” with “across”) is called an imperfect, half, near, lazy, or slant rhyme.

Academics and poetry editors prefer imperfect to perfect rhymes, they really do! But again…never rhyme by intent: let the language do that job automatically.

This is the whole story on rhyme in contemporary American poetry, at least from my perspective.

-Lance

P.S. Don't forget internal rhymes: those count, too.

The use of blank verse dominates all of Shakespeare’s plays. Some (such as Richard II) are written almost entirely in blank verse… in other plays, he combines blank verse with some prose. The “What a piece of work is a man” speech, one of the greatest, is actually in prose.

To review, blank verse is a way of putting down words according to a regular metric scheme — Shakespeare used iambic pentameter — but only occasionally rhyming, and then only for emphasis.

There is at least one super practical advantage of blank verse. It frees the playwright from punctuation. The actors, if they need to take a breath, take it at the end of a line… whether there is a full stop (a period) at the end of the line or not.

So what does this matter? Because the playwright is liberated from having to put in full stops, or even commas, at regular intervals. Consequently, the playwright is freed to write exceptionally long sentences if the spirit moves him, piling metaphor upon metaphor, allusion upon allusion, as Shakespeare did

Blank verse is especially helpful in MacBeth. I once forgot this rule of how to read blank verse and I auditioned for a role by reading MaBeth’s “Is this a dagger I see before me?” speech.

I forgot to take the breath at the end of the lines but rather tried to wait for periods, which were few and far between. Result? By the end of that speech, I was almost asphyxiated for lack of oxygen. I almost passed out.

Shakespeare uses the verse scheme so he can write in a different way from how playwrights and screenwriters write today.

For a contrast, look at any of the plays of David Mamet or any of his contemporaries. Lines are short, clipped, incomplete, and fast-paced. Mamet writes lines like “Will you go to lunch?” and “You can’t sell shit. You are shit.” Shakespeare likes to write lines of iambic pentameter such as the following — no less psychologically real:

Uneasy lies the head that wears the crown.

That’s from Henry IV, part 2. Here’s something from MacBeth:

And all our yesterdays have lighted fools

The way to dusty death. Out, out, brief candle!

In a crude analogy, it’s a little like the difference between a rap song and an aria, Shakespearean speeches being more like the latter.

It’s certainly okay for poems to use anything between unrhymed verse and a strict rhyme scheme. However …

What’s not okay is when it detracts from the possible impact of the poem.

For example, imagine that you’ve written most of a poem in an ABAB rhyme scheme using perfect rhyme. There’s one stanza you’re having a hard time making work, so you stick in an “almost-rhyme”.

When someone reads this poem, the one imperfect rhyme will stick out to them. It won’t look like a conscious choice. It’ll look like you were trying to do one thing but failed or gave up on it, and that will take the reader out of the experience of reading the poem.

It’s even more clear when someone starts out writing poetry and writes a poem full of almost rhymes and kinda rhymes. It gives the impression that the author is trying to write rhymes and failing.

I love poems that use imperfect rhymes (sometimes called slant rhymes) to create a more interesting effect than perfect rhymes would. Some examples (first from Emily Dickinson):

> A NARROW fellow in the grass
> 
> 
> Occasionally rides;
> 
> 
> You may have met him,—did you not?
> 
> 
> His notice sudden is.
> 
> 
> 
> [1]

It’s hard to describe the feeling “His notice sudden is” gives to me. It doesn’t leap out at you like a true rhyme would - you might skate over it and then “suddenly notice” the echo of “rides” in “is”.

And just because I want to, an early poem from Sylvia Plath:

> Flintlike, her feet struck
> 
> 
> Such a racket of echoes from the steely street,
> 
> 
> Tacking in moon-blued crooks from the black
> 
> 
> Stone-built town, that she heard the quick air ignite
> 
> 
> Its tinder and shake
> 
> 
> A firework of echoes from wall
> 
> 
> To wall of the dark, dwarfed cottages.
> 
> 
> 
> [2]

The amount of rhymes Plath stuffs into one stanza would sound very formal, and perhaps too artificial, if they were perfect rhymes. But she uses slant rhymes, so the effect is more subtle. In addition, her end rhymes in lines 1, 3, and 5 play off of all the “k” sounds within the lines.

Some commonalities here; it works better if you don’t just go for “any word that sounds similar” but have rules for your imperfect rhymes. Rhyming “self” with “filled” will always sound a bit amateurish. Using the same consonant sound at the end of words with different vowel sounds is a much more stable, repeatable way of doing it. From the last example, “struck”, “black”, and “shake” set up a pattern. Also, watch your perfect rhymes! If you use a bunch of slant rhymes and then a perfect rhyme, it will really stand out.

English is a rhyme-poor language compared to some others. Dante, writing in Italian, was able to rhyme each line with two others. English-language poets usually don’t try to do anything like that. Rhyme wasn’t even used in English poetry until the Norman Conquest, and even after that, poets tended to ignore it for their longer works; many sections of Shakespeare plays, for example, were written in blank verse, as were the epics of Milton. This may be one of the major reasons English-language poets have found slant rhyme interesting. Robert Pinsky wrote a translation of Dante’s Inferno that used slant rhyme to replicate terza rima in English, though unfortunately I don’t think his results were anything spectacular. But I definitely urge you to explore the interesting world of writing poetry without perfect rhyme!
