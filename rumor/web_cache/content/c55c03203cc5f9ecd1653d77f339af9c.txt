1 Introduction
--------------

Since the 1990s, the concept of “core competency” has become a pivotal topic in global educational discourse [1, 2]. In China, the 2022 curriculum standards reflect this shift by emphasizing competency-oriented teaching. Science education is expected to foster students’ scientific concepts, scientific thinking, inquiry-based practical skills, and responsibility, while English education aims to develop linguistic proficiency, cultural awareness, critical thinking, and autonomous learning [3, 4].

In an era of rapid technological change and global knowledge exchange, fostering adolescents’ science literacy and competency with an international perspective is increasingly important [5]. As a global lingua franca, English grants students access to frontier scientific developments and enhances their ability to engage with global issues. English-taught science education can therefore serve as a powerful vehicle for cultivating both language competence and scientific thinking [6].

In response to these evolving demands, China’s “Double New” education reform—encompassing new curricula and new textbooks—has encouraged interdisciplinary, inquiry-based learning models such as project-based learning (PBL). These reforms aim to promote holistic development by linking subject knowledge with real-life applications. Nevertheless, challenges remain. Despite curricular innovations, many science classes continue to rely on traditional, exam-oriented approaches. Interdisciplinary integration is often limited, and the pedagogical potential of English-taught science instruction remains underexplored in public secondary schools.

While international research has increasingly addressed bilingual and interdisciplinary STEM education, there is a lack of empirical studies examining how English-medium science instruction can support China’s ongoing educational reforms. To address this gap, the present study investigates the current landscape of English science education in Shanghai’s secondary schools. Through a mixed-methods approach, it explores students’ and teachers’ needs, identifies existing barriers, and proposes an interdisciplinary, project-based English science education framework to enhance science literacy and inform future curriculum design.

2 Enhancing science literacy: a core objective of science education
-------------------------------------------------------------------

Science literacy is widely recognized as a fundamental competency for 21st-century citizens and a key goal of science education worldwide [7]. The term was first introduced in the 1950s and has evolved from a narrow focus on scientific knowledge to a broader, multidimensional construct, emphasizing its critical role in enhancing national comprehensive strength [8]. Through the contributions of numerous scholars, the academic consensus has positioned science literacy as a goal of civic education rather than specialized education [9,10,11,12]. According to the OECD’s PISA framework, science literacy includes the ability to explain scientific phenomena, evaluate and design scientific inquiry, and interpret data and evidence scientifically [13]. In China, the State Council’s Outline of the Action Plan for Improving Science Literacy for All (from 2021 to 2035) further outlines five indicators: upholding the spirit of science, establishing scientific ideas, mastering basic scientific methods, understanding scientific concepts and processes, and applying them to analyze and solve real-life issues [14].

Adolescence is considered a critical developmental stage for cultivating science literacy [15, 16]. Research on science literacy’s pedagogical practices has increasingly focused on fostering critical thinking and innovation through interdisciplinary teaching approaches [17, 18]. Studies suggest science education should employ problem-based teaching methods, and stimulate students’ interest and motivation to cultivate scientific thinking and practical skills [19,20,21]. Additionally, attention to diverse student backgrounds and inclusive teaching experiences is essential for promoting science literacy [22, 23], while cultivating students’ cognitive literacy and digital media literacy in school curricular also contributes to it [24,25,26].

Within this context, integrating English into science education has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance both scientific and language competencies [27]. Content and Language Integrated Learning (CLIL), widely practiced in Europe, emphasizes the simultaneous learning of subject content and a foreign language. CLIL has demonstrated positive outcomes in terms of content understanding, language development, and student motivation [28, 29].

In China, although English and science are taught as separate subjects, integrating them through interdisciplinary models is gaining attention. For example, the “Talk on Science” project in some secondary schools in Shanghai has piloted science modules delivered in English, combining PBL with academic language development. Such models serve as practical references for broader implementation and provide an empirical foundation for exploring English-based science literacy enhancement in public school contexts.

3 Research design and methodology
---------------------------------

### 3.1 Research questions

This study adopts a mixed-methods approach, integrating qualitative interviews and quantitative surveys to explore the current status and challenges of science education in secondary schools in Shanghai. It focuses on the following questions:

1.   (1)
_Distribution and suitability of resources._ What types of teaching resources are currently used, and are they aligned with students’ cognitive levels and language proficiency?

2.   (2)
_Challenges encountered by students and teachers._ What difficulties do students face in learning science? What interdisciplinary competencies are required of teachers, and what obstacles do they experience in instructional practice?

3.   (3)
_Expectations for improvement._ What are the key expectations of students and teachers regarding future curriculum design and pedagogical models?

### 3.2 Methodology design

This study followed a sequential exploratory mixed-methods design, in which qualitative interviews informed the development of a quantitative questionnaire.

#### 3.2.1 Phase I: Qualitative interviews

Using purposive sampling, eight participants were selected based on their direct involvement in science or bilingual education: three secondary school students, two English teachers, one science teacher, one science event organizer, and one scientist. The semi-structured interview protocol (see Online Appendix A) included open-ended questions on resource access, classroom experiences, project-based learning, and interdisciplinary needs. Interviews were audio-recorded with informed consent, transcribed verbatim, and coded independently by two researchers (Cohen’s Kappa = 0.82). Member checking was conducted to validate the interpretations.

#### 3.2.2 Phase II: Survey design and distribution

Themes and codes from the interviews were used to construct the _Shanghai Youth English Science Popularization Survey_ (see online Appendix B). The survey assessed students’ interest in science, participation in extracurricular science activities, perceptions of interdisciplinary learning, and demand for English-based PBL.

A stratified purposive sampling strategy was adopted to ensure representation across urban, suburban, and rural districts. The survey was distributed in collaboration with district education bureaus to 25 junior high schools and 22 senior high schools across 16 districts, yielding 3,459 valid responses (see Figs.1 and 2). Participation was voluntary, and parental consent was obtained for all student respondents under the age of 18.

### 3.3 Data analysis

This study adopted an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design. Insights from the initial qualitative phase—comprising semi-structured interviews with English teachers, science teachers, students, and experts—were thematically analyzed to identify key constructs and inform the design of the questionnaire used in the quantitative phase.

Prior to analysis, the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was 0.89 and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (_p_< 0.001), indicating suitability for exploratory factor analysis. Four latent factors were extracted (cumulative explained variance = 68.3%), confirming the questionnaire’s construct validity.

Descriptive statistics were used to describe frequencies and trends. Correlation analysis explored relationships between variables such as student interest and resource accessibility. Chi-square tests examined associations between categorical variables (e.g., school type and access to interdisciplinary activities). All quantitative analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0.

### 3.4 Ethical considerations

This research was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Shanghai International Studies University. Consent was obtained from all participants, with parental consent and school administrator approval required for minor participants. All responses were anonymized and stored securely in compliance with data protection regulations.

4 Analysis of the current state of science education in secondary schools
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

### 4.1 Distribution of existing science resources

Students in Shanghai access science resources through various channels, including school curricula, extracurricular activities, media platforms, and social science institutions.

Survey data indicate that 25% of students primarily rely on school-based science and English courses, with 23.6% reporting participation in English-taught elective science programs co-taught by English and science teachers. 18% of respondents indicated involvement in extracurricular science activities such as visits to science museums, guest lectures, and school-based science clubs. 2% of students had attended lectures delivered by domestic or international scientists, reflecting an increased institutional effort to provide real-world exposure to scientific inquiry.

Beyond school, 41% of students reported using online videos, science-themed apps, and social media platforms (e.g., Bilibili, TikTok) as supplementary learning resources (see Fig.3). These media were favored for their accessibility, vivid visuals, and engaging formats. One student noted:

> “I often watch short videos about science on Bilibili—they’re fun and easy to understand, especially when they mix animation with experiments.”

Shanghai’s 38 Youth Science Innovation Practice Stations provide a wide range of hands-on opportunities. Some stations conduct short-term research activities via summer camps, offering students a rapid immersion in scientific research; others offer systematic research projects, enabling students to engage deeply in scientific inquiry. Additionally, some stations implement a mentor-based model, pairing students with professional mentors for personalized online guidance, enhancing their ability to apply science knowledge and expanding their scientific thinking.

A chi-square test confirmed a significant association between students' willingness to enroll in school-based English science courses and their access to diverse resource channels (χ 2 = 23.72, _p_< 0.001), suggesting that resource accessibility substantially boosts students’ interest and engagement.

#### 4.2.1 Student challenges

Despite the availability of resources, several key barriers were reported (see Figs.4 and 5).

_Knowledge Comprehension Difficulties._ 29.6% of students reported difficulty understanding science materials in English, especially due to complex terminology and abstract scientific concepts. Lower-grade students were significantly more likely to report these difficulties (t = 2.37, _p_< 0.05).

“It’s not just the English that’s hard. The science content itself is too abstract without examples or visuals,” one junior high student reflected.

_Limited Accessibility to Quality Materials._ Over 22% of students expressed concern about the credibility and quality of English science materials online. Only 12.5% had access to bilingual textbooks vetted by their teachers. Moreover, the lack of platforms for post-class interactions with teachers or scientists restricts deeper exploration of scientific concepts.

_Barriers to Activity Participation._ The correlation between students’ English proficiency scores and their participation frequency in science activities was statistically significant (r = 0.23, _p_< 0.05). Students with lower language ability reported less confidence in joining science camps or competitions. Time constraints due to academic pressure were also frequently mentioned.

#### 4.2.2 Teacher challenges

Interviews from teachers revealed three core challenges

_Lack of Cross-Disciplinary Training._ Inadequate teacher training and support systems has left many English and science teachers with a limited understanding of the goals and significance of English science education, resulting in weak cross-disciplinary integration. English teachers often overemphasize language instruction at the expense of scientific principles, while science teachers prioritize knowledge delivery over fostering inquiry. This imbalance can lead to fragmented understanding or an overemphasis on abstract concepts, reducing accessibility and practical application.

> “We want to try project-based teaching, but we don’t know where to start, especially when the content involves another subject.”

_Knowledge Limitations._ English teachers lacked confidence in delivering scientific explanations, while science teachers struggled with English expression. Many found it difficult to simplify content for students without distorting scientific accuracy.

_Resource Limitations._ Limited teaching resources further hinder educators. Some Youth Science Innovation Practice Stations struggle with a shortage of mentors, restricting their ability to provide personalized research guidance to every student.

### 4.3 Expectations of students and teachers for science education

#### 4.3.1 Students’ expectations

_Diverse Resources and Engaging Formats._ A majority of students (76.4%) expressed the desire for richer learning resources and more hands-on, exploratory science activities. Among resource types, audiovisual materials (e.g., documentaries, animations) were rated as “highly effective” by 68.1% of students, particularly for explaining complex scientific concepts in English.

“Animations and videos help me visualize the topic. They’re much easier to understand than reading long texts,” shared a senior high student.

_Interactive and Inquiry-Based Activities._ Students also emphasized the value of project-based learning and real-world problem-solving. 59.7% favored activities such as science fairs, simulations, and experiments that allow them to apply theory to practice. Intensive programs—like English science summer camps—were praised for improving English fluency, confidence, and scientific inquiry skills.

_Access to Authentic Expertise._ Nearly half (47.9%) of students expressed strong interest in interacting with real scientists, whether through mentorship, guest lectures, or Q&A sessions (Fig.6).

#### 4.3.2 Teachers’ expectations

Teachers strongly supported reforms in the science education ecosystem, emphasizing:

_Interdisciplinary Training_. 82.6% of surveyed teachers hoped for cross-disciplinary professional development programs. Many advocated for joint workshops that bring together English and science educators to co-design lessons and share strategies.

_Collaborative Teaching Mechanisms_. Interviewees suggested institutional support for team-teaching models.

> “It’s not enough to have enthusiasm—we need time, coordination, and a system that supports us working together.”

_Resource Development_. Teachers called for bilingual or scaffolded science textbooks, aligned with students’ language proficiency and cognitive levels. Additionally, they expressed the need for digital tools and online repositories of vetted English science content.

_Systemic Support and Incentives_. Participants emphasized that school leadership and policy frameworks should incentivize innovation in teaching practices, including curriculum flexibility, assessment reform, and recognition of interdisciplinary instruction.

5 Discussion
------------

### 5.1 Interpretation of findings

_Fragmented Curriculum and Resource Gaps._ The study reveals significant misalignment between existing English science teaching materials and students’ cognitive, linguistic, and cultural profiles. Imported materials are often decontextualized, and emerging scientific topics—such as climate change or AI—are insufficiently integrated into standard curricula. This echoes concerns raised in international studies regarding the accessibility and relevance of bilingual STEM content.

_Limited Interdisciplinary Capacity Among Teachers._ The lack of interdisciplinary training among educators presents a substantial barrier. Many English teachers are unfamiliar with scientific discourse, while science teachers may lack English proficiency or pedagogical tools for bilingual instruction. This finding aligns with Shulman’s [30] pedagogical content knowledge (PCK) framework, underscoring the need for teacher preparation that bridges both domains. In particular, the concept of “synthetic PCK” [31]—which emphasizes teachers’ ability to interweave language, content, and pedagogy—is highly relevant in bilingual STEM education. Without such integrated knowledge bases, teachers may struggle to scaffold scientific understanding in a second language, reducing the efficacy of interdisciplinary instruction.

_Students’ Preferences for Bilingual and Experiential Learning._ Students expressed a clear preference for bilingual instruction, inquiry-based learning, and exposure to real-life scientific applications. These preferences suggest a strong alignment with constructivist learning theories and the science literacy framework proposed by Bybee [32], which advocates integrating conceptual understanding, inquiry skills, and social context. Bybee’s model, which defines science literacy in terms of multiple dimensions—including functional, conceptual, procedural, and epistemic literacy—provides a comprehensive lens through which students’ desire for hands-on and bilingual learning can be understood. Their preferences reflect a move toward epistemic engagement with science, wherein language becomes a medium for inquiry rather than mere transmission.

_School-Level Limitations._ Although institutional policies increasingly support curriculum reform, operational gaps remain. Few schools provide dedicated interdisciplinary programs or stable platforms for teacher collaboration. Without systemic support, reforms may remain symbolic rather than substantive.

### 5.2 Strategic recommendations

Based on the key findings, the following strategies and enabling conditions are proposed to enhance the development of English science education:

_Strengthen Localized and Culturally Relevant Resources_. Schools and curriculum designers should co-develop science materials tailored to Chinese students’ linguistic and conceptual needs. Incorporating high-quality online platforms (e.g., video courses, virtual labs), science books, and audiovisual materials into classroom teaching would provide students with accessible and engaging tools. Internationally, programs like CLIL have emphasized the importance of adapting subject content for language learners [29]. Localized science content embedded in real-life contexts can increase engagement and comprehension.

_Promote Interdisciplinary Teacher Training._ Develop specialized courses to bridge knowledge gaps between disciplines, enhancing both science literacy and English teaching abilities. Provide continuous opportunities for teachers to refine their interdisciplinary skills through workshops, seminars, and collaborative learning. These should be part of a sustained professional development program with regular feedback sessions and peer collaborations to help teachers fine-tune their approach and stay updated with new interdisciplinary teaching methodologies. Encourage interdisciplinary teaching teams to create and implement activities and curricula, raising teaching standard. These teams should develop a unified pedagogical approach blending scientific concepts with language development, ensuring cohesive learning. Additionally, teams can share best practices and innovative teaching techniques.

_Develop Collaborative Learning Ecosystems._ Collaborations with universities, research institutes, and industry can provide students with real-world learning experiences and access to professional mentors. These partnerships are essential to extend learning beyond the classroom and foster career-oriented science literacy.

_Promote Interactive Learning Opportunities._ Science competitions, summer camps, and similar initiatives help students cultivate scientific reasoning and cross-cultural communication skills. These hands-on experiences are essential for reinforcing classroom learning and fostering curiosity-driven exploration.

_Strengthen Policy and Institutional Support._ For strategic recommendations to be effectively implemented, coordinated policy support is essential. Policymakers should develop targeted policies that clarify the role of bilingual science education in national curriculum reforms and provide guidance on teacher training, subject integration, and resource allocation. At the school level, leadership teams should align English science education with broader curricular goals and national development priorities, while promoting interdisciplinary coordination and teacher collaboration.

_Expand Extracurricular and Community-Based Support._ To address the lack of practice-oriented learning opportunities, schools should collaborate with external organizations—including universities, research centers, and private enterprises—to create extracurricular programs such as experimental projects and science workshops. Community organizations and local businesses can also support bilingual science education by offering sponsorships, volunteer engagement, or guest lectures. These joint efforts not only expand students’ access to real-world scientific contexts but also foster a supportive learning culture grounded in community participation.

By implementing these strategies and cultivating multi-level support systems, schools can build a more sustainable and inclusive bilingual science education ecosystem—one that empowers students with both scientific literacy and global competencies.

### 5.3 Practical challenges of implementing PBL

While PBL has shown promise in enhancing science literacy and interdisciplinary thinking, its broader implementation faces several practical obstacles in the Chinese secondary school context.

_Time and Curriculum Constraints._ Many schools operate under rigid curriculum schedules with strong emphasis on exam-oriented instruction. Integrating PBL, which requires time for planning, experimentation, and group reflection, may be viewed as a distraction from “core” content. Similar concerns have been raised in international contexts [21], where teachers report difficulties aligning PBL with standardized curricula.

_Inconsistent Teacher Readiness._ As highlighted earlier, not all teachers are equipped with the interdisciplinary knowledge or project facilitation skills needed for effective PBL delivery. Without adequate scaffolding, PBL may devolve into loosely structured activities lacking depth.

_Assessment Misalignment._ Traditional assessment systems in China still rely heavily on rote memorization and written exams. PBL’s emphasis on collaboration, creativity, and critical thinking is rarely captured in these formats. This mismatch discourages both students and teachers from fully investing in PBL. Reforms are needed to integrate alternative assessment formats—such as portfolios and performance-based evaluations—to align with PBL’s learning objectives.

_Equity and Access Issues._ Access to quality PBL experiences often depends on school funding and geographic location. Urban schools may benefit from partnerships with universities and industry, while rural or under-resourced schools may lack such opportunities, thereby exacerbating educational inequality.

### 5.4 A pilot practice in shanghai: “Talk on Science”

The “Talk on Science” (TOS) project is a pilot initiative designed to implement a PBL approach in science education. Organized by a research team from Shanghai International Studies University, in collaboration with teachers and scientists, the summer camp aims to foster connections between scientists and youth, and enhance teenage science literacy. During the camp, students acquire scientific knowledge through on-site visits, hands-on participation in scientific experiments, expert lectures, and instructor guidance. By the end of the project, students exhibited their understanding of the topics through group presentations. Its implementation has demonstrated several notable strengths.

The program’s collaboration among English instructors, scientific researchers, and speech coaches represents a significant pedagogical innovation. Throughout the camp, students have multiple opportunities to engage directly with renowned scientists from institutions such as Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Shanghai Normal University, the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility, and the Chinese Academy of Sciences contribute to the camp. Through this interdisciplinary cooperation, students were able to engage with scientific concepts in English, thereby enhancing both domain-specific knowledge and academic communication skills. This dual development echoes the tenets of science literacy theory, which emphasize not only understanding scientific content but also the ability to communicate and apply it within social and linguistic contexts [33]. The bilingual mode of delivery serves as both a cognitive tool and a communicative bridge, amplifying students’ ability to engage with science critically and globally. The project’s emphasis on hands-on experiments and exploration of frontier topics—such as unmanned aerial vehicles and carbon sequestration—greatly increased student engagement by aligning learning content with their interests and real-world concerns. Furthermore, the requirement for students to present scientific findings in English promoted critical 21st-century competencies, including public speaking, teamwork, and cross-disciplinary thinking.

In response to the demand for more science reading materials, the project provides various resources, including the “I Wonder Why” series, National Geographic magazines, _Scientific American_, and _Science Fusion_. To accommodate different age groups, the difficulty of materials is adjusted, with complex vocabulary replaced and examples and metaphors added to aid understanding.

For instance, in the “Seagrass Carbon Sequestration” unit, the teacher brakes down the topic into smaller assignments, including the definition of seagrass, the mechanism of seagrass carbon sequestration, and the environmental impacts of CO 2. Students research the topic in groups, with access to authoritative resources. The teacher explains scientific principles in greater detail through multimedia. A scientist then delivers a lecture on “Carbon Neutrality,” using animations to vividly explain the mechanisms of carbon sequestration by seagrass, while addressing students’ questions. Throughout the process, the lessons are conducted primarily in English. Students present their understanding of the seagrass carbon sequestration mechanism in a group presentation, enhancing their understanding of the topic and improving their communication, teamwork, and research skills.

However, while the outcomes of the TOS project are promising, several limitations must be acknowledged. One significant concern lies in the generalizability of the model. As a summer camp, the project was conducted outside of the regular school schedule and benefited from considerable external resources, including university-level experts and institutional partnerships. These conditions are difficult to replicate in mainstream public schools, where curriculum constraints, limited staffing, and resource disparities may hinder implementation. For long-term integration into the secondary education system, it is essential to develop mechanisms that enable in-service teachers to independently design and facilitate similar interdisciplinary, bilingual projects.

Another challenge is the lack of comprehensive evaluation data. While qualitative feedback indicates positive student experiences, the absence of rigorous pre- and post-assessment limits the ability to draw concrete conclusions about the program’s impact on science literacy, language proficiency, and critical thinking. Future iterations should involve incorporating mixed-methods evaluation frameworks, including surveys, interviews, and performance-based assessments, to systematically measure learning gains and identify areas for further refinement.

6 Conclusion
------------

This study is the first large-scale empirical investigation in China to evaluate the current landscape and future potential of interdisciplinary, project-based English science education at the secondary level. Drawing on 3459 valid student responses and extensive interviews with teachers, administrators, and experts, the research highlights systemic barriers that hinder bilingual science instruction, including limited interdisciplinary teaching capacity, lack of suitable instructional resources, and insufficient institutional support. Despite students’ growing interest in scientific topics, their ability to engage in interdisciplinary learning through English remains underdeveloped.

However, the study has limitations. The sample is geographically confined to Shanghai, and the research design is primarily cross-sectional. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal methods, expand regional coverage to include less-developed areas, and apply advanced statistical techniques to evaluate long-term learning outcomes and institutional change.

This research offers actionable recommendations for interdisciplinary science education. For educators, there is an urgent need for targeted professional development on interdisciplinary pedagogy, particularly in models such as CLIL and PBL. Policymakers should integrate science-English modules into national curriculum standards and allocate sustainable funding for teacher training and resource development. Curriculum designers are encouraged to develop scaffolded instructional materials that support both scientific inquiry and language acquisition, tailored to students’ developmental stages.

In an era that demands both scientific literacy and global competence, this study underscores the transformative potential of integrated bilingual education. By bridging disciplinary divides, it paves the way for cultivating a generation of learners who are both scientifically informed and globally engaged.
