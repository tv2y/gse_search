If you eat processed meats and read nutrition labels, you’ve probably seen lots of bacon, sausage, and cold cuts on store shelves that claim to be free of added nitrates and nitrites.

These proclamations make it sound like these compounds are automatically bad for you — and that you can easily avoid them. But the picture isn’t that simple, and steering clear of processed foods containing nitrates and nitrites won’t necessarily get these out of your diet.

“Nitrates and nitrites are naturally found in foods, and they have both healthy and unhealthy effects in the human body,” says Samantha Heller, RD, a senior clinical nutritionist at NYU Langone Health in New York City.

“Both nitrates and nitrites are found in fruits and vegetables and are added, primarily as sodium nitrite, to processed foods such as deli meats, bacon, hot dogs, ham, and jerky,” Heller says.

Nitrates vs. Nitrites: What’s the Difference?
---------------------------------------------

These are both chemical compounds that contain a combination of nitrogen and oxygen. The basic difference between nitrates and nitrites is the number of oxygen atoms they have. Nitrates have one nitrogen atom and three oxygen atoms, while nitrites contain one nitrogen atom and two oxygen atoms.

When people eat foods containing nitrates, bacteria and enzymes in the mouth and digestive tract can convert nitrates into nitrites. Then nitrites can be converted into one of two compounds: nitric oxide, which has a variety of health benefits; and nitrosamines, which are associated with certain cancers.

“Nitrites are often used to cure and flavor meat,” says Andrew Chan, MD, MPH, a gastroenterologist at Massachusetts General Hospital and a professor at Harvard Medical School in Boston. “However, they can be harmful to health if they get converted to nitrosamines through cooking at high temperatures or if they interact with inflammatory factors in the body.”

Why Nitrates and Nitrites Are Used in Food Manufacturing
--------------------------------------------------------

Nitrates are naturally found in air, soil, and water as well as in some plants. These chemicals are also added to red and processed meats like bacon, sausage, and cold cuts during food manufacturing. Nitrates can help cure meats, give them a reddish color, act as a preservative, and prevent bacteria growth, according to the European Food Safety Authority.

Common Foods With Nitrates and Nitrites
---------------------------------------

Bacon may be the first food that comes to mind as being high in nitrates, but many people are surprised to hear red and processed meats often contain less of these chemicals than many green, leafy vegetables.

Those foods are typically thought to be part of a healthy diet, and so are a variety of fruits and vegetables that also can contain nitrates. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, some top sources of nitrates in healthy food include:

*   Celery
*   Lettuce
*   Spinach
*   Bok choy
*   Carrots
*   Beets

Nitrates are also found in a lot of manufactured foods that aren’t always as good for us, per the CDC. Foods containing these chemicals may list them in a variety of ways on the label — often as potassium nitrite or nitrate, or as sodium nitrite or nitrate. Products labeled as organic or natural may still contain nitrates, often derived from celery. Some of these foods include:

*   Bacon
*   Deli meat
*   Ham
*   Hot dogs

Potential Health Effects of Nitrates and Nitrites
-------------------------------------------------

For many people, the biggest dietary source of nitrates and nitrates is green leafy vegetables, and these chemicals found naturally in veggies can have some positive health effects. Nitrates on their own don’t impact health much. Nitrites — formed when nitrates are exposed to bacteria and enzymes — can then break down into one of two compounds: nitric oxide or nitrosamines.

Nitrites have health benefits when they’re converted to nitric oxide, a compound with antioxidant properties that has been linked to improved cardiovascular and metabolic health, according to a study published in _Oxidative Medicine and Cellular Longevity_. Some positive health effects include:

*   Lower blood pressure, according to a previous study
*   Improved exercise endurance, according to a study published in July 2021 in the _Journal of the International Society of Sports Medicine_
*   Reduced risk of cardiovascular disease, according to a study published in April 2021 in the _European Journal of Epidemiology_

Nitrites become problematic when they break down into nitrosamines, which research published in August 2020 in _Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition_ link with certain cancers and genetic mutations. Nitrosamines form when nitrites are exposed to proteins — especially foods cooked at high heat, Columbia University Irving Medical Center notes. This means the negative health effects of nitrates are most likely to develop when people eat red and processed meats that are broiled or grilled at high heat. Some negative health effects include:

*   Increased risk of colon, kidney, stomach, thyroid, and ovarian cancers, per the National Cancer Institute’s Cancer Trends Progress Report
*   Oxidative stress, which can damage cell tissue and DNA and cause inflammation, according to a paper published in March 2020 in _Antioxidants_
*   Methemoglobinemia, or nitrosamine poisoning, which causes insufficient oxygen in the blood and most often affects infants, per the aforementioned _Antioxidants_ article

How to Limit Your Exposure
--------------------------

Suffice it to say if you’re eating fresh spinach, carrots, bok choy, and beets, you’re in the clear. There is less research on the potential effects that certain cooking methods have on nitrite levels in nitrate-containing veggies. But some research offers a clue. For example, stir-frying vegetables may result in more exposure to nitrites than boiling veggies, according to a study published in December 2021 in _Foods_. More studies on the effect of cooking veggies with nitrates, especially cooking veggies at high heat, are needed.

As for processed foods, steering completely clear of nitrates and nitrates can be hard to do even when you scrutinize food labels. But you still can shop and eat in ways that limit your exposure and minimize health risks of consuming foods with these compounds.

If you do just one thing, focus on limiting your consumption of processed meats.

“Probably the unhealthiest foods rich in nitrites are processed meats,” says Mingyang Song, MBBS, ScD, an assistant professor of clinical epidemiology and nutrition at the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health in Boston.

There are several things to keep in mind when trying to minimize your exposure to processed meat, according to the University of Texas M.D. Anderson Cancer Center, including:

*   **Beware “nitrate-free” labels.**This just means processed meats lack artificial nitrates, but they can still contain natural nitrates such as celery powder. These natural nitrates can still be harmful, according to M.D. Anderson.
*   **Realize red meat isn’t the only problem.** Other meat can be, too. Chicken or turkey cold cuts most likely have nitrates, and so does turkey bacon and any other type of processed meat, M.D. Anderson notes.
*   **Check the sell-by date of your foods.**Fresh meat expires in days, not weeks, and if it lasts more than a few days, it’s probably processed and has nitrates.

One other thing to consider is your water source. People who drink well water should have it tested for nitrates, especially if they live in an agricultural area where fertilizer runoff might contain these compounds, according to the CDC. Bottled water is best if well water hasn’t been tested yet.

Parents of newborns should also avoid feeding infants homemade foods that contain certain nitrate-rich vegetables like spinach, beets, green beans, squash, and carrots until babies are at least 6 months old, due to the risk of poisoning, according to Nationwide Children’s Hospital. Because solid foods are not recommended until 6 months old anyway, this shouldn’t be an issue. until babies are at least 3 months old, due to the risk of poisoning, the CDC advises. These newborns can still have commercially prepared baby food, including food with nitrate-containing veggies, and breast milk.

This risk of nitrate poisoning in newborns happens because babies have red blood cells — which are responsible for carrying oxygen through the body — with a limited ability to prevent a chain reaction caused by nitrates that impairs oxygen delivery. By the time they’re 3 to 6 months old, most babies make red blood cells capable of resisting this chain reaction, limiting the harm that nitrates can cause.

Summary
-------

One thing to keep in mind about nitrates and nitrites is that these compounds are just one small aspect of what might make — or break — a healthy diet.

Eating a plant-based diet, or following heart-healthy eating patterns like the Mediterranean or DASH diets that limit red and processed meats and emphasize a variety of fresh, whole foods, can all help reduce the risk of potential health harms from nitrates, says Heller.

“The nitrites and nitrates naturally occurring in fruits and vegetables are unlikely to cause health issues in adults,” Heller says. “However, the consumption of processed meats has been associated with an increased risk of several diseases. The larger the portion sizes and more frequent the consumption, the greater the health risks.”

### Common Questions & Answers

Which is worse for you: nitrates or nitrites? 

It’s complicated. Nitrates aren’t a problem on their own, but they can get converted to nitrites during digestion. Nitrites pose health risks when they’re converted into nitrosamines in the digestive tract. So, technically, nitrites converted into nitrosamines are the real issue.

What are the worst food offenders for nitrates and nitrites? 

Meats that are heavily processed or cooked at high temperatures are the biggest source of nitrates that convert to nitrosamines in the digestive tract.

What do nitrates do to your body? 

Depending on how they get digested and what foods they come from, nitrates can either have benefits like improved cardiovascular and metabolic health — or carry risks of certain cancers and cell damage.

Does all bacon have nitrates? What about all deli meat? 

All bacon — even organic or turkey bacon — typically contains nitrites. So does packaged deli meat. Even if it’s labeled “nitrate-free,” it can still have nitrites from celery powder.

Can humans get nitrate poisoning? 

People can get nitrate poisoning from overexposure to medicines or foods containing this compound, or from contaminated well water. Infants are at particular risk.
