What legal action can I take if someone sells my house without informing me?

Disclosure: I’m not a lawyer. For legal advice, please see a lawyer.

The answer depends on the circumstances and conditions of the sale, as well as some definitions.

Let’s look at some possibilities.

Definition: “My house.” The typical understanding of this phrase is that you own the house. The deed is in your name. However, I’ve heard tenants refer to the house they’re renting as “my house.” The owner of the house has the ability (with some restrictions in some areas) to sell a house that’s currently being rented. Sometimes the owner has to give the tenants a first right of refusal. Or the owner must provide advance notice. Still, an owner can sell his/her house. It’s not “your” house if you’re renting it. If this has happened, check with a lawyer to make sure that all the required steps were taken.

Recognize, too, that there’s a difference between the deed being in your name and the mortgage being in your name. It’s possible that your name is on the mortgage but not on the title. Maybe it started out like that. Maybe at some point you agreed to your name being removed from the title. You can do that, and you no longer own the home even though you’re still responsible for the mortgage. In that case, it’s not your house. Whoever’s name is on the title can sell the house. If this has happened, check with a lawyer.

Another possibility: You’re purchasing the house with a lease-option. You’re in the house; you’ve been paying; and now the owner sells it. That happens. One step to reduce that likelihood is to “cloud the title”—basically, by filing a document at the courthouse that would make it difficult/impossible to sell. Sometimes, you can file a “Memorandum of Agreement.” Some people advise actually filing a copy of the option. Others suggest a slightly different procedure. While all these should raise a red flag for someone doing a title search, it’s not foolproof. I did a lease-option back on 2007 and filed a Memorandum of Agreement. The property was sold twice with no problem. Only on the third sale did a title company catch my Memorandum of Agreement. (In this case, check with a lawyer. But here the title company that handled the sale should have “Errors and Omissions” coverage to cover its mistake.)

Another possibility: Your house is in a trust. Depending on the type of trust, either the trust owns the house or (in the case of a land trust) the trustee owns the house. While the trust documents should specify what the trust or trustee can and can’t do, it’s entirely possible for the trustee to sell the property. Or, in a somewhat similar arrangement, the house may be owned by an LLC. The LLC, in turn, can be sold . . . and with its sale goes the ownership of the house. If this has happened, check with a lawyer.

But that’s only the beginning.

We’ll assume the deed’s in your name. (In all these cases, your next step is to consult with a lawyer.)

If you’ve fallen behind on taxes, the city or county may place a lien on your property. (A “tax lien.”) If you don’t pay those back taxes, the city/county can sell your property to someone else. The law varies greatly. In some cases, the sale is final. In other cases, there’s a redemption period—such as 3 years—during which you can repurchase your property for the amount of back taxes, interest, and fines or fees.

If you have a debt to the water company (oddly, it works differently for other utilities), the water company can have a lien placed on your property and—if you don’t pay the bill—it can seize your property.

If you live in a condominium (or sometimes even within a homeowner’s association) and have fallen behind on your condo fees, the condo association can put a lien on your property and eventually seize it. This happened to a good friend of mine. To two of his condos.

If you live in a house and the deed is structured as “tenants in common” (in which case each owner has a specifically defined ownership percentage), then the other person may sell his/her ownership. He/she can’t sell yours, but can sell his/her portion.

If you’ve granted another person power of attorney (either a general power of attorney or a limited one that covers the disposition of your real estate), then that person likely has the power to sell your house without your consent.

If a court has judged you unable to manage your own affairs (incompetent) and someone has been appointed to do so, then that person can sell your house without your consent.

Or, as another answer here suggests, you may be the victim of outright fraud. Check with a lawyer and report the sale to your local law enforcement authorities.

There are a lot of other possibilities as well.

In virtually all cases, see a lawyer. In some instances (assuming you owned the house), you may be able to recover it. In other instances, it’s gone.

Good luck.

No one can sell your property .

If someone does so and executes and registers a sale deed in favour of someone else with or without your knowledge, it makes little difference so far as your right of ownership is concerned in the same property in the eye of law.

Law will treat the buyer as acquiring not an iota of interest by the said transaction.

Because in any transfer of property, the transferor can transfer to the transferee at best the interest he is having.

If someone sells your property, the acquires absolutely no rights title and interest in the said property. Law will not treat that as a real transaction, but only a paper transaction. Your right, title and interest in the said property ( sold to other person) remains intact. The said buyer bought merely a bag full of heir by that document.

The said alleged buyer may try to take possession of the property or disturb you in many other ways. Resist such endeavours with all your might but within the limits of law.

If you can do this, you will remain in an advantageous position.

But if the alleged buyer can take possession of the property somehow, you will be placed in a disadvantageous position.

In that event, the situation would require a long drawn legal battle. But ultimately the ownership would remain with you theoretically.

You’ll need to provide more context.

On the one hand if some random is wandering around selling other peoples’ properties to wood ducks like people used to sell swampland in Louisiana, you don’t need to do anything: the property is still yours (if it was before).

To take the opposite extreme, if a bank, holding a mortgage on you property, to whom mortgage payments were in arrears, and who has made reasonable attempts to contact you, then sells the property, you no longer own the house and there is nothing you can do about it.

Let’s hear more of the story. An important question is the nature of property law wherever the house is. Civilized countries have introduced Torrens Title (named after Robert Torrens of South Australia, who developed the system). This controls transfer of property and prevents many type of irresponsible property trading. A few states of the USA have introduced Torrens Title, but most still live in the times of Dick Dastardly with the mortgage in the inside pocket threatening the poor widder woman.

So far, except for one answer related to real estate in India, all of the answers are for U.S. law. There are ways of house stealing in the U.S., which may involve identity theft (someone takes on the identity of the true homeowner and goes through the acts of selling to a cohort as if the person was that true homeowner), or title alteration, where they are able to modify the recorded title in some way. Other ways might be by exercising some partial right that is not the full right on the property. And so on.

Regardless, in the U.S., it can be a total hassle to sort this out, but there is a general understanding that, after hassles, court hearings, expenses, and the like, if it truly is a case of Party B selling to Party C real estate that is owned and titled to Party A, eventually Party C will be out of luck and Party A would have clear title. Or, if it turns out that Party C is a bone fide purchase for some reason and cannot be dispossessed, Party A has a claim against Party B.

This question relates to a house Zambia, and I presume that U.S.-based answers would have little relevance there.

What can I do if my landlord sells the house I rent without my knowledge?

In the US, the landlord has no legal obligation to tell you he’s selling the house.

Your lease continues to be valid until you are given proper written notice, in accordance with the law. Continue to pay the rent as your lease requires, until and unless you are instructed, IN WRITING, to do otherwise.

The new property owner MUST give you written instructions if he wants the rent paid to anyone else, or anywhere else, than your current lease requires. Be SURE you keep receipts for payment!! Pay by check so you can prove the money was accepted by the last-know legal landlord. Or get written receipt for payment, including the recipient’s signature and date. DO NOT SKIP THIS STEP!! It’s your ONLY proof that you have paid your rent!
